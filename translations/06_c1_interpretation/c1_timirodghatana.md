# C1 — Timirodghāṭana (commentary)

*A commentary on the Timirodghāṭana, an archaic Kaula scripture (pre-11th century, quoted
by Abhinavagupta and Kṣemarāja) that survives in an ancient palm-leaf manuscript full of
lacunae. It is, on the evidence of Törzsök's study, a rewriting of the earlier
Siddhayogeśvarīmata — the same revelation recast, with its soteriology centered on the
possession by the Power of Rudra. The commentary follows the text in reasoned prose,
placing it in its historical frame; the audited translation is toggled on beneath. Proposal,
for review.*

---

## The revelation and its rewriting

The Timirodghāṭana is old, and its antiquity is visible in its form: the archaic aiśa
grammar, the palm-leaf manuscript (NGMPP A35/3) full of missing folios and corrupt
readings, and the quotations by Abhinavagupta and Kṣemarāja that place it before the
eleventh century. It belongs to the stratum Sanderson has mapped as the earliest of the
nondualist revelation — the Yoginī-centered scriptures of the Vidyāpīṭha, the corpus built
around the goddess and her circle of Yoginīs, in which the Siddhayogeśvarīmata and the
Tantrasadbhāva stand as the earliest Trika strata. The Timirodghāṭana is part of that
world: a Yoginī-scripture of the archaic Kaula, predating the vast Kubjikā tradition it
anticipates.

But its antiquity is not merely its date; it is, more precisely, a *rewriting*. Törzsök's
study shows that the Timirodghāṭana takes up the iconography and rituals of the earlier
Siddhayogeśvarīmata and recasts them — changing key attributes, shifting the ritual focus,
and making the possession by the Power of Rudra (rudraśakti-samāveśa) the essential
operator of liberation. The text must be read with this in view: it is not a neutral record
but a deliberate re-formation. The Siddhayogeśvarīmata is one of the earliest witnesses of
the Yoginī-revelation; the Timirodghāṭana is that revelation taken up and turned toward the
possession. What it keeps, what it changes, and what it makes central are the doctrine.

## The Bhairava visualized (paṭala 1)

The text opens with the great Bhairava on Kailāsa, surrounded by the siddhas and the
accomplished yogis, and the visualization is the first act of the rewriting. He is
five-faced and sixteen-armed, tawny-haired, blazing like fire, the serpent as his sacred
thread, his body half-joined with Umā's. He holds the sword and the skull, the trident and
the vajra, the spear and the axe, the ḍamaru and the wheel and the bow; he wears the
elephant-hide and the tiger-skin, laughs with the kila-kila cry, hard to look at even for
the gods. The form is the Kaula absolute in image: the terrible and the blissful joined,
the destroyer who yet laughs, the one who holds every weapon because he is the power behind
every act.

The Törzsök-rewriting gives the visualization its edge. The Timirodghāṭana's Bhairava
differs from the Siddhayogeśvarīmata's: the corpse-garland becomes a simple rosary, the
attributes shift, the emphasis changes. The text is not describing an image that exists
independently; it is choosing an image, deliberately altering the received iconography. The
sixteen arms and the laughing form, the half-joined Umā and the weapons — these are the
text's own selection. And the selection is the theology: the visual shift from the
corpse-garland to the rosary marks the text's turn from the mere dread of the goddess to
the possession that is her grace. The visualization is the first site where the rewriting
does its work.

## The liṅga and its rejection (paṭalas 2, 12)

The text's central polemic is the rejection of the outer liṅga — the earthen, the stone,
the silver, the gold — in favor of the inner. The settled reading is exact: the list is
clay, stone, silver, and gold, the whole range of the material worship that the orthodox
perform upon an external emblem. The Kaula rejects them all. The liṅga is not an object to
be approached but the own-body, pervaded by the supreme Power; the worship of the inner
body replaces the worship of the outer stone. The gesture recurs across the archaic Kaula
world — the Kulasāra makes the same move — and it is the Kaula's founding act. The
rejection is not iconoclasm for its own sake; it is the assertion that the reality the
stone only symbolizes is already the body, already present, already the site of the
possession that liberates.

## The possession and its symptoms (paṭala 4)

The heart of the text is the rudraśakti-samāveśa — the possession by the Power of Rudra —
and here the rewriting is most deliberate, for it makes this possession the essential
operator of liberation. The dossier on the possession is exact: the samāveśa is the
"entry," the state of being-taken-by-the-divine, and the great exegetes treat it as the
practice's goal — the surge of the state of entry, the designation of the entry into Śiva
and Śakti, the ground of the supreme Lord's entry. The Timirodghāṭana makes this the whole
of its soteriology. The Power issues forth from the lord — his Īśānī, the power of the
north-eastern face, emerging as the nāda-bindu — and enters the practitioner. The
possession is not a metaphor; the text describes its symptoms with a clinical exactness:
the heart trembles, the sound rises in the palate, the head spins, the body whirls, the
possessed one no longer knows day from night, is freed from hunger and thirst, is bound in
the mudrā.

The symptoms are the doctrine made experiential. The trembling heart, the rising sound,
the whirling, the loss of time — these are the marks of the limited self being displaced
by the supreme Power. The possessed one no longer knows day from night because the one who
knows time has been taken over by what is beyond time; he is freed from hunger because the
self that hungers has been displaced. The samāveśa is the Kaula's liberation: not a gradual
cultivation but a possession, a takeover by the divine Power. The text's center is its most
intimate moment — the practitioner no longer the agent but the vehicle.

## The free yoga and its fruits (paṭala 5)

From the possession flows the kaula-yoga, and the text defines it negatively against every
constrained practice. The free yoga is by one's own will, free of the retention and the
piercing, free of karma, the nectar-yoga. The distinction is the Kaula's polemic: the
bound yoga labors by technique; the free yoga is what the possessed one does
spontaneously, moved by the Power that has taken him. Its fruits are the eight lordships —
the aṇimā, the laghimā, the mahimā, the attainment, the prākāmya, the lordship (īśitva),
the vaśitva — and among them the khecarī, the sky-goer, and the siddhi. The powers are the
signature of the possession: the one possessed by the Power of Rudra naturally commands
the powers that the bound yogi labors for, because he is no longer the doer but the vehicle
of the all-powerful.

## The guru and the disciple (paṭalas 9–10)

The text insists on the rarity of the true guru, and the insistence is doctrinal. The three
powers — the guru's own, the transmitted, the grace — and the teacher who is possessed by
the rudraśakti are the giver of liberation, and such a guru is rare. The examination of the
disciple is the text's other gate: the rejected and the acceptable are distinguished, and
the acceptance is not a matter of birth but of readiness for the possession. The guru is
rare because the possession is not manufactured; it is given, and the one who can give it
is the one who has been taken. The secrecy is the same law as in every Kaula text: what
cannot be taught can only be received, and the reception requires the possessed teacher
and the ready disciple.

## The inner liṅga and the close (paṭala 12)

The text closes as it began, with the liṅga — now fully inner. The rejection of the outer
clay, stone, silver, and gold is completed by the assertion of the inner: the own-body is
the liṅga, pervaded by the parā-śakti. The appended Jñānaśūla (the knowledge-trident of the
three powers) and the Nirvāṇakārikā (the dissolution of the breath into the supreme Śiva)
carry the same doctrine to its end. The whole text — the rewritten Bhairava, the rejected
liṅga, the possessed practitioner, the free yoga, the inner body — is the archaic Kaula's
single claim: the supreme Power is present in the body, takes the practitioner in
possession, and the liberation is not an escape but a being-taken. And the text's
distinctiveness, against its own source, is the sharpness of that claim: where the
Siddhayogeśvarīmata preserved the goddess's dread, the Timirodghāṭana turns it into the
possession that is grace, and makes the being-taken the whole of the path.
