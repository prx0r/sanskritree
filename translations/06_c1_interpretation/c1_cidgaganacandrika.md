# C1 — Cidgaganacandrikā (commentary)

*A commentary on the Cidgaganacandrikā, the "Moonlight of the Sky of Consciousness," a hymn
to Kālī of the late Kālīkrama, attributed in its colophon to Kālidāsa and in its final
verse to Śrīvatsa. It is the Krama's poetic crown — the doctrine of the Mahānayaprakāśa
rendered as praise. The commentary follows the hymn in reasoned prose, placing it in its
literary and historical frame; the audited translation is toggled on beneath. Proposal,
for review.*

---

## The moonlight and the double name

The title is the text's whole doctrine in three words: the sky (gagana) is consciousness,
the open field in which everything appears, and the moonbeam (candrikā) is its
self-knowledge, the light by which the sky's contents shine. The Cidgaganacandrikā is the
moonlight of the sky of consciousness — the hymn in which the absolute's own reflexion is
praised. Its double attribution is a small literary puzzle with a real historical point.
The colophon names Kālidāsa, the great poet; the final verse names Śrīvatsa. The two are
likely the same figure under two names, or Śrīvatsa assuming the mantle of the poet — and
either way the attribution is the text's claim to a certain register: this is a *kāvya*,
a poem, not merely a liturgy, and it is to be read as literature as much as scripture. The
hymn's own closing reveals its geography: the transmission is traced to the Sarayā/Avadh
region, and the manuscripts and citations suggest it was composed in the south. The text
is thus a poem of the Krama's diffusion — a Kashmirian doctrine carried south and given
verse.

## The four reflexions

Its structure is four vimarśas — four reflexions — of uneven length (22, 52, 105, and 133
verses). The first is the prelude, stating the whole in brief; the other three are its
expansions, each reflecting the theme from a new angle. The name is exact, and it is a
literary structure as much as a doctrinal one. The work does not argue forward in a line
but reflects the one truth from four surfaces, as moonlight reflects the same light from
every face of the sky. Each vimarśa turns the goddess over again — the first announcing
her, the second and third unfolding the phonemes and the stations, the fourth the forms
and the closing — and the repetition is the poem's method. The four reflexions are the
four ways the one consciousness looks at itself, and the hymn's four-part form enacts the
very reflexion it praises.

## The Full Moon and the New Moon

The goddess of the hymn is Kālī as the moon in its two phases. When she merges the
universe into herself, she is the Full Moon, the gathered fullness of consciousness; when
she empties herself to emanate the world, she is the New Moon (kuhū), the emptied point
from which all form arises. The two are one, and the image holds the Krama's central
claim: consciousness is not a static ground but a *cycle*, gathering the world back and
pouring it out again. The Full Moon is the saṃhāra, the withdrawal; the New Moon is the
sṛṣṭi, the emanation; and both are the one lunar power of awareness. The lunar image is
not an ornament but the poem's theology: the goddess who waxes and wanes is the
consciousness that rises and withdraws, and her phases are the Krama's own cycle made
visible in the sky.

## The khecarī and the movers (vv. 32, 90–95)

The hymn's proof-text for the sky-goer is the khecarī-verse, and the settled reading is
the anchored one. The khecaratā is the goddess's own condition, described as the
fire's-seeing-station (analadṛṣṭidhāma) — the place where consciousness gazes as fire
does, consuming and illumining and itself unchanged. The reading is anchored because the
same verse is quoted in the Yoginīhṛdaya's Dīpikā; the two texts must agree, and the
Cidgaganacandrikā's reading is the older one the later commentary preserves. Around the
sky-goer are the four movers (khecarī-catuṣṭaya), each attached to a sense: the sky-mover
gathers sight, the earth-mover is touch, the direction-mover taste, the object-mover
scent. The movers ARE the sense-powers — the goddess's activity in the body — and the
khecarī, the sky-mover, is the highest, the one whose field is the open sky of
consciousness itself.

## The Time-Compressor (v. 95)

Among the powers stands the Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī, the Time-Compressor — she who draws time to
herself. The saṃkarṣaṇa is the drawing-together, the compressing, and her name means she
who gathers the whole flux of succession into the single point of awareness. She is the
same goddess across the Krama — the Mahānayaprakāśa's yāga-deity, the Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī of the
whole school. Her presence here is the school's deepest claim about time: time is not an
external current in which consciousness floats but a power of consciousness itself, which
gathers the succession into itself and holds it. The Time-Compressor is the proof that the
Krama's cycle — emanation, persistence, withdrawal — is not suffered by consciousness but
enacted by it. The hymn praises, in her, the very power by which the hymn's own sequence of
verses is gathered into the single act of praise.

## The mirror and the reflexion (vv. 188–197)

The hymn's epistemology is the mirror-doctrine, and the settled reading is precise about
what the mirror does. The world is not a reflection of an outer reality — a copy, an
illusion — but the reflexion's *own act*: the world's image is consciousness articulating
itself, the absolute's self-manifestation. The distinction is the A-thesis, and it is
everything. If the world were a reflection, it would be secondary, derivative, less real
than what it copies — and the whole nondual claim would collapse. But if the world is the
reflexion's own act, then it is consciousness itself in its self-articulation, real as the
act that produces it. The mirror does not hold an image of something else; it is the
consciousness shining as the world. The hymn's most striking image makes this concrete —
the object-petalled lotus (v. 258), the world as the petals, full of the nectar of the
goddess's reflexion (tvad-vimarśa-makaranda), going to its seat: the world is the lotus
whose petals are objects and whose nectar is the reflexion that holds them.

## The memory and ownership (vv. 205–210)

The hymn's second great philosophical move is its doctrine of memory, and here it argues
rather than sings. Memory is not the storage of past experiences but the proof of
ownership: the memory is the knower's life, what the knower has as its own in its reflexion
of what has passed. The trace is not the owner; what owns the memory is the reflexion that
gathers it into the "I." At the memory's dissolution, the experience alone remains — and
this remaining, this owning, is the continuity of consciousness through time. The argument
recurs across the batch — the Ajaḍapramātṛsiddhi's memory-passages, the Recognition's
ownership — and here it is given its most compact form: the memory is the seed of the "I,"
the proof that consciousness is not a series of disconnected flashes but a single owner
gathering its experiences into itself.

## The closing and the signature (vv. 281–312)

The hymn's closing movements gather the whole. The stations are threaded by the beyond —
the fourth dwelling in the second, the fifth in the third, the levels mutually pervading;
the guru is declared necessary, the scriptures inadequate; the deity-host is arrayed, the
mantra-seat and the pulse-seat and the liṅga-body and the nine-wheel are named; the "I"
station is reached and the personal stain dissolves. And then the signature: the
consciousness-form, the author's name, the phalaśruti with its verse-count, and the double
attribution — Kālidāsa in the colophon, Śrīvatsa in the verse, the Sarayā/Avadh geography
beneath. The closing is the hymn's self-completion: it has praised the goddess as the
moonlight of the sky of consciousness, and in naming its author and its region it locates
that praise in a real transmission, a real poem, a real history. The hymn's work is the
recognition, and the recognition is both the liberation it praises and the poem it is.

## The moonlight's work

The Cidgaganacandrikā, read whole, is the Krama's doctrine as poetry. It takes the
cognition-sequence — the cycle of emanation and withdrawal, the reflexion that owns the
world, the time that consciousness gathers — and turns it into a hymn, structured as four
reflexions, imaged as the moon, signed by a poet. Its single claim is that the absolute is
not a remote ground but the reflexion that is the world's own light, and that to praise
that reflexion is to recognize it. The hymn is the recognition enacted in verse: the
moonlight of the sky of consciousness, shining as the poem that knows what it shines on.
