# C1 — Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava (commentary)

*A commentary on the Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava, the first part (pūrva) of the Vāmakeśvara — the
scripture of the Śrīvidyā tradition whose second part (uttara) is the Yoginīhṛdaya. The
commentary follows the text in reasoned prose, placing it in its historical and doctrinal
frame; the audited translation is toggled on beneath. Proposal, for review.*

---

## The pair and its halves

The Vāmakeśvara is a work of two halves, and the Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava is the first. Its
name names its subject — the sixteenth (ṣoḍaśī) goddess as the ocean (arṇava) of the
nityās, the eternal powers — and its position names its place: it is the pūrva, the
"prior" half, the part that teaches the goddess and her worship, while the Yoginīhṛdaya,
the "heart of the Yoginī," is the uttara, the teaching of the cognition and the pūjā that
realizes her. The two are one text, and each is incomplete without the other: the
Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava gives the goddess, the mantras, the maṇḍala; the Yoginīhṛdaya gives
the saṃketas — the cakra, the mantra, the pūjā — by which she is realized. Padoux's
translation of the Yoginīhṛdaya (2013) has made the second half known; the pūrva treated
here is its foundation.

The date is late — the text's doctrine is the mature Śrīvidyā, and its sister-text, the
Nityākaulatantra, is the earlier, "pre-Śrīvidyā" witness to the same goddess (Dyczkowski
judges it especially important for reconstructing the development of the Tripurā-cult
before the mature system). The Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava stands at the far end of that
development: the goddess who was worshiped in the early Nityās has here become the
Ṣoḍaśī of the fully-formed śrīcakra, and the text is the system's own scripture, setting
out the cakra that will be the center of the whole Śrīvidyā. Its antiquity is not its
date but its position: it is the point where the cult of the sixteen nityās becomes the
worship of the one goddess in her sixteenfold form.

## The name and the counting

The goddess is Ṣoḍaśī, "the Sixteenth," and the name is a doctrine of number. She is the
sixteenth where the fifteen-syllabled mantra is the fifteen — the point beyond the
counting that yet completes it. The fifteen syllables (the pañcadaśī) are the powers of
her manifestation; the sixteenth is the goddess herself, the one in whom the fifteen
subsist. The same arithmetic runs through the whole Śaiva-Śākta world — the sixteenth
digit of the moon, the amā, beyond the fifteen that wax and wane; the ṣoḍaśī who is the
moon at its full, the light of the three eyes of Śiva that illumines the three worlds. The
Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava is the scripture of that sixteenth: the text in which the goddess who
is the completion of every count is taught. Its name, arṇava (the ocean), adds the second
image — the goddess is not only the sixteenth but the ocean of the nityās, the eternal
powers that are her waves.

## The cakra and its geometry (vv. 65–79)

The worship begins with the cakra, and the text's treatment of it is exact. The maṇḍala is
three-cubit, its seventy-two parts divided by the third, second, and first cubits; the
cubit is defined as twenty-four aṅgulas by the commentary (the Setubandha of
Bhāskararāya), and the division is the construction. The heart of the cakra is its
forty-three triangles — the one, the eight, the two ten-rayed, the fourteen — and the
number is not arbitrary. It is the same forty-three that the Yoginīhṛdaya will repeat: the
five powers of consciousness set against the four fires of the world, the forty-three
triangles in which the goddess's energies are held, and the forty-fourth that appears only
with the central bindu, the goddess beyond the triangles. The two halves of the Vāmakeśvara
are bound by this number: what the pūrva constructs, the uttara's cakrasaṃketa
re-construes, and both count the same triangles. The geometry is the shared skeleton of the
tradition, and its precision is its point — the cakra is not a symbol but a construction,
and the worship is the construction recognized.

## The speech-goddesses (vv. 83–86, 191–193)

Within the cakra dwell the eight vāgdevatās, and their placement is a correspondence. Each
phoneme-group of the alphabet has its deity: the a-varga's Vaśinī, and so through the
groups to the eight — Vaśinī, Kāmeśvarī, Modinī, Vimalā, Aruṇā, Jayinī, Sarveśvarī,
Kaulinī. The goddess of speech is the alphabet's own power, distributed through its
groups, and to articulate the mantra's syllables is to engage the goddesses who are those
syllables. The eighth, Kaulinī, is the kula-goddess — the one who gathers the seven
before her into the family of speech — and her presence at the octad's end points toward
the pañcadaśī that the eight serve as limbs. The same octad will be used in the
Yoginīhṛdaya's pūjā; the correspondence is the tradition's shared technology of speech.

## The three kūṭas (vv. 105–124)

The fifteen syllables are built of three kūṭas, three clusters — the vāgbhava (the seed
of speech), the kāmarāja (the seed of desire), and the māyā (the seed of māyā) — and the
text exposes their composition. The commentary does not stop at the canonical seeds
(aim, klīṃ, sauḥ); it builds them, exposing the consonant joined with the vowel, the
anusvāra, the nāda, so that the seed is seen as a construction of the phoneme-powers. The
doctrine follows from the names: the vāgbhava establishes the goddess's lordship of
speech, the kāmarāja her rule, the māyā her play of manifestation. The three kūṭas are
the three powers of the one goddess — to speak, to rule, to manifest — and the fifteen
syllables that carry them, gathered under the sixteenth that is their source.

## The dhyāna and the portrait (vv. 138–159)

The text turns from the cakra's parts to the goddess who fills it, and the register
changes from construction to vision. The dhyāna is a portrait: Tripurasundarī in her
redness, seated on the lotus in the sun's circle, the color of the japā and the
pomegranate and the ruby, saffron-robed, the crescent on her brow, three-eyed, holding the
goad and the noose, the bow and the arrows of desire, the conch at her throat, her arms
like lotus-stalks. The iconography is precise and meant to be seen: the worship of the
Śrīvidyā uses no image, and the adept's own visualization is the image. The dhyāna is that
visualization given in words — the goddess made present in her form as the cakra is made
present in its geometry. The portrait and the geometry are the two faces of the one
construction: the cakra is the goddess drawn as line and number, the dhyāna is the goddess
drawn as form and color, and both are the means by which the sixteenth is made visible.

## The rings and the gate (vv. 166–186, 200–212)

Around the goddess, in the cakra's rings, are the āvaraṇas — the surrounding powers — and
the text names them by what they do: the All-Churner, the All-Dissolver, the All-Attractor,
the All-Delighter, the All-Bewilderer, the All-Stiller. Each name is a verb of
world-effect, and the commentary's own key, "the name-similar risings," makes the names be
the powers they sing. The cakra is thus a complete cosmology: the one goddess at the
center, and around her, in ordered rings, every power by which the world is churned,
dissolved, drawn, and stilled. The text closes with the phalaśruti and its condition: the
fruits are vast, but they are gated. The lineage-observance and the guru's feet are
required, and the uninitiated are excluded. The gate is not decoration; the worship of the
cakra is a construction that requires instruction, and the guru's feet are where the
numbers and the names and the portrait are transmitted. The goddess is approached by those
who have been taught her form.

## The sixteenth and the ocean

The Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava, read whole, is the Śrīvidyā's own foundation: the pūrva of the
Vāmakeśvara, the text in which the goddess as the sixteenth and her cakra are given. It is
late, systematic, and precise — the mature system's scripture, standing at the end of the
development from the early Nityās to the fully-formed worship of the one goddess. Its
single claim is that the sixteenth is not beyond the fifteen but their completion, and
that the cakra is her form: the forty-three triangles that manifest her, the eight
goddesses of her speech, the three kūṭas of her power, the portrait of her beauty, the
rings of her activity — all of it the one goddess as the ocean of her powers, the Ṣoḍaśī
who is at once the count's completion and its source.
