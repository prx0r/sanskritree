# C1 — Mahāguhyakālīvidhāna (commentary)

*A commentary on the Mahāguhyakālīvidhāna, the "worship-method of the great-secret Kālī," a
Kālīkula ritual manual of the Newari transmission (14th century). It teaches the pūjā of
the Prakāṭakālikā and of the Jvālā-Guhyakālī, the secret Kālī of the blazing
burning-ground. The commentary follows the rite in reasoned prose, placing it in the
Northern current of the tradition; the audited translation is toggled on beneath. Proposal,
for review.*

---

## The manifest and the secret

The Mahāguhyakālīvidhāna is a ritual manual, and its title names its two poles. The
goddess is both the Prakāṭakālikā, the "manifest Kālī" — the one who appears — and the
Mahāguhyakālī, the "great-secret Kālī" — the one who is hidden. The text's whole work is
to bring the two together: to show the manifest form through which the secret is
approached, and to reveal that the manifest form is the door to the hidden. It is a Newari
manuscript of the fourteenth century, and it belongs to the Guhyakālī practical layer of
the Northern current — the same current that Sanderson maps with the Mata, the
Krama/Mahārtha/Mahānaya, and the Guhyakālī as its northern forms, preserved and practiced
in Nepal. The Kālīkulakramārcana of Vimalaprabodha (attested 1002 CE) had already made the
bridge from doctrine into ritual procedure; the Mahāguhyakālīvidhāna is that practical
tradition in its developed form, the worship of the secret Kālī performed as a rite.

The rite is a construction, and its first act is the karāṅga-nyāsa — the placement of the
power in the hand and the limbs. The seed-syllables (hāṃ, hīṃ, hūṃ, haiṃ, hauṃ, haḥ) are
placed in the body, and the placement makes the body a fit vessel for the goddess who is
to be visualized and worshipped. The nyāsa is the text's opening, and it establishes the
frame of the whole rite: the worship is not performed on an external object but in and
through the practitioner's own body, which is first consecrated to receive the goddess.

## The manifest form

The first dhyāna gives the manifest Kālī her form, and the form is the Kālīkula's most
compressed statement. She is three-faced and dark, yellow-dark and red-serpent-hued; she
holds the great skull and the vessel, the trident; she is the skull-garlanded
(muṇḍamālinī), clad in the human hide, the matted-haired, the lolling-tongued, the
terrifying; she is embraced by Bhairava, seated on the corpse. Every attribute is a clause
of the doctrine. The three faces are the three powers; the darkness is the all-devouring;
the skull-garland is the garland of the dissolved world; the human hide is the world she
wears; the corpse and the embracing Bhairava are the union of the still-world and its
Lord; the lolling tongue and the terror are the aspect that devours. The manifest Kālī is
the secret made visible — the all-devouring power shown in her form — and the text
visualizes her with a precision that leaves nothing to the imagination, because the form
is the entry to the worship.

## The armor and the blazing

From the manifest the text passes to the kavaca, the armor that protects the body, and to
the sāvitrī, the prayer that consecrates. Then comes the great dhyāna, the visualization of
the Jvālā-mahāśmaśāna-Guhyakālī — the secret Kālī of the blazing great-burning-ground. She
is the one with the aṭṭahāsa, the wild laughter; the one who breaks the heads of her
enemies; the one whose eyes blaze with flame; the great-skull and the great-garland; the
corpse-seated; she is the Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī, the Time-Compressor, and the Kālarātri, the night
of time; the mother of the world; she vomits the hālāhala poison; she gives the caturvarga,
the four ends of human life.

The Jvālā-Guhyakālī is the manifest Kālī raised to the secret. And here the text's
doctrine touches the Krama's deepest structure. The Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī, the Time-Compressor,
is not an arbitrary epithet; she is one of the Twelve Kālīs, the sequence that Wenta's study
shows to be "the analytical model of the cognitive process" — the twelve powers of the
wheel of consciousness (saṃvit-cakra), rooted in the universal pulse (sāmānya-spanda), the
Sequence of the Sun-Goddess of the northern transmission. The Twelve Kālīs are the
cognition itself, unfolded; and the Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī, the Time-Compressor, is the one who
gathers that whole sequence back into the single point — the terminus of the cognition's
wheel, the power by which the twelve, and the world they constitute, are drawn into the
mother. Her presence in the blazing Guhyakālī makes the secret Kālī the completion of the
twelve: the devourer of time who is also the mother of the world, the night that contains
the wheel.

The blazing, the poison, the wild laughter, and the gift of the four ends: the secret
Kālī is the goddess in whom destruction and motherliness, terror and grace, are one — the
blazing-burning-ground revealed as the womb of the world. The visualizations work as a
pair: the manifest form prepares, the secret form completes, and both are the worship of
the one goddess who is the cognition's own devourer and its mother.

## The offering and the mandala

The rite then proceeds through the pūjā — the five offerings (gandha, puṣpa, dhūpa, dīpa,
naivedya: the scent, the flower, the incense, the lamp, the food) each with its mantra —
and the puṣpāñjali, the flower-offering. The pañcopacāra is the body of the worship: the
goddess is served as the guest, offered the substance of the senses. Then the setting of
the śaṅkha and the śrīpātra, the vessels, and the tripādī, the stand — the material
apparatus of the rite.

The mandala is the cakra in which the goddess is made present: the triangle, the pentagon,
the eight-petaled lotus, the earth-circle, inscribed with the seed-letters and the central
hrīṃ. Around it are placed the directional Guhyakālīs — the goddesses of the east, the
south, the west, the north, and the corners, each a form of the secret Kālī guarding a
direction of the world. The mandala is the cakra of the whole Kālīkula, the goddess at the
center and her powers arrayed around her, and the rite's completion — the washing with the
fire, the marking with the meat — closes the construction. The mandala is not an image to
be looked at; it is the goddess's presence, built, and the worship within it is the
recognition of that presence.

## The construction of the goddess

The Mahāguhyakālīvidhāna, read whole, is the Northern current's ritual construction of the
secret Kālī. It consecrates the body, visualizes the manifest form, invokes the blazing
secret form, offers the five substances, and builds the mandala with its directional
goddesses. Its single claim is that the secret is approached through a precise
construction — that the great-secret Kālī is not vague but made, step by step, in the
body, the vision, and the cakra — and that the making is the worship. And the doctrine it
constructs is the Krama's own: the Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī at the heart of the blazing goddess is
the Time-Compressor of the Twelve Kālīs, the cognition's wheel gathered back into its
mother. The manifest and the secret are one goddess; the rite is the way the secret is
brought to manifestation in the practitioner, and the pūjā's end is not an image invoked
but a presence recognized. The worship of the Guhyakālī is the construction of the goddess
in the practitioner — the twelve Kālīs of the cognition raised to their completion and
their source, and the construction is the liberation.
