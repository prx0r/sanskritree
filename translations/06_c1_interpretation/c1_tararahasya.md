# C1 — Tārārahasya, paṭala 1 (commentary)

*A commentary on the Tārārahasya, the "Secret of Tārā," a worship-manual of the Tārā
tradition composed by Brahmānanda (Brahmānandagiri) in the dialogue of Hara and Gaurī. The
paṭala treated here is the sṛṣṭi-prakaraṇa, the origin-section. The commentary follows the
text in reasoned prose, placing it in its historical and doctrinal frame; the audited
translation is toggled on beneath. Proposal, for review.*

---

## Tārā, the ferry and the blue

The text's goddess is named in its first verse: Tārā, the essence of the world, the mother
of the three worlds, the giver of all the attainments. The name carries the whole doctrine.
The dictionaries say Tārā means "star," but the Tantras take its true etymology as "she who
brings us to the other shore" — from *tāra*, to cross — and it is under this aspect that
the text calls her the essence of the world and the mother of the three worlds in the same
breath. She is not the world's opposite but its crossing. She is also Nīlasarasvatī, the
Blue one who gives the power of speech, and she gives both enjoyment and liberation — the
maṅgala says exactly this, that in her recollection the enjoyment and the liberation are
both in hand. The two names name her twofold nature: the ferry and the blue, the crossing
and the devourer, the one who carries across and the one who consumes. And the text's
worship is directed at the goddess in whom these two are one.

## The lineage and its geography (m.4)

The maṅgala locates its teaching in a lineage, and the list is a map of the Tārā-cult's
own tradition. Brahmānanda names his sources: the essence of the Tārā, the Tārā-nigama, the
Mahānīla, the Mahācīna, the Nīla-tantra, the Tārā-kalpa, the Śakti-kalpa, the
Rudra-yāmala, the Nīla-sārasvatī, the Liṅga-tantra, the Yoni-tantra, the Ṣoḍhā-tantra —
"these are the all-of-the-lineage (kula-sarvasva) of Tārā, especially in the Upper
Transmission (ūrdhvāmnāya)." The names are the tradition's self-identification. The
recurring *nīla*, "dark," ties the list to the goddess's own blueness — the Mahānīla, the
Nīla-tantra, the Nīla-sārasvatī all carry the color that is Tārā's — and the Mahācīna, the
"great China," marks the tradition's northern, trans-Himālayan reach. The list is not a
bibliography but the lineage's own map: the Tārā-cult as it understood itself, a
transmission of the dark goddess, gathered in the Upper Transmission, and the Tārārahasya
is its secret, its essence distilled.

## The origin-myth (1.1–1.7)

The sṛṣṭi-prakaraṇa begins the story of how Tārā came to be, and the story is the doctrine
made narrative. It is set on the jewel-island, with Sadāśiva questioning Śivā. The myth is
the slaying of Rāvaṇa: the goddess, in her terrible form, with lolling tongue and fierce
aspect, destroyed the demon, bearing the sword and the skull, holding the boon and the
gesture of fearlessness. This is the fierce Tārā, the all-devouring. But the text
immediately turns the myth inward. After the slaying, the goddess is overcome with shame at
her own fierceness — ashamed to be seen as the devourer — and she turns away, her face
bowed, her garment fallen. Brahmā takes up the fallen garment as his skin-cloak; the
goddess holds the girdle and the knife, casts her crown to the ground, and invokes Rudra.

The moment of shame is the hinge of the myth, and it must be read doctrinally. The fierce
goddess who is ashamed of her own devouring is the same goddess who will be called Tārā,
the ferry. The shame is the transition from the aspect that destroys to the aspect that
crosses: the devourer, who consumes the world, becomes, by turning, the one who carries it
across. The myth is the text's way of saying that the fierce and the luminous are one
goddess, and that the fierceness is the condition of the crossing — for what devours the
world is what carries it over. The lolling-tongued one and the blue one are not two but
one, and the origin-myth is the story of their unity.

The close completes the transformation. Tārā takes the dark lotus given her, takes up the
drinking-vessel, and is established: the serpent Akṣobhya on her head, and that very one
is the mahākāla in the form of Tārā in the three worlds. The establishment of the goddess
is the establishment of the identity the myth has enacted — the devourer and the ferry are
one, and she who is recollected gives the enjoyment and the liberation both in hand.

## The body and the rite (the prātaḥkṛtya)

The paṭala's second movement is the morning-rite (prātaḥkṛtya), and it completes the
sṛṣṭi-prakaraṇa by grounding the myth in practice. The rite is the worship of the guru
from the crown (brahmarandhra), the twelve-syllable japa, the offering — the daily
discipline by which the goddess of the origin-myth is made present in the practitioner's
own body. The structure is the text's own: the myth establishes who Tārā is; the
morning-rite establishes where she is worshipped — in the body, at the crown, through the
lineage that the maṅgala's source-list named. The sṛṣṭi-prakaraṇa is thus a complete
self-presentation: it names the goddess, her nature, her lineage, her origin, and her
morning-worship in one continuous movement.

## The philological work (paṭala 4, in passing)

The later paṭalas of the Tārārahasya are the yogācāra, the practice of the yogin, and the
review of that section has already surfaced the real philological work the text requires.
The kuṇḍalinī of the yogācāra is the "three-and-a-half-coiled" (sārdhatrika-valaya), the
standard measure of the coiled power around the liṅga; the "three-koṭi tīrthas in the
body" is the hyperbolic topos that the body contains all the pilgrimage-sites; and the
"sleep" into which one enters when the power merges with the liṅga is the yogic sleep,
the deep absorption, not ordinary sleep. The errors are worth noting because they show what
the text is actually doing: it is a yogic manual of the body, mapping the kuṇḍalinī and the
tīrthas and the absorption onto the practitioner's own form, in the same frame as the
Kākacaṇḍeśvarīmata's alchemy and the Mahāguhyakālīvidhāna's pūjā. The Tārārahasya's body-
practice is the same tradition's work on the body, and its philology must be exact because
the practice is exact.

## The secret's work

The Tārārahasya, read whole, is the Tārā-cult's compact statement: the ferry-goddess in her
blueness, her lineage, her origin-myth, her morning-rite. Its single claim is that the
most feared goddess is the most liberating — that the devourer who slays Rāvaṇa and blushes
at her own ferocity is the ferry who carries across, and that the two aspects are one. The
secret of Tārā is the recognition that the world's devourer is the world's crossing — that
the power that consumes all things is the power that carries them over, and that to worship
her, in the body, through the lineage, is to be carried.
