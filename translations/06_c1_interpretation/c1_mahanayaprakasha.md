# C1 — Mahānayaprakāśa (commentary)

*A commentary on the Mahānayaprakāśa, the "Light of the Great Path" — the Mahānaya, the
other name of the Krama — a work of the school's exegetical peak, composed by Śitikaṇṭha
(Rājānaka Śitikaṇṭhācārya) with his own commentary. The commentary follows the text in
reasoned prose, placing it in its historical and doctrinal frame; the audited translation
is toggled on beneath. Proposal, for review.*

---

## The great path and its light

The Mahānayaprakāśa is the Krama's own doctrine, given systematic form. Its title says
what it is: the mahānaya is the "great path" — the school's other name, which Maṅkhā's
Śrīkaṇṭhacarita already recognized as a distinct teaching — and the prakāśa is its light,
its self-illumination. The text even carries a second name, the Mahārthaprakāśa, "the light
of the great meaning," and the two titles together mark its claim: it is the light of the
great path, the illumination of the great meaning. It is a work of the exegetical period,
after the scriptures — the Kramasadbhāva and the Cidgaganacandrikā sang the goddess, but
here the tradition turns on itself and expounds its own principles. The author is
Śitikaṇṭha, and he supplies his own commentary, so the text carries its interpretation
within it.

The frame that governs everything is the saṃvit-krama — the cognition-sequence: the claim,
which Sanderson's reconstruction makes the school's key, that the order of the worship
(pūjā-krama) reflects and is the order of cognition itself (saṃvit-krama). The Mahānayaprakāśa
is that sequence, unfolded. And the text's own signature move is the daśākhaṇḍa — the ten
Kālīs as the sequence of cognition. The goddesses are not ten deities beside the mind; they
are the ten movements of consciousness, the ten moments of the cognition-sequence, praised
in their order. The text works simultaneously on the sound, the body, and the mind, because
the saṃvit-krama is all three: the phonemes are the powers of the sequence, the body's
stations are its places, and the cognition is its movement.

## The vernacular and the method

The text's most distinctive feature is its language. Much of it is in the vernacular —
the deśabhāṣā, the Apabhraṃśa of the region — and the Sanskrit commentary decodes it verse
by verse. The method is the Krama's own, and it marks the text's place in the tradition.
The school that expounds the cognition-sequence in the learned tongue of the śāstra
preserves its devotional hymns in the living speech, and the two registers are the two
faces of the one teaching: the vernacular sings the goddess, the Sanskrit unfolds the
cognition. The diglossia is not an accident of transmission; it is the school's
self-understanding, the same pattern that runs through the Krama's poetry — the Cidgagana
- candrikā's kāvya, the Mahārthamañjarī's gāthās — where the doctrine is carried in the
vernacular gāthā and unfolded in the commentary. The Mahānayaprakāśa is the most systematic
instance of the method: the verse in the mother-tongue, the meaning in the śāstra.

## The phonemes and their powers (the śāktasiddhā's varṇa-order)

The eighth udaya opens on the phoneme-order of the power-accomplished one, and the method
is visible at once. The verse speaks in the vernacular; the commentary decodes it into the
doctrine. The yakāra-group and the śakāra-group (the yādi and the śādi), the four-voiced
and the great-voiced, the retraction-phoneme the kṣakāra: these are the alphabet's powers,
and the commentary unfolds them. The phonemes are not letters but energies: the a-kāra's
departure leaves the seed of the breath, the vowel becoming the makāra, the anusvāra; the
yakāra-and-the-rest are the members of the melāpa, the joining; the śakāra-and-the-rest
are the voiceless; the hakāra is the great-voiced. And all of them are pervaded by the
powers-goddesses — by this phoneme-worship, the commentary says, the power-abode
(śāktadhāma) is attained.

The method is the Krama's own, and it must be read as such, not as occultism. The phonemes
are the constituents of speech, and speech is the cognition's articulation; to order the
phonemes is to order the cognition itself. The yādi and the śādi, pervaded by the goddesses,
are the saṃvit-krama in the medium of sound. And the kṣakāra, the retraction-phoneme, is
the point toward which the whole moves: being naturally four-phonemed, the commentary says,
it does not desire the other phonemes' nourishment. The kṣakāra is the terminus of the
sequence, the phoneme that has returned, that no longer reaches outward — the retraction
in which the sound-sequence completes itself by ceasing to seek more.

## The stations and the moon (the śāktasiddhā's dhāma-order)

From the phonemes the text passes to the bodily stations, and the two orders are one
sequence. The Śākinī — the one-seat-born, the eighth — rises through the eight stations of
the body: from the birth-support, from the bulb's-fire's-circles, the upward-going rising
and resting; within the heart's door, the staying at the mouth and palate; the drop-wheel
and the crown-wheel; the enclosing, the downward-going, the kula-entry, and the rest in
the supreme station. The stations are the places of the cognition's movement, the body read
as the map of the sequence. And the text's lunar doctrine threads through them: the
moon's-ointment, the drinking of the tongue's-juice, the egg's-expansion, the rest in the
drop, the new-moon. The moon is the cognition's own light, waxing and waning as the
sequence rises and withdraws; the new-moon is its terminus, the point of the retraction.

The amā-kalā, the new-moon phoneme, is the key. The commentary's doctrine is exact: rising
from the drop's door, entering by the bridge-path, the eight risings culminate in the last
amā-phoneme, which pervades the whole kula-mass's life. And by the eight risings, the
moon's melting is accomplished — the melting of the cognition's light into its own supreme
bliss. The amā-kalā is where the sequence turns: the eight risings of the cognition melt
into the single point of its own delight. The moon's sixteenth — the amā, beyond the
fifteen — is the same ṣoḍaśī-structure as the Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava's goddess, the point
beyond the counting in which the counting completes itself.

## The one and the many (M00033:598)

At the heart of the text is the Krama's self-examination, and it is the school's most
honest moment. The question is posed in the commentary at M00033:598: *kimiyam ekaiva
saṃvit, uta anubhūtiviśeṣāt pṛthak pṛthak* — "is this cognition one only, or separate by
the difference of the experiences?" The Krama, whose whole doctrine is the sequence of the
many Kālīs, asks whether the many are finally one or irreducibly many. The question is the
school's own razor. The sequence of the cognition — the ten Kālīs, the eight risings — is
the unfolding of the many; the amā-kalā, the sixteenth, the new-moon, is the point where
the many return to the one. The Mahānayaprakāśa does not flinch from the question; it
raises it and answers it with the moon's melting — the many that, in their completion,
dissolve into the one delight they all express. The text is at once the most systematic and
the most self-questioning of the Krama's works, and its honesty is its depth.

## The Śāmbhava and the recognition-yoke (the śāṃbhavasiddhā)

The eighth udaya closes on the Śāmbhava-accomplished one, and its phoneme-order is the
retraction made explicit. The last-phoneme, the kṣakāra, the retraction-phoneme, is divided
into its four parts by the kakāra, the ṣakāra, the akāra, and the visarjanīya, and by the
Śāmbhavī peace-states it propping, splits, and enters within. The sequence completes
itself: the phonemes that rose in the śāktasiddhā now return in the śāṃbhavasiddhā, the
powers drawn back into their source. The two accomplished ones are the two movements of the
one cognition — the going-out of the power and its return to the supreme.

And the text's self-understanding is the Recognition-yoked Krama. Its commentary cites the
praises and the Gītā's knowledge-verse to anchor its terminus, and the frame is the same
as the Mahārthamañjarī's g.10: the Krama-path and the Pratyabhijñā are one, the
cognition-sequence is the recognition. The Mahānayaprakāśa is where the Krama, having sung
its goddess and worked its phonemes and stations, turns and names what it has been doing:
the order of cognition is the order of recognition, and the great path is the path of
consciousness returning to itself.

## The light of the great path

The Mahānayaprakāśa, read whole, is the Krama's self-possession. It takes the school's
devotion — the goddess, the Kālīs, the vernacular hymns — and gives it the form of a
system, unfolding the cognition-sequence through the phonemes, the body, and the mind, and
naming its terminus in the supreme rest. It is dense, technical, and self-commentated, and
its density is its point: the Krama is not a vague mysticism but a precise order, worked on
the alphabet and the body as much as on the mind. And it is honest — it raises the
question of the one and the many and answers it with the melting of the moon, the many
returned to the one. The great path is the cognition's own movement, and the light that
shows it is this text — the school turned back on itself to see what it had always been
doing: consciousness, rising and returning, recognizing itself.
