# C1 — Kālikārahasya (commentary)

*A commentary on the Kālikārahasya, the "Secret of Kālī," a Kālīkula text drawn from the
Mahābhairavatantra, in the dialogue of Umā and Maheśvara. It treats the Dakṣiṇākālikā as
the supreme of the mantra-hosts. The commentary follows the text in reasoned prose, placing
it in the late Kālīkula layer of the tradition; the audited translation is toggled on
beneath. Proposal, for review.*

---

## The southern Kālī and her tradition

The Kālikārahasya is named for what it guards: the secret of Kālī. Its goddess is the
Dakṣiṇākālikā, the southern Kālī — the most familiar and most feared form of the goddess,
the one with the lolling tongue, the severed head and the sword, seated on the corpse of
Śiva in the burning ground. The text belongs to the late Kālīkula — the stratum of the
tradition that Sanderson maps after the Krama's early scripture. Its roots go back to the
Krama's prototypes: the Devīpañcaśataka, the Kālīkulapañcaśatikā, is one of the two
principal Krama sources, and the northern transmission carried the worship of the northern
Kālī and the Guhyakālī into Nepal, where the Kālīkulakramārcana of Vimalaprabodha (attested
by 1002 CE) made the bridge from doctrine into actual ritual procedure. The Kālikārahasya
stands in this later layer: the same goddess, the Kālī of the Krama's sequence, now
worshiped in the fully-developed ritual of the Kālīkula. Its claim to be drawn from the
Mahābhairavatantra places it in the great Bhairava corpus, and its dialogue of Umā and
Maheśvara is the frame of that revelation.

The text's doctrine is that the Dakṣiṇākālikā is the supreme of the mantra-hosts — the
crown of a whole pantheon of goddess-mantras that the first paṭala catalogs: the Gāyatrī,
the Bagalā, the Śyāmalā, the Nakulī, the Tripurasundarī, the Dhūmāvatī, the Yakṣiṇī, and
the rest, each a power of the great goddess. The Dakṣiṇākālikā is not one goddess among
them; she is their sovereign. The text is frank about what her powers are: the enjoyment
and the liberation, the removal of sins, the warding of enemies and bad dreams, and the
command of the world — the subjugation, the freezing, the bewitching, the killing, the
drawing. The catalog is the Kālīkula's own, and it must be read in the frame the whole
tradition establishes: the same goddess who liberates also commands, because her power is
one — the all-devouring power that both destroys and frees.

## The gift and its gate

The text is equally frank that this knowledge is not for everyone. The mantra is not to be
given to the foolish-minded, the guru's-wealth-robber, the devotion-less, the cruel —
"even at the relinquishing of life, O Pārvatī." The gate is the Kālīkula's self-guarding,
and it is constitutive. The practice is given to the examined — the siddha, the sādhya,
the susiddha, the one tested in the cakra — on the auspicious day, with the worship of the
guru. The gate is the same in the whole northern tradition: the Kālīkulakramārcana, the
practical manual, is precisely the record of how the doctrine is made into procedure by
those who have been taught, and the secrecy is the transmission's own law. The mantra's
power is real, and precisely because it is real, it is entrusted only to the one ready for
it. The power without the readiness is dangerous, and the readiness is what the lineage
tests.

## The mantra's construction

The heart of the text is the arithmetic by which the Dakṣiṇākālikā-mantra is built. The
seed is the vāmākṣī (the left-eyed), the fire, the anusvāra; the dveṣa twofold; the
trimūrti-bindu; the eighteen; the fifty-and-fifth; the Govinda; the Pūṣan-above; the
svāhā-end. The construction is the mantra's body, and the text exposes it as the other
Kālīkula and Krama texts expose theirs — the Nityāṣoḍaśikārṇava's kūṭas, the
Mahānayaprakāśa's phonemes. The mantra is not a brute sound but a composition of the
powers, each part a deity, each joining a step of the goddess's own being. To build the
mantra is to build the goddess, and the practitioner who recites it does not merely invoke
her but re-enacts her construction.

## The form and the practice

The dhyāna gives the goddess's form. The Dakṣiṇākālikā is four-armed, holding the severed
head and the sword, her two other hands in the gestures of the boon and the fearlessness;
she is seated on the corpse of the great Śiva in the burning ground; beside her stands the
Mahākāla; around her is the circle of the yoginīs. The iconography is the whole doctrine
in image: the goddess of the severed head and the sword is the all-devouring; the corpse
beneath her is the stilled world she consumes; the gestures of the boon and the
fearlessness are the freedom she gives. The form is not an idol but a statement — the
terrible and the liberating are one goddess, and the devotee visualizes her as both at
once.

The sādhana is the solitary practice in the burning ground: the vīrāsana, the lone
practitioner, the control of the senses, the diet of the pepper and the mustard, the
four-thousand japa, the offering, the feeding of the learned, the worship with the
red-and-black flowers, the thousand-name armor, the hundred-and-eight stava. The setting —
the burning ground — is the Kālīkula's chosen place, and it is doctrinal: the practitioner
goes where the world's destruction is most visible because that is where the goddess is
most present. The fruit is the prasannā-Kālikā, the gracious Kālī, the presence of the
Mahālakṣmī, the freedom from disease and death. The sādhana's end is not the powers but
the goddess's grace — the all-devouring one made gracious, the terror recognized as the
mother. The whole arc — from the catalog of the powers through the mantra's construction to
the burning-ground sādhana — is the late Kālīkula's worship of the goddess who is at once
the sequence's devourer and its grace.

## The secret of the secret

The Kālikārahasya, read whole, is the late Kālīkula's compact statement: the southern Kālī
as the sovereign of the powers, her mantra built, her form seen, her practice done. Its
single claim is that the most feared goddess is the most liberating — that the
all-devouring power, approached rightly, is the one that gives the boon and the
fearlessness, the freedom from disease and death. The text is stark — the severed head, the
burning ground, the hostile powers — because the goddess it worships is stark; but its end
is the prasannā-Kālikā, the gracious one. The secret of Kālī is that the devourer is the
mother, and that the one who worships her in the burning ground, with the sword and the
skull before her, receives from the terrible the gift of freedom. And the text's place in
the tradition — the late Kālīkula, heir of the Krama's Kālī and the Nepalese ritual's
procedure — shows that this worship is the developed form of the oldest claim: the goddess
who consumes the sequence is the one who grants its release.
