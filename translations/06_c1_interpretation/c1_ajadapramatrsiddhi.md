# C1 — Ajaḍapramātṛsiddhi (commentary)

*A commentary on Utpaladeva's Ajaḍapramātṛsiddhi, the "Proof that the subject is not inert."
The commentary follows the argument through the key verses, letting the reasoning breathe as
it develops; the audited translation is toggled on beneath. Proposal, for review.*

---

## The premise and its refusal (vv. 1–3)

The poem does not open with a defense of consciousness but with the concession that
makes the defense necessary. If each existent is considered only in the isolation of its
own moment — as the Buddhist stream-theorist insists it must be — then nothing within it
identifies it as existent rather than non-existent, conscious rather than inert. The
distinction, on the opponent's own ground, collapses; the momentary particular, shut in
upon itself, has no mark by which it could be told from a stone. Utpaladeva does not
dispute this. He lets the field stand, because the battle is not to be fought over the
reality of an external self, which the Buddhist has no difficulty in dissolving, but over
the nature of the momentary cognition itself — the one thing the opponent cannot abandon
without abandoning consciousness altogether.

It is there that the counter-move begins. Existence, the text urges, is not a property
that a thing possesses in its own right; it is being-manifested, being disclosed and
determinately apprehended. The two marks of existence — *prakhyā* and *upākhyā*,
manifestation and designation — are, as the Vṛtti makes explicit, the two modes of
consciousness itself: *prakāśa* and *vimarśa*, luminosity and reflexive apprehension. The
being of the world is thus from the first folded into the being of the knower. But the
Buddhist may still refuse the conclusion: let existence be relational, let the object's
status be borrowed from the cognition that discloses it; a pragmatic distinction between
existence and non-existence suffices, and no eternal essence is required.

The reply, when it comes, turns the same instrument against the opponent. If the object's
being rests on being-manifested, then it rests on another, and what rests on another has
no one stable nature of its own — it is not one-formed, and so oversteps existence. The
dilemma that dissolves the object dissolves, by the same stroke, the consciousness that
was to establish it. For a momentary cognition, shut in upon its own flash, is as
incapable of grounding its object as the object was of grounding itself. The Buddhist has
traded a self that cannot be found for a consciousness that cannot do anything; and it is
precisely this incapacity — this inertness of the bare flash — that the argument now
seizes upon and destroys.

## The crystal and the sky-flower (vv. 9–10)

The decisive image is the crystal. A crystal set among red things displays redness, yet
knows neither the redness nor itself; it shines and remains, for all its shining, a thing.
If consciousness were luminosity without self-apprehension, it would be exactly such a
crystal: it would illumine the world and be, for that very luminosity, as inert as the
stone the Buddhist makes of it. The refutation gathers its targets in a single glance — the
Buddhist's bare flashes, and with them the "calm-Brahmā" view of an inactive, merely
luminous absolute, are alike pictures of a light that does not know it shines. What
converts luminosity into consciousness, what separates the mind from the stone, is not
that it shines but that it *reflects* — that it apprehends its own shining. Reflexivity is
not an addition to consciousness; it is the very trait by which consciousness is
consciousness, and the Buddhist, in stripping his cognition of it, has left himself with a
glowing thing and called it a mind.

The consequence is drawn with an image that admits of no reply. The non-reflexive
awareness cannot even establish itself as occurring — it has no hold on its own being — and
what cannot establish itself cannot establish another. The sky-flower, which cannot exist,
cannot give birth to fragrance; the bare flash, which cannot found itself, cannot found the
object the Buddhist still wants to call real. The opponent's consciousness, reduced to a
flash, explains neither itself nor its world. It is not that the Buddhist has too little
consciousness; it is that what he has left himself is not consciousness at all. This is the
foundational claim of the Pratyabhijñā, and everything that follows in the system — the
recognition, the freedom, the lordship — is the elaboration of this single difference
between a light that knows itself and a light that does not.

## The "this" and the "I" (vv. 11, 15)

The argument has so far been negative: a non-reflexive light is inert. But to stop here
would be to win only a solitary self-awareness — a reflexion that knows itself and nothing
else, that has not yet explained why the world is real or why its objects are genuinely
ours. The poem therefore turns to the positive work, and the turn is the more original half
of its achievement.

Every experience of an object presents it under a double aspect. There is the "this" — the
thing set over against the subject, spread out, distinct; and there is the "I" in which
that "this" appears as mine. The two are not separable experiences but two moments of one
presentation, and Utpaladeva's claim is that the first is unintelligible without the
second. An object disclosed as "this" is established, brought to completion, only by
resting in the "I" that owns it; the Vṛtti's formulation is exact — without the resting in
the "I," the "this" remains unestablished from itself. The reflexion that completes the
world is *so 'ham*, "I am this": the divided object-world is not abolished by consciousness
but gathered into it, owned by it, and in that ownership made real. This is the seed of the
Recognition — that consciousness is not a witness of objects but the field in which objects
are gathered into the self, and that this gathering, not mere perception, is what gives the
world its reality.

## The contested summit (v. 16)

The poem concludes by asserting what it cannot, within these verses, fully demonstrate:
that this very self is twofold, limited and unlimited, the breath-bound individual and the
undivided absolute being one and the same. The claim must be read honestly. The argument
from reflexivity secures the unity of one knower across its own moments — it shows that the
cognitions of a single subject are bound by a single synthesizing reflexion. Whether it
reaches the further claim, that the finite "I" and the infinite self are numerically one,
is genuinely open; the verse asserts the identity where the wider Recognition corpus argues
it. The commentary does not manufacture a proof the text does not give. It lets the
assertion stand in its full reach, and lets the reader weigh whether the reflexion that
owns this "this" is the reflexion that owns all "this"s — one awareness breathing as many.
