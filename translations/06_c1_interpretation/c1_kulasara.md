# C1 — Kulasāra (commentary)

*A commentary on the Kulasāra, an early Kaula scripture (pre-11th century, quoted by the
nondualist Kashmiri Śaiva authors) preserved in a single incomplete manuscript. The
surviving text treats the body as the field of liberation and the akula-kula-kaula as the
doctrine. The commentary follows the text in reasoned prose, placing it in the trajectory
of the Kaula's central term; the audited translation is toggled on beneath. Proposal, for
review.*

---

## The body as the field

The Kulasāra opens, as far as the surviving manuscript allows, on the pīṇḍa — the
psycho-physical body — as the field where liberation is won. The practice is the
one-pīṇḍa absorption: the whole drawn together into a single body, the all made body, the
world absorbed into the one form. The five element-yogas follow — the earth, the water,
the wind, the ether, and the being-less beyond them — as the ladder by which the body is
mastered and then transcended. The frame is the Kaula's founding orientation: the body is
not the obstacle to liberation but its site. The elements that make the body are the
elements that constitute the world, and to master them in the body is to master the world.
The pīṇḍa is a microcosm, and the Kaula works on it directly, winning the siddhis and the
no-mind state in the same field.

The kāmarāja is the mantra-power of this practice. The "king of desire" is the seed by
which the body's mastery is effected — the desire that, turned inward, becomes the power
to gather the world into the one body. The text is unembarrassed about the name: desire is
not renounced in the Kaula but transfigured. "By desire all is accomplished for him —
whatever is in his mind. Therefore it is called kāma." The line is the Kaula's theology of
will: the same force that scatters the self into the world is, recognized, the force that
gathers it back.

## The term and its history

The Kulasāra's central doctrine is the triad of the akula, the kula, and the kaula, and the
terms carry a long history that gives them their force. The word *kula* is one of the most
mobile in the whole tradition, and the dossier on it traces its trajectory. In the early
Yoginī-sources it means the family, the lineage — the classification of the Mothers and the
Yoginīs; in the developed Kaula it is extended by homonym to mean the body, the totality of
power and phenomena; in the Kubjikā tradition it becomes the mantra-body, the structured
Kula; and in Abhinavagupta it is finally fixed as the manifest pole opposed to the
transcendent *akula* — the supreme abode is akula, and its visarga, its emission, is the
kaulikī power. The Kulasāra's triad is the meeting-point of this whole history: the kula
as the aggregate that holds the transcendent, and the kaula as their union.

## The akula, the kula, the kaula (paṭala 11)

The triad is stated with a precision that rewards close reading. The akula is first: that
which has no kula, the transcendent supreme — all-going, all-doing, all-pervading, the
great lord, devoid of the to-be-rejected-and-taken, the one alone, the second-less. It is
the absolute as it is in itself, beyond every relation. The kula is the aggregate: the
gathering in which the akula is held — the bearer of the knowledge and the action, the
power by which, through the will alone, the moving-and-unmoving world arises. And the kaula
is the union of the two — the recognition that the transcendent akula and the immanent
kula are one, that the absolute is not a remote beyond but the very gathering that
constitutes the world.

The relation is everything. The akula without the kula would be an inaccessible beyond;
the kula without the akula would be a mere heap. The kaula is the third that unites them,
and it is the Kaula's whole claim. The text presses this into the doctrine of the
pervasion: the kula's power runs from Śiva down to the earth, filling the whole, so that
there is no point at which the transcendent is absent. And it presses it into the strongest
assertion of the paṭala — that without the kula there is no knowledge, no yoga, no siddhi,
no sādhana, no mantra, no perception of one's own form. The transcendent is only reached
through the aggregate that holds it; the akula is known in and through the kula, and the
knowing of their union is the kaula. The text is the developed form of the term's whole
trajectory: the lineage-family of the early Yoginī-sources has become the aggregate-body of
the developed Kaula, and the kaula is the recognition that the body is the transcendent.

## The warning and the gift (paṭala 11, close)

The paṭala turns from the doctrine to its conditions, and the turn is exact. The knowledge
of the kula is given by the guru's mouth as nectar and awakened by the descent of power
(śaktipāta); the warning that follows — those who abandon or scorn it go to the hells and
the low wombs — is the text's own ritual-ethics. The gift and the warning are two faces of
the same law: the knowledge is given by the lineage, and it is given because without it —
without the recognition of the kula that holds the akula — there is no liberation at all.
The lineage that transmits the kula is itself the kula in its social sense: the family of
the teachers through whom the doctrine descends, the concrete form of the aggregate that
the doctrine names transcendent.

## The non-dual conduct (paṭala 14)

The Kulasāra's most daring paṭala is the non-dual conduct, and its doctrine is the mind's
purification. The external rites — the washing, the ablutions of a body born of blood and
semen — do not purify, the text insists, any more than charcoal loses its nature by washing
or the black streak is scrubbed away. Purity is not a property of the body but of the
mind, and only the mind's purification frees. The critique of the Vedic rites follows from
the same principle: they seek heaven, a reward in another life, and so they presuppose the
duality of this world and the next that the Kaula dissolves. The non-dual is the
recognition that the world is one — the five elements pervaded by the one tattva, the one
seed from which all arises, so that there is no real two in the eatable and the uneatable.

The paṭala carries this to its most provocative conclusion: the renunciation of both the
dvaita and, as a grasping, even the advaita, in favor of the abiding that is the
kula-kaula-bhāvanā. The non-dual is not a position held against the dual; it is the state
in which the very distinction of holding-and-rejecting has ceased. And the fruit is the
jīvanmukta — the one liberated while living — who is the sākṣād-Bhairava, equal everywhere,
like milk in milk. The jīvanmukta does not escape the world; he is the world's own truth
made manifest, the absolute living as a body. The word *bhāvanā* is the key: the
kula-kaula-bhāvanā is the *cultivation* of the union, the practice by which the mind is
brought to rest in the aggregate that is the transcendent — the whole conduct-doctrine
existing to produce this one who is already the absolute.

## The madya and the māṃsa (paṭala 14, close)

The paṭala ends with the text's most famous doctrine — the wine and the meat — and it must
be read, as the text itself insists, non-dually. The madya and the māṃsa are not, at
bottom, the literal substances; they are the supreme substances understood in the frame the
paṭala has established. The "mā" is the na-seed, the supreme amṛta; the māṃsa is the
substance; the śiva-yajña is the best of sacrifices. The reversal is the Kaula's
signature: what the orthodox call impure is here the vehicle of the supreme, because purity
and impurity are states of the mind, and the mind that has purified itself is fit for the
substance in which the one pervades. The wine and the meat, taken in the non-dual conduct,
are the substances of the recognition — the world's own matter, seen as it is, pervaded by
the one seed from which it all arises.

## The recognition of the kula

The Kulasāra, read whole, is the Kaula's foundational text: the body as the field, the
kāmarāja as the will's power, the akula-kula-kaula as the doctrine, and the non-dual
conduct as the way. Its single claim runs through all of it: the transcendent is not
beyond but present — present in the body, in the aggregate, in the substance — and
liberation is the recognition of that presence, the kaula that unites the akula and the
kula, lived as the jīvanmukta who is already the absolute. The text is early, corrupt in
its manuscript, and deliberately transgressive; and it is, for all that, one of the
clearest statements of what the Kaula is — not the rejection of the world but its
recognition, the supreme known in and through the kula that holds it.
