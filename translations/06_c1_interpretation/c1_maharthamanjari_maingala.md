# C1 — Mahārthamañjarī, maṅgala (commentary)

*A commentary on the maṅgala of the Mahārthamañjarī, the Krama's crown-work of
Maheśvarānanda (c. 1150 CE), with his own commentary, the Parimala. The maṅgala is the
poem's self-positioning: it names the author's allegiance, his deity, and — above all — the
frame through which he will read the whole tradition. The commentary follows that
self-positioning in reasoned prose; the audited translation is toggled on beneath. Proposal,
for review.*

---

## The tusk and the root (m.1–2)

The maṅgala opens with the double homage proper to a work of this kind, and the two objects
of homage are themselves a first statement of the doctrine. The tusk and the trunk — the
lord who toys with his trunk, tusk against the lotus-stalk, the inner lotus-root, ear-leafed
— is Gaṇeśa, the remover of obstacles, the guardian of beginnings. But the second verse
names what the tusk's play points toward: the principle that is rootless, unwithered,
supreme, non-dual — the great lotus of the nectar that is the pulse, the pulse's rest, and
the pulse's outpouring. The maṅgala has already sounded the Krama's signature note. The
supreme reality is not a static ground; it is *spanda* in its threefold rhythm — the pulse,
its rest, its outpouring — and this rhythm, not a bare stillness, is the lotus from which
the whole work will unfold. The pulse is the cognition's own rhythm, and it is against
this frame that every technical term in the maṅgala is to be read.

## The pearl and the opening-and-closing (m.3–4)

The homage passes to the Goddess — the weighty form bearing the pearl-wrought earring, risen
as it were from the whirlpool of the compassion-nectar sea. The pearl at the ear is not an
ornament only; it is the gathered drop of the sea of compassion, the compassion itself
becoming visible. And the next verse names what the Goddess's very being is: the
ever-opened-and-shut, the huskless one — the radiance for the flashing of the world's
craft. The opening-and-closing (unmeṣa-nimeṣa) is the cognition's own pulse, the same
rhythm the tusk-verse named as spanda. The Goddess does not merely have this rhythm; she
is it. The world's craft flashes because she opens and closes — and the two movements,
opening and closing, are not two acts but the one pulse of her awareness, which the maṅgala
already refuses to reduce to a static shining.

## The guru's feet and the cognition-sequence (m.5)

The maṅgala reaches its most programmatic verse, and it is here that Maheśvarānanda states
the frame through which he will read everything. From the pair of the gurus' feet he derives
his plunge into the unsurpassed great pool and his auspicious Śāmbhava-joy; and those feet,
he says, are made of the self's consciousness-order-reflexion — *svātma-cit-krama-vimarśa*.
The guru-feet are the cognition-sequence's reflexion. The compound gathers the whole Krama
in a phrase: the guru is not an external teacher standing before the disciple but the order
of consciousness (cit-krama) apprehending itself (vimarśa); to bow to the guru is to
recognize the reflexive sequence that is one's own awareness. The guru is the
cognition-sequence, and the homage to the feet is the recognition that the sequence is
already one's own.

This is the maṅgala's deepest claim, and it governs the entire text. If the guru is the
cognition-sequence, then initiation and teaching are not the transfer of a doctrine from
one person to another but the recognition of the sequence that the disciple already is. The
great pool into which Maheśvarānanda plunges is the same sequence; the auspicious joy is
its recognition. The whole of the Mahārthamañjarī is the unfolding of this one insight —
that the path, the teacher, and the goal are all the single reflexive order of
consciousness.

## The opening of the secret (m.6–9)

The maṅgala then turns on its own authority and its own audience. Maheśvarānanda names
himself — a Gorakṣa to the world's eye, a teacher in the guru's view — and declares that he
opens the Parimala, the inwardly-grasped commentary on the Mahārthamañjarī. The self-naming
is precise about its audience. He supplies a gloss on his own work, eager for the
reiteration of the enjoyment of his own fervor; or else, for the wonder of the teachable
minds, let this endeavor arise. The two clauses address two kinds of reader: the one who
will recognize the cognition-sequence in the text (and for whom the work is its own
enjoyment), and the teachable one who comes to it for wonder. The maṅgala is careful to
hold both, and the closing of the passage — the verbal flower-offering of Śambhu, its
beauty parted into brief-and-extended — acknowledges that the text is an offering, a
garland of speech laid before the lord, not a claim to have exhausted the meaning.

## The recognition-yoked Krama (m.10–11)

The maṅgala's doctrinal self-positioning is completed in two verses that name the tradition
and its guardians. Maheśvarānanda declares himself dense with the skill of serving the
guru's feet, one who knows the whole secret-springing of the Krama-path's truth — the
meaning of the Śivadṛṣṭi and the Recognition understood — and who unfolds even the dense
inner-sky of the heart. The author explicitly binds the Krama-path to the Śivadṛṣṭi and to
the Pratyabhijñā, making the recognition of consciousness the meaning of the sequence.
The Krama's cognition-sequence and the Recognition's reflexive apprehension are one
teaching — the Pratyabhijñā running, in this text, along the Krama's own track.

The guarding verse that follows is the maṅgala's warning to the unready. The grace of the
awareness-yoginīs prevails for the auspicious rite; but the intelligent who are barren,
tied to the reflection (bimba) of the kula-worship, are told not to open this, even once.
The warning is doctrinal, not merely protective. Those who stop at the reflection — at the
ritual image, the external worship, the bindu as a thing — have not recognized the
cognition-sequence that the reflection mirrors, and to such the text's inner teaching would
be not a recognition but a possession. The mahārtha is not withheld out of secrecy but
because it is invisible to a reading that stops at the reflection.

## The dream-won goddess (m.12–13)

The maṅgala closes with its most intimate image and its benediction. The goddess — the
accomplished yoginī — was won at the time of dream, and the seventy gāthās are offered in
her own-awakened tongue for her delight. The dream-won goddess is not a fanciful origin;
it is the Krama's own epistemology in a single stroke. The supreme meaning is not arrived at
by calculation but given in the spontaneous, awakened state — the dream in which the
cognition-sequence, free of the waking mind's grasping, shows itself as it is. The gāthās
are in "her own tongue," the language of that awakened awareness, and the whole work is
offered to her as the praise that is itself the recognition.

The final verse is the simple benediction that closes a maṅgala: may the blessed teacher
flourish; may the path-of-awareness flourish; may the Māheśvaras flourish; and may
Maheśvara flourish. The list is not conventional. The teacher, the path (saṃvin-mārga), the
disciples, and the lord are all wished to flourish — and in the frame the maṅgala has
established, these are not four separate beings but the one cognition-sequence in its
unfolding: the teacher who is the sequence, the path that is its recognition, the
Māheśvaras who are its practitioners, and Maheśvara who is its reality. The maṅgala ends
where it began, with the pulse of consciousness — now blessed to flourish in all its
forms.
