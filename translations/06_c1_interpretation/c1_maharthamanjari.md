# C1 — Mahārthamañjarī (commentary)

*A commentary on the Mahārthamañjarī, "the cluster of the great meaning," the Krama's
crown-work composed by Maheśvarānanda (c. 1150 CE) with his own commentary, the Parimala.
The commentary follows the text's argument as it develops, letting the doctrine unfold in
reasoned prose; the audited translation is toggled on beneath. Proposal, for review.*

---

## The daze and its cure (g.8–9)

The poem opens not on a doctrine but on the condition that makes a doctrine necessary. The
world is dazed — struck, as by venom, with the dread of saṃsāra — and it is dazed because it
has not examined its own nature. The image is careful: the world is not dazed by being
wicked or lost, but by failing to look into the heart where it already stands. It is *in*
the self's heart, and does not know it. The Mahārthamañjarī is that examination, the
turning of the look that ends the daze by showing the world where it has always been.

The cure is then pictured with the poem's most exact image. The self is a peerless ruby,
luminous with its own light — but wrapped in that light, so that those who look see only
the glow and not the gem. The paradox is the point: the self is utterly manifest, for it is
the very light in which everything appears, and yet unmanifest, for no one sees it, because
it is the seeing. The Ajaḍapramātṛsiddhi had argued this as reflexivity — that what makes
consciousness consciousness is that it apprehends its own shining. The Mahārthamañjarī does
not argue it; it pictures it. The ruby is not hidden behind its light; it is the light, and
that is precisely why it is not seen. The seer cannot see itself as an object, not because
it is far, but because it is too near — it is the very act of seeing.

## The lamp and the agent (g.10–11)

From the jewel the text passes to the lamp, and the scale widens from the single self to
the cosmos. The lamp of auspiciousness burns upward, illumining the seen and the unseen
alike; its fuel is the senses — the flow of sense-oil — and its flame is reflexion, drawn
into the ten faces of the Krama's cognition. The image carries a claim that inverts the
ordinary picture of perception. Consciousness does not receive the world as light falling
on a surface; it *burns* the world into light. Every object fed into the flame becomes
illumination, transmuted from matter into awareness. The lamp needs no external wick; it is
fed by experience and consumes experience into its own flame.

That the lamp is an *agent* and not a witness follows at once, and it is the theological
heart of the Krama. The heart's light, just by being, is the agent of the deed of creation
— no effort, no intention, no will outside itself. To shine is already to act, and the deed
is the reflexion itself: the action-reflexion (kriyā-vimarśa) that, at rest, is the world's
quiet ground and, once stirred, is the world's widening. This is svātantrya, the freedom of
consciousness — not a power it exercises on the world from outside, but the sheer upsurge
of its own light, which does not need to reach out because everything is already within it.

## The differentiation of the world (g.12–15)

The cosmology follows from the same principle, and the text reads the whole ladder of
reality — from earth at the base to Śiva at the summit — as the heart's own rising. What
distinguishes each level from the next, what marks the differences that constitute the
cosmos, is the reflexive upsurge (vimarśonmeṣa). The differences are not brute facts that
consciousness observes; they are differentiations that consciousness *makes*, its own
unfolding. The universe is not a set of already-distinct levels waiting to be seen; it is
reflexion articulating itself, and the ladder is the reflexion's rise.

The many powers and the one that gathers them follow. The thousand śaktis — the endless
energies of manifestation — are, in their single confluence, what is named Śiva: not a
being standing beyond the powers, but their unity-in-upsurge, the supreme spontaneity they
all express. The nondualism is exact: not one thing beside many, but the many as the one's
own self-expression. And because consciousness is not light alone but light with its
reflexion, the cosmos is read as a sequence of the powers' unsealing — at the first
arising, knowledge and action are one (Sadāśiva); at the second sketch, the differentiation
of knower from known, he becomes the limited Lord. The cosmological levels are not spatial
stories; they are degrees of reflexion's contraction. The same one, open, is Sadāśiva;
closed, is the bound lord. Creation is self-limitation, and the levels are the reflexion's
own veils.

## Māyā and the sweetness of matter (g.17, g.25)

The text refuses the easy reading of māyā. She is not an external illusion that traps a
foreign self; she is the bewildering might of the utterly free lord himself — the power by
which the one reflexion, in its freedom, makes the crafts of division well up within its
own one-flavored nature. Māyā does not create a second reality over against the first; she
is the freedom that plays at division within unity. The world of difference is not a lie
laid over the truth; it is the truth amusing itself with the forms of "this" and "that."

The density of matter is the same claim in another key. The five elements — the very stuff
of the material world — are, by the settling of the cane-juice, like lumps of raw sugar
that never let go their sweetness. The sweetness is consciousness. Even the densest matter
is consciousness settled, condensed, sugared — never truly losing the light it is made of.
The Śiva-light runs through the elements as sweetness runs through sugar; there is no point
at which the world becomes finally inert. The inert is only light that has forgotten
itself, and the elements, however far they have settled from their source, still hold the
taste of the light within them.

## The world strung and the paired surge (g.26–27, g.29)

The great might of Śambhu is pictured as a thread on which the worlds are strung — and the
thread itself is reflexion-fervor, the passionate reflexive energy of consciousness. The
image matters because the string is not external to the flowers: the reflexion does not
hold the worlds up from outside; it is the worlds' own stringing. The universe is a garland
that consciousness strings out of itself and wears. The paired surge (yāmala) is the same
insight taken one step further. The six paths of the cosmos divide into two halves — the
half of the light (prakāśa) and the half of the reflexion-word (vimarśa-śabda) — and both
are the one paired surge of Śiva. Consciousness is not light alone; it is light *and* the
articulation of its light, shining and the name of its shining, and the two are inseparable
as the two halves of a single surge.

The argument from scale follows in a tone of triumph. A worm in a body no bigger than a
sesame seed exercises extraordinary power; how vast, then, the power of the whole
world-body, the fortune of the free? The point is not wonder at size but the demonstration
that power is not diminished by being contained. The infinite is not less infinite for
appearing in a grain. The world-body's freedom is the worm's freedom opened to its true
measure — the same reflexion, unconfined.

## The recognition as rite (g.46, g.50)

The ritual language of the Krama turns inward, and the worship is shown to be the
recognition performed as rite. The true oblation (tarpaṇa) is made at the kula's point —
the gathering of all the powers in the full "I" — and what is scattered there is the
world's split-sprouts, the seeds of division. The mantra is not a sound offered outward to
a distant deity; it is the sketch by which the full-I scatters division and collects the
world back into itself. The "pure merit" is the reflexion that unifies, and the rite is the
means by which that unification is enacted and recognized.

Speech is the same doctrine in the medium of sound. The ladder of vāc — vaikharī, madhyamā,
paśyantī, and the subtle one beyond — is mapped onto the powers: the outer speech is deed,
the mid is knowledge, the will is the seer-speech, and above them all is the one-flavored
working that is all of them at once. Speech is not an addition to consciousness; it is
consciousness articulating itself at ever-deeper levels, down to the silent one-flavored
source from which all words well. To know the goddess is to trace speech back to its silent
root, where the many words are one.

## The states and the paired attainment (g.61, g.65)

The three states — waking, dream, deep sleep — are not three separate rooms in which the
self moves. They are pearls threaded on a single cord, and the cord is reflexion. The yogi
does not pass from one state to another; he wears them all at once, strung on the awareness
that is present through all three and is none of them. This is the Fourth — the cord itself
made visible — and liberation is not escaping the states but threading them, holding them
all on one string of reflexive awareness.

The tone at the end breaks into exclamation — the only register adequate to the conclusion.
"Ah" thrice: the world's joy is sport; the fortune of the release-road is easy; the
attainment of the Śiva-yogis is paired, the union of enjoyment and release made one. The
fret-stain of duality is shattered. Recognition is not a grim labor but a sudden, easy,
laughing attainment — the lightness is doctrinal, not merely rhetorical. Because the self
was never actually bound, its release is not an achievement but a recognition, and the
recognition is as easy as seeing that the thread was already through all the pearls.

## The churner and the frame (g.68, g.70)

The text names its own supremacy, and the naming reaches across traditions. The other
teachings are the four seas; this one alone is the nectar, and it is churned by
Manthānabhairava — the Churning-Bhairava, the god of the great Kubjikā corpus. The image
is the churning of the ocean: what is sought is the nectar, and the churner is Bhairava
himself. The verse is the Krama's bridge to the Kubjikā tradition — the Manthānabhairava is
the vast scripture of the goddess Kubjikā, and here the Mahārthamañjarī names that corpus
as the churner of the one nectar this text holds. The schools are not rivals; the one
meaning is churned by many gods.

The closing frame completes the reach. The "great-meaning" was taught at the opening of the
Pāṇḍu-son's war — the Bhāgavad Gītā — by Mādhava, the god of sixteen-thousand powers, a
Krama-Kṛṣṇa identification. The claim is that the doctrine of this text is the ancient
recognition surfacing again, taught under another name at the brink of the great battle,
when the choice between identity and action was most stark. The Mahārthamañjarī is not a
sectarian novelty but the recognition returning — the same truth that Kṛṣṇa spoke when the
world's war was about to begin, spoken now in the idiom of the Krama.

## The closing praise (g.71)

The poem ends by praising the yoginī — the goddess of the great-meaning — whose only wealth
is the throat-bone and the skull, the emblems of the transcendent that has passed through
death. She unites the seventy upsurges into one, descends from waking through the
non-distinct dream, and overleaps the world. The closing is deliberately strange: the
greatest teaching is given by one whose possessions are death's relics, whose path overleaps
the world. The Krama's goddess is not the comforter but the one who has passed through and
beyond everything — and who, in her skull and bone, holds the whole meaning.
