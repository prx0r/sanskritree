# "Beyond the Pair": The Goddess of the Opening in the Kramasadbhāva's Maṅgala

*A reading and commentary on Kramasadbhāva 1.8 and its place in the opening stuti.*

---

## Preface

This is a commentary on the eighth verse of the *Kramasadbhāva* — the "Essence of the
Krama," a scriptural source of the Krama (Kālīkrama) school of Kashmir Śaivism. It is
written to be read on its own: the verse is quoted in full, set in the context of the
hymn that contains it and the tradition that produced it, and its one genuinely contested
word is examined with the evidence on both sides. Nothing here is offered as a final
answer; it is a proposal, for review, by which a reader can judge the question for herself.

The *Kramasadbhāva* is the Krama school's own scripture — a liturgical-philosophical
treatise transmitted, in the form used here, in a Newari manuscript edited by Mark
Dyczkowski. Its name means "the Essence of the Krama," and its central concern is the
goddess Kālī understood as the power of consciousness in its dynamic self-unfolding. The
eighth verse is the heart of the poem's opening hymn.

---

## I. The verse and its text

The verse (Kramasadbhāva 1.8, in the Dyczkowski edition) reads:

> *ooṃ namaste devadeveśi mahākāli namo'stu te /
> namo'stu paramānande nirānande namo'stu te //*

A close rendering:

> Oṃ. Homage to you, mistress of the god of gods, great Kālī — homage be to you.
> Homage be to you, O supreme-bliss; O bliss-less-one, homage be to you.

The verse is a single act of homage (*namas*), broken into four vocatives, each naming the
goddess in a different register: *devadeveśī* (the mistress of the god of gods), *mahākālī*
(the great Kālī), *paramānande* (the supreme-bliss), and *nirānande* (the bliss-less, or the
bliss-at-rest). It opens the *stuti*, the hymn of praise that constitutes the first section
of the text's first chapter, before the dialogue proper begins.

The stanza is not a random list of titles. It is the first move of a carefully constructed
liturgical poem, and its structure — and the one word that resists easy translation — carry
the whole meaning of the hymn.

---

## II. The mistress of the god of gods

The first vocative is *devadeveśī*. The compound repays attention. *Deva-deva* is the
doubling that makes a superlative: "god of gods," the highest god, Śiva himself. The suffix
*-īśī* (from *īśā*, "mistress") feminizes the title: she is the mistress of the god of
gods, the one who governs the supreme Lord. It is the same formation we meet a verse later,
at 1.9, in *kuleśī* and *kauleśī* — "mistress of the kula" and "mistress of the Kaula
[path]" — and the recurrence is not accidental. The hymn is establishing, from its first
syllable, that the goddess is not one power among many but the sovereign of the highest
power, the mistress of the very source of all the gods.

The doubling *devadeva* is the key. It is not that she is a god among gods, or even the
greatest god; it is that she stands in relation to the "god of gods" as its mistress. In
the Krama's reckoning this is precise: the goddess — Kālī — is the *śakti*, the dynamic
power of consciousness, and Śiva is the *śaktimān*, the possessor of that power. She is not
less than he; she is his own power, the mistress of the source. The hymn's first move is
therefore a declaration of nonduality: the supreme reality is not a solitary Lord but a
Lord-in-his-power, and that power is the goddess to whom the hymn is offered.

---

## III. The great Kālī

The second vocative, *mahākālī*, names her by her distinctive form. Kālī is the "black
one," the goddess of time and of dissolution; *mahākālī* is the "great Kālī." Throughout
the hymn that follows, the text will describe her in the language of the body and of the
cosmos — as the one stationed in the heart, in the throat, in the palate; as the
all-devouring (*sarvabhakṣyā*, at 1.20), the destroyer of the worlds.

It is essential to read this correctly. The "devouring" of Kālī is not, in the Krama, a
merely destructive or terrifying image. It is the signature doctrine of the school: Kālī is
the power of consciousness that *consumes* every object, every state, every moment — that
eats the world of duality back into the awareness that alone remains. To be "devoured" by
Kālī is to be dissolved into consciousness. The goddess who will, in the course of the
hymn, be stationed at every locus of the body is the power that absorbs experience into
the light that witnesses it.

So when the verse names her *mahākālī*, it is already invoking the school's central image:
consciousness as the all-consuming, all-absorbing power in which the entire manifest world
arises and dissolves.

---

## IV. The crux: supreme-bliss and the bliss-less

The third and fourth vocatives form a pair, and it is here that the verse's meaning — and
its difficulty — lies:

> *namo'stu paramānande nirānande namo'stu te*
> Homage be to you, O supreme-bliss; O bliss-less-one, homage be to you.

*Paramānande* is unproblematic: "O supreme bliss," the vocative of the goddess as
transcendent joy. The difficulty is *nirānande*.

The word is built on the privative prefix *nir-*: *nir-ānande*, literally "the bliss-less
one," the one without bliss. On the surface, the most direct reading, it is the negation of
*ānanda* — the goddess who is bliss and, in the same breath, its absence. And the parallel
at 1.9 — *namo nitye tvanitye ca* ("homage to you, O eternal, and to you, O non-eternal") —
seems to confirm this pattern. The hymn repeatedly addresses the goddess across a pair: here
supreme-bliss and bliss-less; there eternal and non-eternal; at 1.14, "the cessation-end of
being and non-being" (*bhāvābhāvavirāmānte*). The structure is unmistakable: **the goddess
is addressed across an opposition, and the oppositions are all the same opposition.**

Now comes the question that the privative reading alone cannot settle. If *nirānande* means
simply "bliss-less," then the pair *paramānande / nirānande* places the goddess *within* an
opposition — she is supreme-bliss and, as it were, its opposite. But everywhere else in the
hymn, the goddess is presented as *beyond* the pair, as the term in which both poles
dissolve. At 1.9 she is eternal *and* non-eternal; at 1.14 she is the cessation-end of being
and non-being; at 1.18 she is beyond the peace (*śāntātīte*); at 1.25 she is beyond the
mark-less itself (*nirlakṣe*). The hymn's logic is consistently that of transcendence of
opposites, not the selection of one pole.

The same pressure appears in the Krama tradition's own understanding of the term. The
Mahānaya recension of this very verse renders *nirānande* as "the Bliss of Stillness," and
Kubjikā-related material connects *nirānanda* with *nirācārānanda* — a technical "bliss of
stillness," a joy that is no longer the flux of joy and its loss. On this reading, the pair
is not "supreme-bliss / its absence" but "supreme-bliss / bliss-at-rest" — two poles of one
transcendent state, both beyond the experience of pleasure and pain. The goddess who is
beyond bliss is, precisely, the bliss-at-rest: not the negation of joy but the stillness in
which joy and sorrow have already dissolved.

These are not, in the end, mutually exclusive. The privative morphology (*nir-*) is real,
and it must be honored in the surface reading. But the privative can mean two things: the
negation of bliss ("bliss-less") or the transcendence of the bliss/its-loss opposition
("beyond-bliss, at rest"). The hymn's structure, and the Krama's own usage, point toward
the second. The honest scholarly position is that *nirānande* is genuinely OPEN between
these readings — the surface privative and the technical "stillness" — and that a final
verdict belongs to a specialist in early Kālīkrama.

## V. The frame: what the Krama means

To understand why the crux matters, one must see what the word "Krama" itself means in
this school — and why a goddess addressed as both supreme-bliss and bliss-at-rest is the
goddess of the Krama.

The Krama (whose other name is Mahānaya, "the great path") is, as the tradition and the
eleventh-century poet Maṅkhā both recognize, a school in which reality is understood as a
*cycle*. The Kashmir Śaiva teacher Kṣemarāja defines Krama from the Krama-sūtras as that
which causes emanation, persistence, and withdrawal to appear *in succession* — and which
constitutes the very nature of that succession. The school's liturgy is built on a fourfold
— sometimes fivefold — sequence of the phases of consciousness:

```
sṛṣṭikrama    emanation       the arising of the manifest
sthitikrama   persistence     the holding of the manifest
saṃhārakrama  absorption      the withdrawal of the manifest
anākhyakrama  the inexplicable  the three contained in oneness
bhāsākrama    illumination    the deity manifest with its pure conscious nature
```

The goddess of the Krama is precisely this movement. She is the power that emanates the
world, sustains it, and draws it back — and that, in the *anākhyā*, the "inexplicable,"
contains the whole cycle in a unity that is no longer a phase among phases, and in the
*bhāsā*, the illumination, shines as the pure consciousness beyond all phases.

Read against this frame, the crux at 1.8 resolves into the school's own architecture.
*Paramānande* names the goddess in the register of emanation — the overflowing bliss of the
arising world. *Nirānande* names her in the register of the *anākhyā* — the stillness
beyond, the bliss that is no longer the flux of bliss and its loss, the "bliss-at-rest" that
is the ineffable unity of the cycle. The pair is the Krama's own *sṛṣṭi* and *anākhyā*
transposed into the language of joy. The goddess whom the hymn hails as both supreme-bliss
and bliss-at-rest is the goddess whose very nature is the cycle that turns from bliss into
dissolution and back — without being caught at either pole.

---

## VI. The hymn's significance

The *Kramasadbhāva* 1.8, read whole, is a compressed statement of the Krama's theology. It
names the goddess as the mistress of the source of the gods (devadeveśī), as the
all-devouring power of consciousness (mahākālī), and as the one who is both the bliss of
manifestation and the stillness beyond it (paramānande, nirānande). In four vocatives it
accomplishes what the school's whole liturgy elaborates: the identification of the supreme
reality with a consciousness that is at once dynamic (it arises, it devours, it owns) and
nondual (it transcends every pair of opposites).

The verse is also, in its small way, a theodicy of the devotional register. The hymn does
not flinch from the goddess's darker names — the all-devouring, the black one, the
bliss-less. It does not sanitize her. It takes the terrifying and the blissful, the
pleasure and the pain, and says of them what it says of eternal and non-eternal, being and
non-being: she is both, and beyond both. The devotional claim is that the same power that
consumes the world is the power that is its supreme joy — and that both are the one
goddess, at once the overflowing bliss of the world and the still joy beyond it.

---

## VII. Open questions

1. **The sense of nirānande.** Is it the privative "bliss-less," the technical "bliss at
   rest," or both? The hymn's pair-structure and the Krama's anākhyā-frame favor the
   technical reading; the morphology favors the privative. A specialist verdict on early
   Kālīkrama usage is the decisive next step.

2. **The dating of the technical sense.** Is "stillness" as a technical term attested in the
   Kramasadbhāva itself (the school's scripture), or only in the later exegesis (the
   Mahānayaprakāśa) and related Kubjikā material? The earlier the attestation, the stronger
   the case.

3. **Genre and register.** Is a *stuti*, a devotional hymn, the right place for the
   philosophical reading — or does the praise-register pull toward the simpler devotional
   privative? (The Krama's stuti is itself doctrinal, but the question deserves care.)
