# C1 — Jñānakārikā (commentary)

*A commentary on the Jñānakārikā, paṭalas 2–3, the Kaula work transmitted in the lineage of
Matsyendra (Macchindranātha), the yuganātha who is the teacher of this age in the Kālīkrama
and, with Gorakṣa, the founder of haṭha-yoga. The commentary follows the text's argument as
it develops — from the discrimination of merit and demerit through the doctrine of the
fourth mind to the conduct that is the Kaula path — in reasoned prose. The audited
translation is toggled on beneath. Proposal, for review.*

---

## The knowledge and its rarity (2/1)

The knowledge to be declared is rare in the three worlds, and it is born of the
Siddha-nātha's lineage. The rarity is not a boast but a location. This knowledge is not to
be found by reasoning or by
scripture-read as doctrine; it descends through a line of perfected teachers, and its source
is the lineage itself — the siddha-order that transmits it from body to body. The text is
from the first an insider's text, addressed to those already within the line, and the whole
of its difficulty — the deliberate strangeness of its conduct-teaching — is the shape of
that exclusivity: what is most secret is most precisely reserved.

## The body and its boundaries (2/2–3)

The first teaching is the map of the body and its transcendence. The yogi goes free of the
moon and the sun, free of the doors and the eyes, beyond earth, water, wind, and ether. The
list is a progressive stripping: the two lights (moon and sun, the two channels), the
gates of perception, and finally the elements themselves are set aside in turn. Yet the
verse immediately checks the ascent. The body is said to be built of the five elements,
in the Lord of principles, and the tranquil one who is the pervader of all has no
pervasiveness of his own. The crux is genuine and the text does not hide it: how can the
unbounded pervader be free of the bounded body, if the body is what he pervades? The
passage refuses the easy dualism — a transcendent self that merely inhabits a body — as
much as it refuses materialism. The answer, which the paṭala unfolds rather than states, is
that the body is not the self's prison but its own most contracted form, and that the
pervader is not *in* the body as in a vessel but is the body's reality, as ghee is in milk.

## Ghee in milk (2/4)

The image is decisive and deserves its weight. Standing in pervasiveness directly — as ghee
is in milk — the thing taught by the principles is the knowledge-jewel, very hard to
obtain. Ghee is not in milk as a separate thing that could be lifted out whole; it is
latent, present, requiring only the right operation to be drawn forth. So the self is not
added to the world; it is the world's own essence, present in it as sweetness is present in
sugar, and the "obtaining" of it is a drawing-out, not a discovery of something absent.
The knowledge is hard to obtain not because it is far but because it is so near that the
ordinary motion of the mind, which reaches outward, cannot grasp it. What must be done is
not to go anywhere but to turn — to operate on what is already there.

## The fourth mind (2/6–11)

The paṭala's central doctrine is the analysis of mind into its kinds, and the fourth is its
goal. Three minds are named and dismissed in turn: the tamasic, restless and low; the
rajasic, coming and going; the sāttvika, joined with the dharma, ever standing. Each is a
form of the mind's activity — agitation, craving, merit — and each is still mind, still the
grasping that binds. Then comes the fourth, declared specifically: the mind free of the
three guṇas, ever the mark of nirvāṇa. It is the best of the best, purified, beyond the
restraint of the fires, beyond the senses, imperishable.

The subtlety of the passage is in what the fourth mind is *not*. It is not the mere absence
of the other three — not a blank where agitation, craving, and merit have been stopped. The
text is exact: the principle is always without practice, free of all thought, freed even
from the mind's non-being. The last clause is the key. To be free even of the mind's
*non-being* is to be neither the mind nor its suppression — not the stilled state of the
yogin who has conquered thought, but the state that is prior to the whole opposition of
thought and its absence. The word "even" is the crux: the principle is freed from the
mind's presence *and* from its absence. The fourth mind is not a fourth kind of thinking
but the ground in which the three kinds of mind appear and dissolve — the consciousness
that the three are forms of, reached not by adding a state but by recognizing what was
never absent.

## The sword and the two bonds (2/19–32)

The second paṭala closes by naming what binds and what cuts it. The two bonds are merit and
demerit, dharma and adharma — not sin alone, and notably not ignorance as a lack, but the
whole economy of good and bad action in which the bound one lives. By merit one enjoys
heaven, by demerit the states of hell; and the two together are the binding of the three
worlds. The bound one does not escape by accumulating merit, for merit is one half of the
bond; the discrimination of merit and demerit is itself the first step of knowledge, and
the cutting of both is release. The sword of knowledge cuts the binding of the two bonds.

The image of the potter's wheel makes the point about the false path. Those joined with
the fabrication-knowledge wander like the potter's wheel — turning, productive, never still
— and their spinning is exactly what keeps them from the full. The "siddha-order"
(siddhakrama) of which the text says they are devoid is the graded path of the perfected
teachers — the word is generic here, not a sectarian marker — and the discrimination must
not be collapsed into a school-name. The text's own word is enough: to be devoid of the
siddha-order is to have no line of transmission, and the line is what carries the
knowledge the wheel cannot reach.

## The Kaulika liṅga (3/7–9)

The third paṭala turns from knowledge to conduct (caryā), and its first act is to redefine
the very seat of worship. The liṅga to be taught is the Kaulika: the eightfold
dissolution-worship, the liṅga that is the moving-and-unmoving all. It is not born of
stone, gold, or silver; it is the body-born liṅga, one only, no second. The radicalism is
deliberate and structural, not merely iconoclastic. The Kaula does not reject the liṅga —
it relocates it. The sacred emblem of the tradition is not an image to be approached but
the practitioner's own body, and the "worship" of it is the dissolution of the world into
the body and of the body into awareness. The single liṅga, the body, replaces the stone and
the gold because it is what the stone and gold only point toward: the one reality that
dissolution leaves.

## The burning-ground and the inverted tree (3/10–17)

The relocation of the sacred is carried to its most provocative form. The burning-ground
(śmaśāna) where the sādhaka is to sit is the body itself — the body that shines for all the
embodied, joined with breath and out-breath, is proclaimed the burning-ground. The practice
is not an escape to a cremation-ground but the recognition that one already sits there: the
body is the place where everything is consumed into awareness, and the yogi who realizes
this is already at the burning-ground wherever he stands. The empty house, the cave, the
tree-root, the confluence — the stations the sādhaka seeks are not places but the body's
own conditions.

The tree-root carries the image furthest. The body is called the tree, with the feet as
roots and the hands as branches; the mouth is the upward-root, the root is the tree's
holder. The Upaniṣadic ūrdhvamūla — the inverted tree with its root above — is transposed
into the body: the root of the tree is the mouth, the source from which the whole form
hangs. To know the tree-root is to be free of the outer tree — free of the merely physical
body as the tree's true ground, the mouth, is seen. The four powers that guard this root —
Jayā, Vijayā, Ajitā, Aparājitā — are the śakti-quartet born of the four Mothers, the
unconquered powers of the breath and the sense at the root of the body-tree.

## The tide and the four (3/18–20, 72–78)

The breath-theology is given in the image of the tide. The yogi stands at the shore; the
breath moves as the current, coming and going; and the tide — the still fullness at the
shore's edge — is the state in which the coming and going no longer stains. The movement of
the breath, upward and downward (the Gaṅgā rising, settling below), is the world's own
tide, and the yogi who watches it without being caught in it stands as the shore stands to
the sea. The four — the bindu as the supreme power, standing at the shore, of lovely form —
are the powers of that standing, the points at which the tide turns.

The three-and-five are named at the close: sattva, rajas, tamas, and the five elements, as
they stand in unity. The "three-and-five" are the guṇas and the elements held together —
the whole of the world's texture, mind and matter, seen as one standing. The tide does not
abolish the sea; it is the sea's own fullness at rest. The yogi's stillness is not the
absence of the world but the world's own tide recognized as still.

## The naked one and the wine (3/79–85)

The conduct-teaching reaches its most deliberate reversal. The naked one, the loosened-hair,
the drinker of wine — the figures of the Kaula caryā that scandalize the orthodox — are
redefined from within. The hair is called the bond, the hinderer of the liberation-path;
the one freed from the six (the six foes) is the loosened-hair. Nakedness is not the
casting off of clothes but the casting off of the six inner enemies, the passions that are
the true clothing of the bound self. The wine is the internal kula-nectar; the one drunk
with it is the great yogi, the intoxicated one whose "drunkenness" is the absorption of the
mind in its own source. The garland-wearer carries the cast-off offering-garland (nirmālya),
the discarded garland of the ritual — the emblem of what has been offered and released.

The Kaula reversal is not the flouting of a rule for its own sake. It is the transposition
of the entire vocabulary of purity and impurity, sacred and profane, into the inner register
of consciousness. The same letters, the same substances, the same acts that bind the
orthodox — because the orthodox take them as external and therefore as needing external
observance — liberate the Kaula, who sees them as states of the mind. The naked one, the
drinker, the wanderer alone: each is a figure of the self stripped of the six enemies,
absorbed in the nectar, moving without the company of a divided mind. The conduct is the
doctrine enacted, and the doctrine is the fourth mind made visible in a way of life.

## The lone one (3/86–87)

The paṭala ends with its most austere figure: the lone one (ekākī), the guest who wanders
and speaks himself as a guest — the one without a companion, the steadfast one, whom the
text calls the treatise, the root-kārikā (mūlakārikā). The lone one is the text's own
self-naming, the root-teaching, the one who carries the knowledge. He
is alone because the fourth mind is alone — prior to the three kinds of mind and to every
companion they bring, the agitation, the craving, the merit. To be without a companion is
to be without a divided mind, to move as the consciousness that the world's forms all rise
in and dissolve into, owning nothing, following nothing, carrying the whole teaching in the
single movement of its own freedom.
