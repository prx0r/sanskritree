## Verse 1 — Thesis

**IAST [T]:**
> tatra kasyacid ekasya vaśe viśvaṃ pravartate /
> iti sādhayituṃ pūrvaṃ pūrvapakṣaṃ pracakṣmahe //

**Literal:**
There, to establish that the world operates under the control of some single [being], we first state the opponent's view.

**Commentary on *kasyacid ekasya*:** "Of some single one" — the text does not name this being yet. The argument proceeds from the general claim (an effect requires a single intelligent cause) to the specific conclusion (that cause is Īśvara/Siva). The *pūrvapakṣa* (opponent's view) is stated first, following the standard śāstra method.

---

## Verse 2 — Opponent's challenge

**IAST [T]:**
> vyavasthitamitasvārthaṃ na tāvad iha laukikam /
> sādhanaṃ tena sarvārthatajjñānāder asiddhitaḥ //

**Literal:**
An ordinary means of proof, whose scope is fixed and limited, is not [sufficient] here — because knowledge of all objects, etc., is not established [through it].

**Philosophical:**
Ordinary means of knowledge (*pramāṇa*) have determinate, limited objects. They cannot establish the existence of a being who knows all objects and controls the entire world. A special argument is needed.

## Verse 8 — The material cause argument

**IAST [T]:**
> upādānaṃ pṛthivyādi yāgadānādi sādhanam /
> sākṣātkartuṃ kṣamante yat sarva eva ca cetanāḥ //

**Literal [T]:**
Material [causes] like earth, etc., and instruments like sacrifice, gift, etc. — all conscious beings are capable of directly perceiving them.

**[R] Reconstruction:**
The opponent (probably a Mīmāṃsaka or naturalist) argues: conscious beings can directly perceive material causes and instruments. They can perform actions using these means. So why is a Lord (Īśvara) needed? Ordinary agents see the materials and perform actions — that's sufficient to explain the world's operation.

---

## Verse 9 — Distinguishing power from its possessor

**IAST [T]:**
> karmaṇaḥ śaktirūpaṃ yad pūrvādipadāspadam /
> mā bhūt pratyakṣatā tasya śaktimad dhy akṣagocaraḥ //

**Literal [T]:**
That which is the power-nature of action, which is the basis of expressions like "prior" etc. — let there be no direct perception of that; for the possessor of power is within the range of the senses.

**Commentary:** This is a crucial distinction. The POWER of action (*karmaṇaḥ śaktirūpa*) — what makes an action effective, the unseen potency (*apūrva* or *adṛṣṭa* that Mīmāṃsā posits) — is not directly perceived. But the possessor of that power (the agent) IS directly perceived. The point: we can know the agent (Īśvara) through inference even if His power is not directly perceived, just as we infer a potter from the pot even though the potter's skill is not directly visible.

## Unnumbered verse 1 — The body analogy (key argument)

**IAST [T]:**
> nanv eka-cetana-adhīnaṃ vivāda-adhyāsitaṃ jagat /
> acetanena anārabdhatvād aroga-sva-śarīravat //

**Literal:**
[The opponent says:] "Surely the world, which is in dispute [as to its cause], is dependent on a single conscious being — because it is not commenced by an unconscious [agent] — like one's own healthy body."

**[R] Argument structure:**
This is a formal inference (anumāna):
- **Pakṣa (subject):** the world (jagat)
- **Sādhya (to be proved):** is dependent on a single conscious being (eka-cetana-adhīna)
- **Hetu (reason):** because it is not begun by an unconscious agent (acetanena anārabdhatvāt)
- **Dṛṣṭānta (example):** like one's own healthy body (aroga-sva-śarīravat)

The body, when healthy, operates as a unified whole under the direction of consciousness. The world similarly exhibits coordinated operation that suggests a single conscious supervisor.

## Verse 29 — Prose: The problem of many agents

**IAST [V] [R]:**
> ekacetanādhīnapravṛttitve tu prabalabahujanasarabhasaprayatnapracālyairupalatarurathādibhirvyabhicāraḥ /
> ārabdhatvādeva caitatsādhyasiddhāvadhikamidamupādānaviśeṣavacanam/

**Literal:**
If [the world] had its activity dependent on a single consciousness, there would be deviation (*vyabhicāra*) [from the inference] in the case of stones, trees, chariots, etc., which are moved by the violent effort of many strong people. And because they are indeed commenced [by many], this is an additional statement about the particularity of the material cause, once the goal is established.

**[R] Philosophical significance:**
Utpaladeva addresses an objection: many things in the world (buildings, chariots, etc.) are clearly produced by MANY agents working together, not by a single conscious being. Devotion (*vyabhicāra*) — also called *anaikāntika* (inconclusive) — is a fallacy where the reason fails to establish the conclusion because there are counterexamples. If the world were produced by many, the inference to a single Lord would fail.

## Verse 47 — Prose: Agency is not limited to direct perception

**IAST [V]:**
> na copādānādisākṣātkāriṇa eva kartṛtvam ity api niyamaḥ /
> upādānam upakaraṇaṃ cāviduṣa evātmano jñānādiṣu kartṛtvāt /

**Literal:**
Nor is there a rule that agency belongs only to one who directly perceives the material cause, etc.—because the self, though ignorant [of its own nature], has agency in cognitions, etc., with respect to material causes and instruments.

**Philosophical:**
Even an ignorant person acts on materials using instruments — you don't need to understand the essence of the material to act on it. Similarly, the Lord can act on the world without being limited by its material nature.

---

## Verse 48 — The three fallacies

**IAST [T]:**
> ataḥ — asiddhatvād viruddhatvād anaikāntyācca varṇitāt /

**Literal:**
Therefore — because of being unproven (*asiddha*), contradictory (*viruddha*), and inconclusive (*anaikāntika*), as described.

**Commentary:** This appears to be listing the three fallacies (hetvābhāsas) that Utpaladeva's inference avoids, or that the opponent's inference commits. The three Nyāya fallacies are:
1. *Asiddha* (unproven): the reason is not established
2. *Viruddha* (contradictory): the reason proves the opposite
3. *Anaikāntika* (inconclusive/deviation): the reason is present without the conclusion

---

## Verse 51 — Effecthood cannot be denied

**IAST [T]:**
> atra brūmo na kāryatvaṃ kṣityādau śakyanihnavam /

**Literal:**
To this we say: the state of being an effect cannot be denied of earth, etc.

**Philosophical:**
The world (earth, etc.) is an effect — it has a beginning, it is composed of parts, it undergoes change. Therefore it requires a cause. And the cause of a coordinated effect must be intelligent. This is the standard Nyāya argument for the existence of God (Īśvara).

## Verse 56 — Prose: The nature of supervision (adhiṣṭhāna) [LONGEST PASSAGE]

**IAST [V] (abridged):**
> adhiṣṭhānaṃ ca tatpravṛttyanuguṇasaṅkalpavadīśvarasannikarṣaḥ, kṣetrājñeneva svaśarīrādau /
> sa ca dravyaiḥ saṃyogalakṣaṇaḥ, tadguṇaistu saṃyuktasamavāyarūpaḥ /
> pravṛttiśca paramāṇūnāṃ parispandalakṣaṇā /
> dharmādharmayostu phalodayānukūlatādṛśadeśakālādisahakārisahitatā /
> tābhyāṃ dharmādharmābhyāmeva phalam, cetanānadhiṣṭhitānāṃ sarveṣāmeteṣāmacaitanyenākiñcitkaratvāt /

**Literal:**
Supervision (*adhiṣṭhāna*) is the proximity of the Lord, who possesses volition (*saṅkalpa*) favorable to that activity — like the field-knower with respect to one's own body, etc. And that [proximity] is of the nature of conjunction (*saṃyoga*) with substances, and of the nature of inherent-relation-through-conjunction (*saṃyuktasamavāya*) with their qualities. The activity of atoms is of the nature of vibration (*parispanda*). For dharma and adharma [the unseen moral forces], it is the state of being accompanied by assisting factors such as time, place, etc., that are favorable to the arising of their result. The result comes from dharma and adharma alone — because all these [entities], when NOT supervised by a conscious being, are incapable of anything due to being unconscious. For an axe not supervised by a conscious carpenter is unable to function, even with a hundred assisting factors of time, place, etc.

## Verse 67 — All conscious beings act when supervised

**IAST [T]:**
> ekenādhiṣṭhitāḥ kāryaṃ kurvate sravacetanāḥ /

**Literal:**
All conscious beings, when supervised by one, perform their function.

**Commentary:** This is the conclusion. All individual conscious agents (including humans, gods, etc.) act effectively only when supervised by the single Lord. Just as the body's parts function when supervised by the self, the world's agents function when supervised by Īśvara.

## Argument Summary

### What Īśvarasiddhi argues:
1. **The world requires a single intelligent cause** (verse 1, unnumbered verse 1) — inferential argument using the body analogy
2. **Ordinary pramāṇas are insufficient** to establish this (verse 2) — a special inference is needed
3. **The power of action is distinct from material causes** (verse 9, unnumbered verse 2) — we infer the agent even without seeing the power
4. **Agency does not require direct perception of materials** (verse 47) — the Lord can act on the world without being limited by it
5. **The world is an effect** (verse 51) — therefore requires a cause
6. **Supervision (adhiṣṭhāna) is the Lord's proximity** that enables all activity (verse 56)
7. **All conscious beings act when supervised by one** (verse 67)

### What it does NOT establish:
- **Numerical identity across subjects** — the text argues for one Lord who supervises all activity, but it does NOT argue that all individual subjects ARE that Lord. The argument is for a cosmic supervisor (like a king or carpenter), not for the identity of all subjects
- **The universalization gap remains** — the text establishes īśvara as the single cause/supervisor of the world, but this is compatible with Nyāya's Īśvara (who is also a single Lord). The specifically Pratyabhijñā claim that all finite subjects are identical with this Lord is not argued here — it's assumed from the framework

### Contribution to Dossier 1:
This text appears to establish the Lordship of a single conscious being over the world through inference. But it does NOT supply the missing premise for universalization (that reflexive manifestation cannot be numerically partitioned). The text's argument for a single supervisor is compatible with Nyāya theism — both traditions accept a Lord who supervises the world. What distinguishes Pratyabhijñā (the identity of finite subjects with the Lord) is not argued here.
