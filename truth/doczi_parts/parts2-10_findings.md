## Chapter 2: Dinergy in the Crafts (Basketry, Pottery, Weaving)
The same dinergic patterns (straight + spiral, golden section proportions) appear in human-made objects. Spider webs, baskets, and pottery all use radial + spiral construction matching φ proportions. Fourteen samples of Northwest Indian hats measured in a museum all approximated φ or Pythagorean triangle ratios. Doczi argues this is evidence the makers *intuitively* used these proportions.

**For rasa**: if humans across cultures and time periods independently converge on φ proportions in craft, this supports the overdetermination principle — the same constraints produce the same structures regardless of cultural context.

## Chapter 3: Dinergy in the Arts of Living (Gestalt, Body Language)
Gestalt psychology: perception organizes into wholes (figures against grounds). Dinergy as the principle underlying gestalt formation — wholes are created by the union of complementary opposites. Body language, dance, and writing systems show dinergic patterns (forward/backward, open/closed, straight/curved).

**For rasa**: the gestalt principle "the whole is greater than the sum of its parts" IS a description of how dinergy operates in perception. The "greater" is the felt quality — the rasa — that emerges from the dinergic union.

## Chapter 4: Timeless Patterns of Sharing (Cross-Cultural Patterns)
The same patterns (spirals, meanders, mandalas, pentagrams) appear across cultures separated by time and geography. Doczi argues these are not coincidences but expressions of universal dinergic pattern-forming processes. The mandala (circle + square + center) is a universal symbol of wholeness.

**For rasa**: cross-cultural pattern convergence is evidence that the constraints producing these patterns are not cultural but structural — they reflect properties of the human perceptual/cognitive apparatus interacting with physical reality. This supports the extended common practice argument (Tymoczko) extended to visual and cross-modal domains.

## Chapter 5: The Anatomy of Sharing (Shells, Bones, Embryos)
Golden section proportions appear in nautilus shells (logarithmic spiral), human skeletons (femur:tibia ratio ≈ φ), and embryonic development. Doczi: "sharing is the nature of nature" — parts sharing proportions create harmonious wholes. The Fibonacci series describes the growth pattern of the nautilus chamber by chamber.

**For rasa**: the nautilus spiral is a natural example of a structure whose growth follows φ at every stage. The "felt rightness" of this shape may be a recognition of this invariant proportion. If the same proportion appears in *our own bodies* (femur:tibia ≈ φ), we are structurally resonant with it.

## Chapter 6: Order and Freedom in Nature (Butterflies, Beetles, Fish)
Organic patterns (butterfly wing proportions, beetle body segments, fish scales) follow φ and Fibonacci ratios. Doczi distinguishes "organic" (living, imperfectly φ) from "inorganic" (crystal, exactly φ) patterns. Organic patterns approximate φ dynamically; inorganic patterns instantiate it statically. Freedom within order = living systems vary around φ; crystals exactly realize φ.

**For rasa**: the distinction between exact φ (crystal) and approximate φ (living) is crucial. The rasa hypothesis predicts that living systems with *dynamic* approximation to φ feel more alive and meaningful than exact instantiation. Perfect symmetry = dead. Near-symmetry = alive.

## Chapter 7: Hellas and Haiku (Greek Temples, Japanese Poetry)
Greek temples use φ proportions in column spacing, height ratios, and pediment dimensions. Japanese haiku (5-7-5 syllables) follows a 5:7 ≈ 0.714 proportion close to 5:8 = 0.625 (Fibonacci neighbors) and 2:3 = 0.666 (musical fifth). Doczi: haiku's power comes from its proportional structure — 5+7+5 = 17, and 17/27 (the ratio of the first 5 verses to the whole) approximates 0.618.

**For rasa**: if Japanese haiku independently converges on φ-like proportions, this is evidence for a cross-cultural, cross-modal harmonic principle. The "aha" moment of a haiku may be the gestalt completion at a φ proportion.
