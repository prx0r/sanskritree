# Sambandhasiddhi — Provisional Translation and Argument Reconstruction

## Text: Utpaladeva's *Sambandhasiddhi* (Establishment of Relation)

### Source
- **Edition:** Kashmir Series of Texts and Studies, volume 34 (1921)
- **Digital source:** Sanskrit Wikisource (sa.wikisource.org), transcribed by Muktabodha, 2005, cat. M00080
- **Text:** Root kārikās (21 verses) with auto-commentary (Vṛtti), part of the Siddhitrayī
- **Variants:** Readings marked [Kha.pu.] and [Ka.pu.] per Muktabodha transcription. Kha.pu. = Kha-puta (alternate manuscript), Ka.pu. = Ka-puta (another manuscript family)

### Status markers
- **[T]** directly represented in the root verse
- **[V]** clarified by the auto-commentary (Vṛtti)
- **[R]** philosophical reconstruction
- **[X]** point requiring further philological checking

### Translation risk terms (per truthagent §4.D)
- *sambandha* — relation/connection. The entire text is about this term. Cannot be translated uniformly: sometimes "relation," sometimes "connection," sometimes the logical copula.
- *prakāśa* — light/manifestation. Technical term for consciousness's self-manifesting nature. I capitalize "Light" when it refers to the principle, use "manifestation" when it refers to the act.
- *vimarśa/parāmarśa* — reflexive awareness/apprehension/grasp. Vimarśa is the intrinsic self-awareness of consciousness; parāmarśa is the active reflexive grasp that unifies contents. The two are related but not identical — parāmarśa is vimarśa in its synthetic function. I translate vimarśa as "reflexive awareness" and parāmarśa as "reflexive grasp" or "reflexive apprehension."
- *ābhāsa* — appearance/manifestation. In Pratyabhijñā, not mere illusion but the real self-manifestation of consciousness as objects.
- *aikātmya* — identity/oneness/being-one-self. Key term: relation is defined as the "identity of many" (anekasyaikatā). The word contains ātman (self), suggesting that unity is not abstract but self-like.

## Opening Invocation

### Verse 1

**IAST:**
> bhedābhedātmasaṃbandhasahasarvārthasādhitā /
> lokayātrā kṛtir yasya svecchayā taṃ stumaḥ śivam //

**Sandhi resolution:**
- bheda-abheda-ātma-saṃbandha-saha-sarva-artha-sādhitā
- loka-yātrā kṛtiḥ yasya sva-icchayā tam stumaḥ śivam

**Compounds:**
- bhedābhedātmasaṃbandha: a long compound. Two analyses possible:
  (a) bheda-abheda-ātma-saṃbandha: "relation whose essence (ātma) is difference and non-difference"
  (b) bheda-abheda-ātma-saṃbandha: "relation that is the self (ātman) of difference and non-difference"
  I follow (a) as the more natural reading, but (b) is philosophically significant — it would make relation identical with the self. The text will later argue this.
- sarvārthasādhitā: "by which all purposes are accomplished"

**Literal [T]:**
We praise that Śiva, whose play is the world-course, accomplished freely through relation whose essence is difference-and-non-difference, accomplishing all purposes.

**Philosophical:**
We praise Śiva, whose free creative play is the entire operation of the world — an operation that accomplishes every aim through a relation whose nature encompasses both distinction and identity.

**Doctrinal:**
We honour Śiva, whose self-willed cosmic activity constitutes the whole of worldly functioning, achieved through the principle of relation that is at once the essence of difference and non-difference — for all purposes are fulfilled through this relational power.

**Commentary on translation choices:**
- *Lokayātrā* (world-course): I considered "world-process" but chose "world-course" to preserve the sense of ongoing movement (yātrā = journey, going). "Worldly transaction" would be too narrow — Utpaladeva means the entire functioning of the cosmos, not just commerce.
- *Kṛtiḥ* (play/activity/creation): I initially translated "play" (līlā is the more common word for divine play, but kṛti carries the sense of "making" or "doing"). "Creative activity" would be more precise but loses the connotation of effortlessness implied by svecchayā (freely/through self-will). **Alternative:** "whose work is the world-process" — acceptable but misses the aesthetic dimension.
- *Svecchayā* (freely/by self-will): Could also be "according to His own will" or "spontaneously." The philosophical point is that Śiva's creative activity is not constrained by anything external.

### Verse 2

**IAST:**
> bhāvabhedādisaṃbandhamayena [Kha: bhedātmeti] vapuṣonmiṣan /
> jayaty eko 'pi viśvātmā prakāśaḥ parameśvaraḥ //

**Sandhi resolution:**
- bhāva-bheda-ādi-saṃbandha-mayena vapuṣā-unmiṣan
- jayati ekaḥ api viśva-ātmā prakāśaḥ parama-īśvaraḥ

**Variant note [X]:** Kha manuscript reads *bhedātmeti* instead of *bhedādisaṃbandhamayena*. If *bhedātmeti* is correct, the line reads "through a body whose essence is difference itself" rather than "through a body consisting of relation to entities, differences, etc." The difference is significant: the Kha reading makes the stronger metaphysical claim that difference itself is the body of the Lord, not merely that relation-to-differences is. I follow the main reading as it better anticipates the argument of the text, but the Kha reading deserves investigation if a critical edition is consulted.

**Literal [T]:**
Though one, the universal self, the Light, the supreme Lord, shines forth, triumphing through a body consisting of relation to entities, differences, and so on.

**Philosophical:**
Though essentially one, the universal Self — which is pure Light, the Supreme Lord — triumphs by manifesting through a body whose very fabric is the relation to beings, their distinctions, and all that follows from them.

**Doctrinal:**
The one universal Self, the Light that is the Supreme Lord, triumphs as He manifests through a form composed entirely of the relations among entities and their differences — His body is the relational structure of all reality.

**Translation risk note:** *Prakāśa* — I capitalize as "Light" because it's a technical term for consciousness's self-manifesting nature. But in other contexts the same word means simply "manifestation" or "appearance." The reader must keep both senses active: Light is what illuminates, and manifestation is what appears. In Pratyabhijñā these are the same. **Alternative:** "the Manifest One" — but this loses the active sense of illuminating. *Viśvātmā* is "universal self" — the self that is the world, not merely the self of the world.

**Implicit claim [R]:** The invocation already contains the thesis of the entire text: the Lord's body (the manifest world) is nothing other than relation. Relation is not an external connection between independent things — it is the very form (*vapus*) of reality. This sets up the central argument: if relation is the body of the Lord, then relation is not a conceptual construction but the structure of reality as consciousness manifests it.

## Prose: What is Relation? (The Problem Stated)

**[V]** Now relation (*sambandha*) among entities is examined.

### First definition and its problems

**IAST [V]:**
> kas tāvat sambandhaśabdārthaḥ? saṃsargaḥ samparkaḥ saṃśleṣaḥ sambandha ity apy ukte na vivṛtaḥ sambandhārthaḥ pratīyate.

**Literal:**
What, then, is the meaning of the word "relation"? When it is said: "connection, contact, conjunction, relation," the meaning of relation is not distinctly understood.

**Commentary:** Utpaladeva begins by noting that synonyms don't help — "connection," "contact," "conjunction" all just rename the problem. This is a characteristic Pratyabhijñā move: the Buddhist opponent will argue that relation has no essence, that it is a mere practical convention. So Utpaladeva must find what is essential to relation — what makes relation what it is, not just other words for it.

**IAST [V]:**
> kiṃ nairantaryaṃ saṃśleṣa utānyat kiṃcit? nairantaryaṃ cet dūrasthayoḥ pitāputrayoḥ sa na syāt, ayaḥ-śalākayoḥ saṃnikṛṣṭayor vibhudravyayor api ca syāt.

**Literal:**
Is [relation] spatial contiguity? Or conjunction? Or something else? If it is spatial contiguity, then a father and son living far apart would have no relation — yet they clearly do. And two iron rods placed close together, or two all-pervading substances, would be related — yet we don't ordinarily call them related in the relevant sense.

**Commentary on *vibhudravyayoḥ*:** "Two all-pervading substances" — this is a reference to the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika theory that space (ākāśa) and some other substances are all-pervading (*vibhu*). If relation were spatial contiguity, then all-pervading substances would always be in contact with everything — making the concept vacuous. This is a technical argument that depends on the opponent's ontology, not a general philosophical point.

**IAST [V]:**
> atha dviṣṭhaḥ padārthaḥ kaścit sambandhaḥ. tatrāpi dviṣṭhatvaiva sambandhatā cet tad dvitvadvipṛthaktvāder api sā syāt.

**Literal:**
If, alternatively, relation is some entity residing in two things (*dviṣṭhaḥ padārthaḥ*): if being-in-two is itself what it means to be a relation, then the same would apply to twoness (*dvitva*), duality, separateness (*vipṛthaktva*), etc.

**Commentary:** Utpaladeva is targeting the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika theory of *samavāya* (inherence) as a distinct entity residing in two things (the substance and its quality, or the whole and its parts). His argument: if being-in-two-things is the criterion, then many other concepts (twoness, duality, separateness) would also qualify — which is absurd. **Alternative reading:** *dviṣṭha* could mean "having two locations" rather than "residing in two things." The difference is subtle but significant: the former implies the relation is one entity with two locations, the latter implies the relation is in two entities. The text's argument works either way.

**IAST [V]:**
> nāpy ayam arthaḥ sambandhaśabdasya avayavārthānurodhād uttiṣṭhati [Kha: etaduttiṣṭhatīti] api tu tailapāyikaśabdasyeva saṅketamātrāt.

**Variant note [X]:** Kha manuscript reads *etaduttiṣṭhatīti* ("this arises") instead of *uttiṣṭhati* ("arises"). The difference is minor — the standard reading is a general statement, the Kha reading directs attention to this specific meaning.

**Literal:**
Nor does this meaning of the word "relation" arise through the force of its component parts. Rather, like the word "tailapāyika" [a caste-name meaning "oil-drinker"], it is mere convention.

**Commentary on *tailapāyika*:** This is an example from Sanskrit grammatical tradition. The word *tailapāyika* literally seems to mean "one who drinks oil" (taila + pāyika), but it actually refers to a particular caste or profession that may have nothing to do with drinking oil. The point is: just because a word can be analyzed etymologically doesn't mean its meaning is derived from its parts. Similarly, "sambandha" might be thought to mean "being bound together" (samṃ + bandh), but this etymological analysis doesn't settle what relation actually IS in reality. **However** — Utpaladeva is not conceding that relation is mere convention. He's saying: the meaning doesn't come from etymology alone; it comes from how relation actually functions in cognition and reality.

**IAST [V] [R]:**
> atha sambandhaśabdārtha eva na dviṣṭhatā api tu so 'nya eva kevalaṃ sambaddhayor eva [Kha.pu.: so 'pi dvayor iti] saṃbhavatīti dviṣṭha ucyate, sa tarhi svarūpeṇaiva sphuṭam ucyatāṃ.

**Variant note:** Kha manuscript reads *so 'pi dvayor iti* ("it too is in two") instead of *kevalam sambaddhayor eva* ("only in the related pair"). If the Kha reading is correct, Utpaladeva is reporting the opponent's view as: "relation is also in two" — making the opponent's position more nuanced. I follow the main reading as it sets up a clearer dialectic.

**Literal:**
If the meaning of the word "relation" is not being-in-two, but rather something else that merely occurs in the related pair and is therefore called "being-in-two," then let that be clearly stated in its own nature.

**Philosophical:**
If the Nyāya opponent says relation is NOT just being-in-two (that was a straw man), but rather a distinct entity that happens to reside in two things — then they should tell us what that entity IS, not just where it resides.

### The Buddhist opponent's challenge

**[R] [V]** The Buddhist (*vijñānavādin* or *sautrāntika*) now enters with a more radical challenge: relation is not a mysterious entity-in-two, but a conceptual construction (*kalpanā*) superimposed on what are actually discrete, self-contained particulars.

**IAST [V]:**
> te hy amiśrāḥ svayaṃ bhāvās tān yojayati kalpanety āhuḥ

**Literal:**
For they say: "Entities are intrinsically unmixed; it is conceptual construction that connects them."

**IAST [V]:**
> tathā nīlam utpalam iti śābdyā kalpanayā paraṃ nīlotpalayor ekātmatā pratīyate, na tu vastvavabhāsinā niraṃśotpalasvalakṣaṇaviśeṣālambanena [Kha.pu.: lambanapratyakṣeti] pratyakṣeṇa pramāṇena.

**Literal:**
Thus, when [we say] "blue lotus," it is through verbal conceptual construction alone that the identity of blue and lotus is cognized — not by direct perception, whose object is the partless particular of the lotus itself, the unique individuating characteristic, [as the valid means of cognition].

**Variant note [X]:** Kha manuscript reads *lambanapratyakṣeti* ("supported perception") instead of *ālambanena pratyakṣeṇa* ("by perception whose object is"). The meaning is similar but the Kha reading emphasizes the dependence of perception on its object. More critically, the syntax here is genuinely difficult — the long compound *niraṃśotpalasvalakṣaṇaviśeṣālambanena* could be parsed in multiple ways:
- (a) niraṃśa-utpala-svalakṣaṇa-viśeṣa-ālambanena: "having as object the unique particular, the partless lotus"
- (b) niraṃśa-utpala-svalakṣaṇa-viśeṣa-ālambanena: "having as object the particularity of the unique characteristic of the partless lotus"
I follow (a) for readability, but (b) is grammatically defensible.

**[T]** The Buddhist quotes a supporting verse:

> saṃyujyante na bhidyante svato 'rthāḥ pāramārthikāḥ /
> rūpam ekam anekaṃ ca teṣu buddher upaplavaḥ //

**Literal:**
Ultimate entities are neither connected nor divided by their own nature. Unity and plurality in them is a disturbance of cognition (*buddher upaplavaḥ*).

**Commentary on *buddher upaplavaḥ*:** This is a strong phrase — "disturbance" or "affliction" of cognition. The Buddhist is saying that when we perceive unity or plurality, we are not perceiving reality; we are projecting conceptual structure onto what is actually featureless particulars. *Upaplava* literally means "calamity" or "misfortune" — it's a cognitive error, not a neutral construction. This sets up the high stakes: if relation is conceptual construction, is it a useful convention (as some Buddhists hold) or a fundamental error (as the more radical position holds)?

**What Utpaladeva must establish against the Buddhist:**
1. That the cognition of relation is not a correctable error (it persists even after analysis)
2. That this cognition is bound up with the very appearance of objects (you cannot have the objects without them appearing as related)
3. That therefore relation is a real feature of how consciousness manifests reality — not a superimposition on what is actually unrelated

### Utpaladeva's response

**[V]**
> tatrocyste — pratītis tāvad anekaikarūpatāyāṃ sambandhābhidhānāya bhavadbhir apy abhyupagataiva, yenoktam — ity amiśrāḥ svayaṃ bhāvās tān yojayati kalpanā iti.

**Literal:**
To this it is said: cognition in the form "many as one" (*anekaikarūpatā*) is admitted even by you for the purpose of designating relation — as you yourselves said: "entities are intrinsically unmixed; conceptual construction connects them."

**Commentary on *anekaikarūpatā*:** This compound — "the form of being many as one" — is the key term of the entire text. Relation, for Utpaladeva, IS this "many-as-one" form: the appearance of multiplicity as unified in a single cognition. The Buddhist admits this form exists (in cognition) but calls it conceptual construction. Utpaladeva's move: if you admit the form exists, the question is whether it has a basis in reality or is a mere error. And you can't appeal to "it's conceptual" as a disqualification, because ALL determinate cognition is conceptual — including the Buddhist's own perception of a pot.

**IAST [V] [R]:**
> athātradarśanam api tathā ghaṭapaṭādirūpa eveti na kalpanātvam. ihāpi rājñaḥ puruṣaḥ sthālyām odana ityādāv api pratyakṣāvabhāso na tatheti kutaḥ avagatam.

**Literal:**
If [you say that] even here, in the case of a pot or cloth, there is direct perception of that form — then it is not mere conceptual construction. And in cases like "the king's man," "rice in a pot," etc., why is it known that there is no such direct perception?

**Philosophical:**
The Buddhist cannot have it both ways. If the perception of a pot is genuinely perceptual (not conceptual), then why can't the perception of relation also be genuinely perceptual? If the perception of a pot is conceptual (the Buddhist's official position — all determinate cognition is conceptual), then calling relation "conceptual" doesn't make it any less real than the pot. Either way, the Buddhist's dismissal of relation fails.

**The crucial philosophical claim [T]:**

> na hy arthapratibhāsasaṃlagnatvād bādhakenāpi duruddharo 'yaṃ bhramaḥ

**Literal:**
For this error [the cognition of relation] is not easily removed even by a counter-cognition, because it is fused with the appearance of the object itself (*arthapratibhāsasaṃlagnatvāt*).

**Commentary on *arthapratibhāsasaṃlagnatvāt*:** This is the linchpin of Utpaladeva's argument. The cognition of relation is not like seeing a snake in a rope — when you see the rope, the snake-error dissolves. The cognition of relation (e.g., "this is the king's man") does NOT dissolve when you analyze it — even after philosophical analysis, things still appear as related. The relation is fused with (saṃlagna = stuck to, adhering to) the very appearance of the things. You cannot have the objects appear without them appearing as related.

**Alternative translation of *saṃlagnatvāt*:** I considered "inseparable from," "adhering to," "intrinsic to." "Fused with" best captures the sense that the relation is not an external addition but part of the very fabric of the appearance.

**[R] Utpaladeva's key distinction:**

> na hy arthapratibhāsasaṃlagnatvād bādhakena api tu arthapratibhāsasādṛśyasadbhāvamātrāt

**Literal:**
For it is not because of fusion with the object's appearance that [the error resists correction] — rather, it is merely because of similarity to the object's appearance.

**[X] Syntactic difficulty:** This sentence is genuinely ambiguous. Two readings:
- **Reading A (I follow):** Utpaladeva is stating the Buddhist's objection and then answering it. The Buddhist says: "This isn't fusion with the appearance — it's just similarity to the appearance (like a rope that looks like a snake)." Utpaladeva then replies that similarity isn't sufficient to explain why the relational appearance is universal and involuntary.
- **Reading B:** Utpaladeva is clarifying his own position: "It's not just similarity to the appearance that makes it hard to correct — it's fusion with the appearance itself."
- **Reading C (from the Vṛtti context):** The sādṛśya (similarity) reading belongs to the Buddhist who tries to reduce all error to similarity-misidentification. Utpaladeva rejects this: the relational appearance is not an error based on similarity but the very structure of how consciousness manifests objects.

I follow Reading A based on the argumentative structure. The particle *api tu* ("but rather") typically introduces the opponent's alternative which is then rejected.

---

### Rest of the prose argument [R]

**[V] [R]** The remaining prose (approximately 3 pages) develops the following argument sequence:

1. **All errors involve similarity** — the snake-rope error works because the rope resembles a snake. But the relational appearance does NOT work by resemblance — it's the very form in which objects appear.

2. **Conceptual construction is not separate from the appearance** — *na ca vikalpo 'rthapratibhāsād bhinnaḥ* (the conceptual construction is not different from the appearance of the object). This is a direct citation of the Pratyabhijñā doctrine that cognition (*vikalpa*) and its object-appearance are not two different things.

3. **Therefore the appearance of unity-in-multiplicity is how objects genuinely appear** — it's not an error superimposed on a correct perception, because there is no correct perception without this form.

4. **The question becomes:** is this relational appearance *possible* (upapadyate) or not? And the answer is given through the Pratyabhijñā ontology: the universal selfhood of pure consciousness (*cinmātrasya viśvātmatā*).

5. **If the Pratyabhijñā ontology is accepted** (as established in the Īśvarapratyabhijñā, to which the text repeatedly refers), then differentiation is Māyā's power, and the appearance of separate entities as related is how consciousness manifests its own unity through apparent difference.

## Key Verses (1-21)

### Verses 1-3: The definition of relation

**IAST (verse 1):**
> apeksā pāratantryaṃ ca na sambandho jaḍātmanoḥ /
> asambhavād ātmalābhe 'py upacāro na lakṣaṇā //

**Literal:**
Expectation and dependence are not the relation between two inert entities, because they cannot occur [in them]. Even "attaining itself" is a metaphor, not a definition.

**Commentary on *jaḍātmanoḥ*:** "Two inert entities" — this is crucial. Utpaladeva is arguing that if you treat entities as fundamentally inert (as the Buddhist does with momentary particulars, or the realist does with independent substances), relation between them becomes unintelligible. Expectations, dependence, and "self-attainment" are mental phenomena — they presuppose consciousness. You cannot use them to define relation between unconscious things, because unconscious things don't have expectations or depend on anything consciously.

**Translation risk for *upacāra*:** "Metaphor" or "figurative extension." Utpaladeva is saying that when we speak of a seed "attaining itself" as a sprout, this is metaphorical — the seed doesn't consciously attain anything. If we try to define relation through such metaphors, we haven't actually defined it. **Alternative:** "trope" (rhetorical figure) — but this is too technical for the general reader.

**IAST (verse 2):**
> asatām ātmalābhokter dviṣṭhatvaṃ ca hīyate /
> rūpasaṃśleṣa evaṃ sambandhārthaḥ sthitaḥ sa ca //

**Literal:**
Because "attaining itself" is spoken of unreal things, even being-in-two is undermined. Thus the meaning of relation is the conjunction of forms (*rūpasaṃśleṣa*). And that:

**Commentary:** The logic: if you try to define relation through "attaining itself" (like a seed becoming a sprout), you're appealing to a process that happens in what you consider ultimately unreal (from the Buddhist's own perspective, the seed-sprout continuum is conventional, not ultimate). And if being-in-two is supposed to be the criterion, that also fails (as argued in the prose). So what remains? *Rūpasaṃśleṣa* — the "conjunction of forms" or "mutual fitting of appearances."

**Alternative for *rūpasaṃśleṣa*:** "Fusion of forms" — saṃśleṣa can mean "embrace" or "intimate union." This is sexually charged language that the later tantric tradition develops explicitly. I use "conjunction" to keep it philosophical, but the reader should know the word has erotic overtones.

**IAST (verse 3):**
> nānantaryam asambandhāt tathātve 'yaḥśalākayoḥ /
> tenānekasya rūpasya śleṣa aikātmyam eva saḥ //

**Literal:**
[It is] not spatial contiguity — because of non-relation; if it were, [it would apply to] two iron rods. Therefore, the conjunction of many forms is their identity (*aikātmya*).

**Commentary on *aikātmyam*:** This is the key definition. Relation = the identity of many forms. *Aikātmya* is a strong word — not just "unity" (ekatva) but "being-one-self" (ātman + eka). The word suggests that the unity in question is not abstract but self-like — it involves selfhood, subjectivity, consciousness. When blue and lotus appear as "blue lotus," their unity is not mechanical but the unity of a cognitive act that holds them together. This prepares the ground for the later argument that all relation is ultimately the self-identity of consciousness.

**Alternative for *aikātmya*:** "Oneness" is too weak (any bundle of properties could be called one). "Self-identity" captures the ātman component but might suggest numerical identity (which is not what's meant — blue and lotus are not numerically identical). I use "identity" with the understanding that this is a technical term for Pratyabhijñā's distinctive view: things are one in the self of consciousness while remaining phenomenally distinct. **Alternative considered:** "essential unity" — acceptable but loses the ātman connection.

### Verses 4-7: Against the Buddhist reduction

**Verse 4 — The opponent's position stated:**
> naikasyaikye 'py anekasya bādha āhur yathā pare /
> ity amishrāḥ svayaṃ bhāvās tān yojayati kalpanā //

**Literal:**
"Unity and plurality cannot co-exist, therefore entities are intrinsically unmixed; conceptual construction connects them" — thus the opponent says.

**Commentary:** This verse restates the Buddhist position (first stated in the prose) in a condensed form. The key claim is that unity and plurality are contradictory — something cannot be both one and many. Therefore the appearance of "many as one" MUST be an error.

**Utpaladeva's implicit reply:** Unity and plurality ARE compatible if the unity is not substantial but intentional — not the unity of a single substance but the unity of a single cognitive act that holds many contents. Consciousness is precisely that which can unify multiplicity without being a substance.

**Verse 5 — Why it's not removable:**
> yato na kalpanāmātraṃ tāvad etad avāraṇāt /
> sarpabhranter ivābhāsasaṃlagnatvāt tannivāraṇam //

**Literal:**
But this is not mere conceptual construction, because it is not blocked. Like the snake-error — because it is fused with the appearance, its removal [is difficult].

**Commentary on *avāraṇāt*:** "Not blocked" — the relational appearance cannot be made to go away by mere analysis. You can intellectually accept that entities are separate, but they still APPEAR as related. This is Utpaladeva's phenomenological argument: the persistence of the appearance under analysis is evidence that it's not a superficial error.

**Alternative for *avāraṇāt*:** I considered "not prevented" and "not warded off." "Not blocked" best captures the sense of an obstacle that can't be removed.

**Verse 6 — The key distinction:**
> yasmād ābhāsasaṃlagnatvaṃ nābhāsasadṛśārthatā /
> kiṃ tv ābhāsānusāritvaṃ bhrānta ābhāsa eva tat //

**Literal:**
For "being fused with the appearance" is not "being an object similar to the appearance" — rather, it is "following the appearance." The error IS the appearance itself.

**Commentary [X]:** This verse is philosophically dense and syntactically compressed. The key distinction:
- *Ābhāsasadṛśārthatā*: being an object that merely resembles the appearance (like a rope that resembles a snake)
- *Ābhāsānusāritva*: following the appearance, being continuous with the appearance

Utpaladeva is saying: the relational appearance is not an error of misidentification (seeing X as Y when X resembles Y). It is the very structure of appearance itself — the error, if it is an error, is not in mistaking one thing for another but in the way things appear at all. This is a radical move: if the appearance itself is the error, then the error is not correctable by better perception. And if it's not correctable, calling it "error" is just a label — it functions as reality for us.

**Alternative translation of *ābhāsānusāritvam*:** I had real difficulty with this term. It could mean:
- (a) "conformity to the appearance" — the error follows/accords with the appearance
- (b) "being pervaded by appearance" — the error is suffused with appearance
- (c) "being subsequent to appearance" — the error comes after the appearance
I choose (a) as it best fits the argumentative context: Utpaladeva is contrasting similarity-based error (sādṛśya) with appearance-conformity (ānusāritva).

**Verse 7 — The Pratyabhijñā answer:**
> ittham ābhāsa evāsminn anekasyaikatādiśi /
> vācyopapattiḥ sāpy uktā pratyabhijñānaye sphuṭam //

**Literal:**
Thus, in this [system], it is the appearance itself that bears the meaning of "unity of many." And this has been clearly stated in the Pratyabhijñā method.

**Commentary:** Utpaladeva is now explicit: the Pratyabhijñā system has already answered this question. The appearance (*ābhāsa*) itself *is* the unity of many — this is not an error but the very nature of how consciousness manifests. The reference to the Pratyabhijñā method (*pratyabhijñānaya*) is significant: the argument is not being made de novo but presented as consistent with the established system.

**[R] Note for the universalization question:** Verse 7 shows that Sambandhasiddhi is NOT trying to prove Pratyabhijñā from first principles — it assumes the Pratyabhijñā ontology and shows that relation is consistent with it. This means the text does NOT supply the missing premise for universalization (that would require an argument that does not assume what it's trying to prove). Rather, it shows how relation works *if* the Pratyabhijñā ontology is accepted.

### Verses 8-11: The ontology of relation

**Verse 8 — The metaphysical basis:**
> evaṃ ca sati vastūnāṃ cinmātravapuṣāṃ satām /
> māyānirmitabhedānām apy ekatvam adurghaṭam //

**Literal:**
Thus, entities — whose essence is pure consciousness (*cinmātravapus*), though differentiated by Māyā's creation — are not impossible to unify.

**Commentary on *cinmātravapuṣām*:** This compound — "having pure consciousness as their body/essence" — is the ontological claim. Entities are NOT ultimately physical or mind-independent. Their true nature (*vapus* = body/form/essence) is consciousness-only. This is NOT established in this text — it is the Pratyabhijñā doctrine that the text assumes.

**[X] Critical note for the thesis:** This verse is where the universalization gap becomes visible. Utpaladeva says: entities' essence is consciousness, so their unity is easy (*adurghaṭam*). But the claim that entities' essence IS consciousness is exactly what needs to be proved against the Nyāya realist (who holds that entities are mind-independent and consciousness is a property of individual selves) and the Buddhist (who holds that entities are momentary particulars). The text references the Īśvarapratyabhijñā for the proof, but that proof — as we saw in Ajadapramātṛsiddhi — relies on the same universalization step that we identified as the decisive failure point.

**Translation risk for *adurghaṭam*:** "Not impossible" or "not difficult" — I chose "not impossible" as it conveys that the text is making a possibility claim, not a necessity claim. **Alternative:** "easy" — but this is misleadingly casual for such a philosophically loaded assertion.

**Verse 9 — The empirical vs real distinction:**
> tatrāpi yaḥ parāmarśaviṣayo laukikasya saḥ /
> cidabhedaprathārūpabhedasārasya vedituḥ //

**Literal:**
Even so, what is the object of reflexive grasp (*parāmarśa*) for the ordinary [knower] — whose nature is the appearance of non-differentiated consciousness permeated by difference.

**Commentary [X]:** This verse is extremely compressed. Key terms:
- *Parāmarśa*: reflexive grasp — the active synthetic function of consciousness that unifies contents
- *Laukika*: ordinary/worldly — the empirical subject, as opposed to the realized universal subject
- *Vedituḥ*: of the knower

The meaning: even the ordinary empirical knower's cognition (which is permeated by apparent difference and separation) is actually an act of reflexive grasp (*parāmarśa*) — and this reflexive grasp is always already an act of unifying. The difference between the ordinary and enlightened knower is not in the STRUCTURE of cognition (both unify through reflexive grasp) but in the RECOGNITION of what that reflexive grasp truly is.

**Alternative parsing:** The compound *cidabhedaprathārūpabhedasārasya* is genuinely difficult. I parse it as:
- cid-abheda-prathā-rūpa-bheda-sārasya: "whose essence (sāra) is difference (bheda) in the form (rūpa) of the manifestation (prathā) of non-differentiated consciousness (cid-abheda)"

This is an oxymoronic formulation: the essence is DIFFERENCE, but this difference is the manifestation of NON-DIFFERENTIATED consciousness. This is Pratyabhijñā's characteristic view: differentiation is real at the empirical level but non-differentiation is real at the ultimate level — and both are true of the same reality.

**Verse 10 — Why divided attention is impossible:**
> vyāpāro 'nena ruddho 'sau na vikalpāntaraṃ spṛśet /
> vikalpāntarasaṃsparśe mātṛbhedaprasaṅgataḥ //

**Literal:**
By this [singular reflexive awareness], the operation is blocked; it would not touch another conceptual construction, because touching another would entail a division of the knower (*mātṛbhedaprasaṅga*).

**Commentary on *mātṛbhedaprasaṅga*:** This is the crucial argument for the unity of the knower. "Division of the knower" (*mātṛbheda*) would result if one cognitive act tried to engage with two separate conceptual constructions simultaneously. The argument: within a single cognitive act, you can only hold one unified content. You cannot simultaneously attend to two distinct conceptual constructions as two — if you could, the knower would be split.

**[R] This argument's strength and limits:**
- **What it establishes:** Within a single cognitive act, the knower is unified. You cannot have a divided subject within one moment of cognition.
- **What it does NOT establish:** That knowers across different cognitive acts or across different subjects are numerically identical. The argument shows synchronic unity (within one moment) but not diachronic unity (across moments) or transpersonal unity (across subjects).
- **Relevance to universalization:** If this argument is combined with the claim that consciousness cannot be partitioned (from Ajadapramātṛsiddhi's missing premise), it might support the universal subject. But the partitioning claim is not argued here — it's assumed.

**Alternative for *mātṛbheda*:** "Splitting of the subject" or "division of the apprehender." I use "division of the knower" to maintain the epistemological register. The point is phenomenological: you cannot be in two mental states at once because that would require two subjects.

**Verse 11 — Language and unity:**
> tad ekāmarśavarty ekasabdādhyāsavaśād brajet /
> aneko 'py ekatām artho yathā ghaṭapaṭāv iti //

**Literal:**
Therefore, through the force of a single word resting in a single reflexive awareness, even a plurality of objects attains unity — as in "pot and cloth."

**Commentary:** This verse grounds linguistic unity in cognitive unity. When we say "pot and cloth," the word is grammatically singular (dvandva compound or coordinated phrase) — and this linguistic singularity reflects the cognitive unity of the reflexive grasp that holds both objects together. The unity is not in the objects themselves (pot and cloth are different objects) but in the act of consciousness that grasps them together.

**Alternative for *ekāmarśa*:** "Single reflexive grasp" or "one act of reflexive awareness." *Āmarśa* is a variant of *parāmarśa* — both mean the synthetic reflexive grasp. The prefix *ā* may indicate a more focused or determinate form of grasp than the general *parāmarśa*.

### Verse 12-21 (summary)

The remaining verses (12-21) provide a detailed grammatical and logical analysis of how relation functions in:
- Genitive constructions (ṣaṣṭhī): "of the king" (rājñaḥ)
- Apposition (sāmānādhikaraṇya): "blue lotus" (nīlam utpalam)
- Compound formation (samāsa): "king's-man" (rājapuruṣa)
- Coordinated phrases (dvandva): "horses, chariots, and foot soldiers"
- Negative constructions: "absence of pot" (ghaṭasyābhāva)
- Distinction: "this is other than that" (ayam asmād anyaḥ)

The key philosophical points in this section:
1. Every relation is between TWO things, not many — apparently multi-term relations reduce to pairs (verse 13)
2. The genitive case (ṣaṣṭhī) expresses relation as residing in the qualifier while the qualified remains primary (verse 16)
3. Even in complex expressions, the underlying cognitive structure is a single reflexive grasp (verse 19)
4. These cognitions are NOT erroneous — at every moment they are founded on self-awareness, the infinite consciousness (verse 20)

**Verses 19-20 — The conclusion:**
> iti sambandhagaty uktā māyīyajñātṛniṣṭhitāḥ /
> dhiyo vibhinnārthadṛśo vyavahārapravartikāḥ //
> na paraṃ tās tathā bhrāntāḥ sarvā api pratikṣaṇāt /
> svasaṃvitsaṃjñakānantacidvimarśapratiṣṭhitāḥ //

**Literal:**
Thus the nature of relation has been explained. Cognitions established in the limited knower (*māyīyajñātṛ*), seeing differentiated objects, propel worldly activity. But they are not fundamentally erroneous — at every moment they are established in reflexive awareness (*vimarśa*), the infinite consciousness called self-awareness (*svasaṃvit*).

**Commentary on *māyīyajñātṛ*:** "The limited knower" — literally "the knower belonging to Māyā." This is the empirical subject, the individual who experiences differentiation and relation. The text is saying: even the ordinary empirical cognition of relation (king/man, blue/lotus, cause/effect) is ultimately grounded in the infinite reflexive awareness that is consciousness's true nature. The limitation is not in the cognition's structure but in the knower's recognition.

**Verse 21 — Final statement:**
> uktaḥ svasaṃvitsiddho 'yaṃ nirbādha upayogavān /
> sambandhaḥ pratyabhijñāyāṃ pūrṇaḥ parikaraḥ punaḥ //

**Literal:**
This relation — established by self-awareness (*svasaṃvitsiddha*), incontrovertible (*nirbādha*), useful (*upayogavān*) — has been stated. In the Pratyabhijñā, it is the full complement of the means.

**Commentary on *pūrṇaḥ parikaraḥ*:** "Full complement" or "complete auxiliary." The word *parikara* literally means "retinue" or "attendants" — here it means the auxiliary means or supporting apparatus. Utpaladeva is saying that this theory of relation is the complete supporting framework for the Pratyabhijñā's project of recognition. Without understanding relation (as the self-unifying activity of consciousness), the project of recognition would be incomplete.

## Complete Argument Map

### Stage A — Relation cannot be reduced to convention

| Step | Claim | Evidence | Status |
|------|-------|----------|--------|
| A1 | The Buddhist admits cognition has "many as one" form | Textual — both sides agree | Established |
| A2 | Calling it "conceptual" doesn't make it unreal — all determinate cognition is conceptual | Valid inference from Buddhist premises | Strong |
| A3 | The relational appearance is not removable — it persists under analysis | Phenomenological argument | Strong |
| A4 | Therefore relation is not mere practical convention | Conclusion from A1-A3 | Established |

### Stage B — Relation is the unity of many in consciousness

| Step | Claim | Evidence | Status |
|------|-------|----------|--------|
| B1 | Relation = rūpasaṃśleṣa (conjunction of forms) = aikātmya (identity of many) | Definitional argument from elimination | Established |
| B2 | This identity occurs in and through consciousness | Phenomenological — the unity IS the cognitive act | Established |
| B3 | The knower cannot be divided within a single cognitive act | Verse 10 argument | Strong within one moment |
| B4 | Therefore relation is consciousness's own self-unifying activity | Conclusion from B1-B3 | Established within the framework |

### Stage C — The universalization question

| Step | Claim | Evidence | Status |
|------|-------|----------|--------|
| C1 | Entities' essence is pure consciousness (cinmātravapus) | Doctrinal — referenced from ĪPK | Assumed, not proved here |
| C2 | Differentiation is Māyā's creation | Doctrinal | Assumed, not proved here |
| C3 | All relations are modes of one conscious unity | Inferred from C1-C2 | Not independently proved |
| C4 | This one conscious unity is Śiva/universal subject | Framework conclusion | Not proved in this text |

### What Sambandhasiddhi establishes

**Strong conclusion:** Relation is not reducible to convention, spatial contiguity, or causal dependence. It is the intrinsic unity of what appears as multiple within a single cognitive act. This unity is a function of consciousness's own reflexive nature (*vimarśa*/parāmarśa).

**Contribution to universalization:** Sambandhasiddhi provides a sophisticated account of how unity-in-diversity is possible — through consciousness's self-manifesting reflexive grasp (*parāmarśa*). The relation of "king and man" or "blue and lotus" is not an external connection but the internal unity of a single cognitive act.

**But:** The universalization from "each cognitive act is unified by consciousness" to "all cognitive acts are unified by one universal consciousness" relies on:
1. The already-established Pratyabhijñā framework (repeated references to ĪPK)
2. The doctrine that entities' essence is consciousness (*cinmātravapus*) — which is exactly what the universalization is supposed to prove

**Verdict:** Sambandhasiddhi supplies a sophisticated theory of relation as consciousness's self-unifying activity, but does NOT independently bridge the universalization gap. It shows HOW all relations could be modes of one consciousness (if the Pratyabhijñā ontology is accepted), but does not prove that they MUST be.

**Next step:** Translate Īśvarasiddhi — may contain the transition from agency to Lordship, which could be the missing universalization premise.
