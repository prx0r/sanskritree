# 10-Text Batch + Spanda Vivṛti — C1 (plain-English interpretations)

*The commentary pass on the openings.*

# 1. Jñānasaṅkalinītantra — v. 7
**What it says:** "The Vedas, the śāstras, the Purāṇas are like common courtesans; but the Śāmbhavī-vidyā is hidden like a kula-bride." The image is precise and shocking: the public scriptures are available to anyone — they are, in the text's blunt simile, common; the vidyā is a bride — hidden, given only once, to one. The secrecy is not prudery; it is the nature of the thing. What can be picked up on a street cannot be the deepest teaching.

# 2. Kulasāra — the soma-myth
**What it says:** The gods lost their power — without the surā, without the strength — and the question is how purity is possible at all. The myth's logic: the orthoprax world (the gods, the rites, the surā-less order) is enfeebled; the kula-mata is the restoration, and the restoration comes through what orthopraxy excludes — the female principle, the forbidden substances, the kula's own order. The myth is the lineage's etiology: the world's weakness is why the kula exists.

# 3. Azeṣakulavallarī — the liturgy
**What it says:** The "all-kula vine" — the complete liturgy of the Mārtaṇḍa-Bhairava: the fire, sun, and moon discs with their kalās; the nine powers; the planets with their bījas; the door-wardens. Plainly: this is the ritual machinery in full — the universe summoned into the ritual space, layer by layer. The liturgy is the ontology in action: the same kalās and powers the philosophy names are the ones the rite invokes.

# 4. Kākacaṇḍeśvarīmata — the goddesses
**What it says:** The text's frame names its goddesses: the Skeleton-Queen, the Night-of-Time, the Crow-Fierce — the Krama's fierce family. And the worship-scene: the yoginī-hosts surrounding Bhairava — some praise him, some dance, some laugh — "by these, devoted through knowledge, Bhairava is worshipped." The Krama's god is not worshipped with rites alone; the devotion through knowledge is the worship.

# 5. Kulacūḍāmaṇi — v. 1
**What it says:** "Countless is the goddess Tripurā; countless also is Kālikā." The goddesses are beyond counting — not one goddess and not a fixed pantheon, but an uncountable family. The text that opens with the countlessness of the goddesses then lists its canon — the sixty-four tantras — the countable scaffolding around the uncountable center.

# 6. Nareśvaraparīkṣā — the examination
**What it says:** Kṣemarāja's "Examination of the Lord of Men": every school's god is examined and found limited — the Sāṅkhya's prakṛti-god is unconscious, the Vedāntin's brahman is non-agent, the Pāñcarātra's Nārāyaṇa is effect-bound — "limited power" is the verdict on all of them. Then the Śaiva's own Lord, the examination's subject: the one who is not limited because he is the freedom itself. The genre is the tradition's own critical method — every rival's claim tested, only the freedom survives the test. The same method our own machine-gate replicates centuries later.

# 7. Kaulāvalīnirṇaya — the canon
**What it says:** The maṅgala names the text's sources — the Yāmalas, the Kulacūḍāmaṇi, the Kulasāra, the Kulārṇava — the canon of the lineage. And the canon is self-referential: the texts it cites are the texts we translate. The lineage's self-image is a library: the Kaulāvalī ("the Kaula-line") stands in the row of the kula's books, each citing the others. The canon-map is the lineage's own geography.

# 8. Nityākaulatantra — the fragment
**What it says:** The fragment preserves the goddess-list before the great consolidation: the Heart-Line, the Moistening, the Delight, the Shaking, the Love-sick, the Sky-Kalā, the Draining, the Swift — and the twelfth, the Madana. Per the scholar who catalogued it, this is the pool from which the sixteen Nityās of the later Śrīvidyā emerged — the fragment shows the goddesses before they were counted, before the great yantra gathered them. The prehistory of the Śrīcakra, in eleven names.

# 9. Spanda Vivṛti — v. 1, what spanda is
**What it says:** The opening verse defines the term the whole school is named for: spanda — the pulse. Not a movement from place to place: the self-experiencing flash of consciousness, "its body the unobstructed light," uncaught by space or time — "the own nature of Śambhu, rich with wonder." The definition is at once metaphysical and aesthetic: the pulse is what reality IS, and it is tasted as wonder. The author is the disciple of Utpaladeva — the recognition-school's heir — and he opens his commentary with a verse that is both a definition and a poem. The pulse is the light's self-enjoyment.
