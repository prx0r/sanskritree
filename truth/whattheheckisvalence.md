# Deepdive 1 — What the Heck Is Valence?

## Central result

Valence is not one thing.

Current science distinguishes at least four levels that are often collapsed:

[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
Sem_i &: \text{objective significance for persistence};\
V_i &: \text{regulatory weighting of states and actions};\
\nu_i &: \text{felt pleasantness or unpleasantness};\
\rho_i &: \text{the proposed basal self-affection of manifestation}.
\end{aligned}
}
]

The first two can exist in systems for which phenomenal experience is unknown. The third is phenomenal by definition. The fourth is the Śaiva–inexternalist hypothesis that manifestation is intrinsically non-neutral before it becomes locally pleasant or painful.

Therefore:

[
\boxed{
Sem_i\neq V_i\neq\nu_i\neq\rho_i.
}
]

The strongest scientific account does not locate valence purely in the stimulus, purely in the organism, or purely in a reflective judgment. Local valence is best modelled as the felt aspect of a temporally evolving relation among:

* bodily regulation;
* action confidence;
* expectation;
* control;
* learning;
* motivational systems;
* the present phenomenal field.

That gives:

[
\boxed{
\nu_i(t)
========

F\left(
R_i,
\Delta C_i,
P_i,
A_i,
H_i,
\Xi_i
\right),
}
]

where:

* (R_i): regulatory condition;
* (\Delta C_i): change in expected control;
* (P_i): prediction and precision dynamics;
* (A_i): primary affective circuitry;
* (H_i): history;
* (\Xi_i): context.

No one currently established variable—symmetry, reward prediction error, homeostasis, processing fluency, or arousal—completely determines valence.

---

# Part I — What valence is not

## 1. Valence is not a property of the stimulus alone

Sugar, pain, music, social contact, and novelty do not possess invariant positive or negative valence independently of the organism.

The same stimulus can become:

* pleasant when needed;
* unpleasant when excessive;
* boring when predictable;
* attractive under deprivation;
* aversive after sickness;
* emotionally neutral in another context.

Reward neuroscience distinguishes at least:

[
\text{liking},
\quad
\text{wanting},
\quad
\text{learning}.
]

Berridge’s work shows that incentive motivation can be strongly elevated without a corresponding increase in hedonic pleasure. The neural mechanisms producing “wanting” and “liking” substantially dissociate. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][1])

This immediately blocks:

[
\boxed{
\text{rāga}
===========

\text{pleasure}.
}
]

Rāga is closer to:

[
\text{motivational attraction or lack}
]

than to consummatory enjoyment.

A person may intensely want something they no longer enjoy:

[
\text{wanting}\uparrow
\quad\text{while}\quad
\text{liking}\downarrow.
]

That is one of the clearest scientific reasons to keep:

[
V_i
]

separate from:

[
\nu_i.
]

---

## 2. Valence is not the same as reward

Reward can refer to:

* an objectively delivered outcome;
* a learned reinforcer;
* incentive salience;
* prediction error;
* behavioural approach;
* conscious pleasure.

These are not interchangeable.

Reward-prediction-error signals encode whether an outcome is better or worse than expected, while surprise concerns the magnitude of deviation. Experimental work indicates that outcome valence and surprise can have partly separable neural representations. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][2])

Thus:

[
\delta_t
========

r_t-\hat r_t
]

is not identical with felt valence.

A positive prediction error can occur without intense pleasure, and pleasure can occur without a new positive prediction error.

---

## 3. Valence is not arousal

Dimensional emotion models often use:

[
\text{valence}
\times
\text{arousal}.
]

Arousal measures activation or intensity. Valence measures positive or negative character.

Both intense joy and intense fear may involve high arousal:

[
A_i\uparrow,
]

while possessing opposite valence:

[
\nu_i>0
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\nu_i<0.
]

Recent work continues to find useful information in both dimensional ratings and discrete emotional categories rather than demonstrating that one exhausts the other. ([Nature][3])

# Part II — Dimensional, discrete, and constructed emotion

## 4. The dimensional approach

Dimensional models propose that affective states can be organized using axes such as:

* pleasant–unpleasant;
* activated–deactivated;
* sometimes dominance or control.

These models are useful for representing broad affective similarities.

But:

[
\text{fear}
\neq
\text{anger}
]

even when both are negatively valenced and highly aroused.

Valence–arousal space gives a coarse geometry, not a complete theory of emotion.

## 5. Panksepp’s primary-process systems

Panksepp proposed evolutionarily conserved subcortical affective systems commonly labelled:

* SEEKING;
* FEAR;
* RAGE;
* LUST;
* CARE;
* PANIC/GRIEF;
* PLAY.

His framework treats primary affective feelings as grounded in ancient mammalian brain systems, while more elaborate emotions incorporate learning and cortical processing. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][4])

This model’s greatest contribution to our framework is that valence is **plural**.

Negative valence is not generated by one generic “badness circuit.” Fear, separation distress, rage, and physical pain have different action tendencies and neural organizations.

Positive valence is similarly heterogeneous:

* exploratory activation;
* social care;
* play;
* consummatory pleasure;
* relief;
* mastery.

Therefore:

[
\nu_i
]

should not be a lone scalar in the complete model.

A richer form is:

[
\boxed{
\nu_i
=====

\left\langle
h_i,
a_i,
m_i,
q_i
\right\rangle,
}
]

where:

* (h_i): hedonic tone;
* (a_i): action tendency;
* (m_i): motivational orientation;
* (q_i): qualitative affective mode.

A scalar may summarize positive versus negative character, but it does not preserve the whole phenomenal structure.

---

## 6. Do Panksepp’s systems map onto the kañcukas?

Only very loosely.

### Rāga and SEEKING

This is the strongest possible bridge:

[
\text{rāga}
\leftrightarrow
\text{motivational reaching}.
]

But Pankseppian SEEKING is an exploratory and appetitive system, while rāga in the kañcuka framework is metaphysical limitation through lack or incomplete possession.

They are not synonyms.

### Kāla and PANIC

The proposed link:

[
\text{kāla}
===========

\text{panic that time is running out}
]

should be rejected.

Kāla restricts temporal access or sequence. PANIC/GRIEF concerns separation distress.

Anxiety about time is one possible content generated inside temporal limitation, not the meaning of kāla itself.

### Niyati and FEAR

Niyati concerns causal or situational determination. Fear may respond to constrained escape possibilities, but the concepts remain categorically different.

### Kalā and agency systems

Kalā, limited power of action, may be related to the aperture’s available policy repertoire. Again, this is a functional bridge, not a direct mapping to one affective circuit.

The safe rule is:

[
\boxed{
\text{kañcukas structure the field in which affects arise;}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{they are not a list of basic emotions.}
}
]

---

# Part III — The adaptation-rate hypothesis

## 7. The central intuition

The adaptation-rate family of theories proposes that affect tracks not merely current error, but how well the agent expects itself to reduce uncertainty or regulatory discrepancy.

A simplified candidate is:

[
\boxed{
\nu_i(t)
\propto
-\frac{d}{dt}
\left[
\epsilon_i(t)-\hat\epsilon_i(t)
\right],
}
]

where:

* (\epsilon_i(t)): current regulatory or predictive error;
* (\hat\epsilon_i(t)): expected error trajectory.

Positive valence occurs when control or error reduction improves faster than expected:

[
\frac{d\epsilon}{dt}
<
\frac{d\hat\epsilon}{dt}.
]

Negative valence occurs when control deteriorates or improvement is slower than expected:

[
\frac{d\epsilon}{dt}

>

\frac{d\hat\epsilon}{dt}.
]

This captures something important:

[
\boxed{
\text{valence may concern the trajectory of adaptation,}
}
]

not just the current state.

## 8. The stronger active-inference formulation

Hesp and colleagues model valence as an inferred estimate of expected precision or “subjective fitness”: confidence that the agent’s action model will successfully regulate its interaction with the world. Valence becomes a second-order belief about the fitness of first-order beliefs and policies. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][5])

In simplified form:

[
\nu_i
\sim
\operatorname{Belief}
\left[
\operatorname{PolicyModel}_i
\text{ is working}
\right].
]

Positive valence means:

[
\operatorname{ExpectedPrecision}(\pi)\uparrow.
]

Negative valence means:

[
\operatorname{ExpectedPrecision}(\pi)\downarrow.
]

This is stronger than “prediction error feels bad.”

The system may tolerate large errors positively if it expects to master them.

Conversely, a relatively small error may feel bad if it signals loss of control.

---

## 9. Why perfect predictability becomes boring

If a situation is completely predictable:

* error is already minimal;
* little new information is available;
* no meaningful improvement is occurring;
* the action model is not being tested or expanded.

Thus:

[
\epsilon\approx0
]

does not necessarily produce high positive valence.

Positive affect may peak where uncertainty is:

* real;
* manageable;
* informative;
* reducible through action.

Formally:

[
\nu_i
\approx
F
\left(
\operatorname{Challenge},
\operatorname{Learnability},
\operatorname{Control}
\right).
]

Too little uncertainty:

[
\text{boredom}.
]

Too much uncontrollable uncertainty:

[
\text{anxiety or helplessness}.
]

Intermediate reducible uncertainty:

[
\text{interest, play, mastery, flow}.
]

This is not yet a universally validated law, but it gives a coherent explanation of why pleasure cannot be identified with minimal surprise alone.

---

## 10. Insight and comprehension

Sudden insight reorganizes a difficult problem into a more compressible or controllable structure:

[
\text{many unresolved relations}
\rightarrow
\text{one coherent model}.
]

The affective “aha” may therefore track a sudden increase in expected model fitness:

[
\Delta
\operatorname{ExpectedPrecision}
\gg0.
]

This gives a serious scientific candidate for *camatkāra* in one restricted context:

[
\boxed{
\text{wonder or delight at insight}
===================================

\text{felt transition into unexpectedly greater intelligibility}.
}
]

It does not establish that all *camatkāra* reduces to prediction-error dynamics.

---

## 11. Play

Play repeatedly generates uncertainty that is:

* safe enough to explore;
* structured enough to learn;
* flexible enough to permit novelty;
* consequential enough to matter.

This fits the adaptation-rate model:

[
\text{play}
===========

\text{self-generated reducible uncertainty}.
]

Panksepp’s PLAY system suggests that play is not merely a cognitive judgment imposed on neutral behaviour; it has a primary affective and social basis in mammalian brains. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][4])

This may be the most scientifically disciplined bridge between:

[
\text{svātantrya}
]

and:

[
\text{positive valence}.
]

The process freely generates constraints and then enjoys mastering them:

[
\boxed{
\text{self-created limitation}
\rightarrow
\text{successful expansion within limitation}
\rightarrow
\text{positive affect}.
}
]

That is structurally close to the Śaiva image of freedom expressing itself through self-limitation, though the science does not establish that metaphysical interpretation.

# Part IV — Semantic information and valence

## 12. Objective significance

Kolchinsky and Wolpert define semantic information as those correlations between a physical system and its environment that are causally necessary for maintaining the system’s viability. They identify relevance through counterfactual interventions that scramble correlations and measure the effect on continued low-entropy persistence. ([Royal Society Publishing][6])

Thus:

[
Sem_i(x)
========

\text{information about }x
\text{ whose removal reduces viability}.
]

This creates an objective, non-observer-relative sense in which information matters to a system.

## 13. Regulatory weighting

A system can then weight states according to expected viability:

[
V_i(x)
======

\Delta
\operatorname{Viability}_i(x).
]

The system approaches, preserves, avoids, or repairs states according to these weights.

This is still not felt pleasure or pain.

A resource-gathering model can possess a measurable semantic-information threshold: beyond a certain loss of environmentally relevant information, expected survival falls sharply. ([arXiv][7])

Nothing in that result proves the model experiences distress.

Therefore the hierarchy remains:

[
\boxed{
Sem_i
\rightarrow
V_i
\stackrel{?}{\rightarrow}
\nu_i.
}
]

## 14. The missing bridge

Why should:

[
\text{bad for the system}
]

become:

[
\text{bad for the system to experience}?
]

Three possibilities remain.

### Functionalist identity

[
\nu_i
=====

V_i
]

when value regulation reaches the appropriate architecture.

### Psychophysical law

[
V_i
\Rightarrow
\nu_i
]

under an additional law.

### Inexternal identity

The regulatory process and felt valence are one event:

[
\nu_i
\equiv_{\mathrm{inext}}
\operatorname{Int}(V_i).
]

### Pratyabhijñā participation

Regulatory valence modulates a field whose manifest nature is already self-affective:

[
\nu_i
=====

\operatorname{Modulation}
\left(
\rho_i,V_i
\right).
]

The last option preserves the distinction:

[
\boxed{
\rho_i
======

\text{that experience is intrinsically self-given},
}
]

while:

[
\boxed{
\nu_i
=====

\text{how this particular condition feels}.
}
]

# Part V — QRI and symmetry

## 15. What STV claims

QRI’s Symmetry Theory of Valence proposes:

[
\boxed{
\nu_i
=====

F
\left[
\operatorname{Symmetry}
\left(
\mathcal Q_i
\right)
\right],
}
]

where (\mathcal Q_i) is the mathematical object representing a conscious state.

In its strongest form, the valence of a conscious moment is precisely determined by the symmetry of its formal structure. ([Qualia Research Institute][8])

This is a clear, bold hypothesis.

It is not an established empirical theory.

## 16. What survives

Three useful principles survive the audit.

### Valence is real

Pleasure and suffering are not merely verbal judgments.

### Valence belongs to the conscious state

An object does not contain its valence independently of the complete field.

The pleasantness of music depends on:

* bodily state;
* context;
* expectation;
* attention;
* memory;
* the entire experienced organization.

### Formal structure may matter

Coherence, conflict, rhythm, integration, and dynamical organization may influence valence.

What does not survive is:

[
\boxed{
\text{symmetry alone has been shown to determine valence}.
}
]

The relevant:

* state space;
* symmetry group;
* spatial or temporal scale;
* neural implementation;
* phenomenological measurement;

remain underspecified.

## 17. The “tyranny of the intentional object”

The valuable insight is that researchers often ask:

> Is this object pleasant?

rather than:

> What is the complete state of the experiencing field?

The correct unit is:

[
D_i(t),
]

not the intentional object (x) alone.

Thus:

[
\nu_i(t)
\neq
F(x).
]

Instead:

[
\nu_i(t)
========

F
\left(
x,
B_i,
H_i,
R_i,
P_i,
\mathcal G_i
\right).
]

This strongly agrees with the aperture framework.

# Part VI — The five blisses and camatkāra

## 18. Do the five blisses form a logarithmic scale?

No evidence currently establishes that the five Śaiva blisses correspond to a logarithmic psychophysical scale.

The doctrinal sequence may articulate differences among:

* innate or immanent bliss;
* cessation or repose;
* superior transcendence;
* expansive great bliss;
* consciousness-bliss.

But this is a phenomenological–soteriological classification, not a quantitative measurement scale.

Therefore:

[
\boxed{
\text{five blisses}
\neq
\text{evidence for logarithmic valence}.
}
]

A legitimate research programme could ask whether practitioners report:

* ordinal progression;
* qualitative transformation;
* increasing intensity;
* decreasing subject–object contraction;
* altered temporal structure.

But numerical spacing must not be assumed.

## 19. Existential and experiential camatkāra

The most useful distinction is the one already established in the rasa audit.

### Existential camatkāra

[
\rho_i
======

\text{manifestation’s intrinsic self-affective character}.
]

### Experiential camatkāra

[
\omega_i
========

\text{a locally intensified state of wonder, relish,}
]

[
\text{astonishment, or expansive recognition}.
]

These are not identical.

Every conscious field might possess basal self-affection without every moment involving explicit wonder.

Thus:

[
M_i\Rightarrow\rho_i
]

is the basal rasa hypothesis.

Whereas:

[
\omega_i
========

F
\left(
\rho_i,
\Delta\mathcal A_i,
\Delta\operatorname{Intelligibility}_i,
\Delta\partial_i
\right)
]

models intensified wonder as expansion of accessible meaning, possibility, or self-world organization.

# Part VII — The three philosophical positions

## 20. Valence realism

Valence is an intrinsic property of conscious states:

[
\nu_i\in D_i.
]

This is almost unavoidable phenomenologically. Pain and pleasure are not reducible to propositions about them.

But realism alone does not explain their causes.

## 21. Relationalism

Valence emerges through organism–environment relations:

[
\nu_i
=====

F(O_i,W_i).
]

This explains why the same stimulus can change valence across contexts and bodily states.

But it may leave open why the relation feels like anything.

## 22. Constructivism

Valence or emotion is generated through predictive, interoceptive, and conceptual organization rather than detected as a pre-formed property. Constructed-emotion theories emphasize active inference, interoception, and learned conceptual categorization. ([PubMed Central (PMC)][9])

This explains:

* cultural variation;
* context;
* categorization;
* flexible interpretation.

But a purely conceptual account struggles with:

* infant affect;
* nonhuman affect;
* subcortical hedonic reactions;
* pain and pleasure without elaborate emotional concepts.

## 23. The hybrid aperture position

The strongest position is layered:

[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\rho_i &: \text{intrinsic self-affection};\
\nu_i &: \text{field-level hedonic character};\
A_i &: \text{primary affective mode};\
E_i &: \text{constructed emotion category};\
N_i &: \text{narrative interpretation}.
\end{aligned}
}
]

This allows:

* basal affect before concepts;
* primary biological affective systems;
* relational regulation;
* constructed emotional categories;
* reflective interpretation.

It avoids reducing all affect to either raw circuits or conceptual judgment.
