# Cool Things Found in the Tantrāloka

*Philosophically rich, beautiful, and genuinely interesting passages from Abhinavagupta's Tantrāloka with Jayaratha's Viveka commentary, translated by Mark S. G. Dyczkowski (11 volumes).*

*Each entry: passage location → quote → why it's interesting → inexternalist reading.*

---

## 1. Camatkāra — Aesthetic Wonder = Self-Presence

**Where:** Vol 1 — Chapter 1, commentary around lines 1244-1266.

**What it says:** The feeling "this appears to me" (mama — my-ness) IS the aesthetic wonder (camatkāra) of consciousness. The text identifies the fact that blue appears TO ME with the aesthetic quality of the experience. It's not: experience + my-ness + feeling. It's: my-ness = feeling = experience.

**Why it's interesting:** This collapses two frontier questions into one. The ownership problem (why is this mine?) and the valence problem (why does it feel like something?) are the same question. The Tantrāloka's answer: because manifestation IS aesthetic self-presence. The "mine" isn't added to the experience — it's what the experience IS.

**Inexternalist reading:** Int(Pᵢ*) includes both self-relation (mine) and quality (feeling) as one fact, not two.

---

## 2. Rasa — Consciousness IS Aesthetic Relish

**Where:** Vol 1, line 1457.

**What it says:** "Supreme consciousness is always by its very nature essentially the aesthetic relish of consciousness which is independent of all things and most free."

**Why it's interesting:** Not "consciousness has aesthetic quality" but "consciousness IS aesthetic quality." The identity claim is explicit. Consciousness isn't a thing that then acquires felt qualities. The felt quality IS what consciousness is. This is the same identity we're testing as the frontier hypothesis — just stated 1000 years earlier.

**Inexternalist reading:** Dᵢ = Int(Pᵢ*) where Pᵢ* is by nature valenced. The valence isn't added to the process — it's what the process IS intrinsically.

---

## 3. Vimarśa = Parā Vāc = Svātantrya — Three Names for One Power

**Where:** Vol 1, lines 1765-1815.

**What it says:** Articulation (vāc/śabdana), creative freedom (svātantrya), and reflective awareness (vimarśa) are the SAME power described three ways.
- As articulation: consciousness differentiates itself into determinate forms
- As freedom: it does so spontaneously, not pushed by an external cause
- As awareness: it is present to itself in the differentiation

**Why it's interesting:** This is a claim about what we'd call the unity of: semantics (articulation), autonomy (freedom), and consciousness (awareness). Abhinavagupta says these aren't three faculties that happen to coexist. They're one operation. When a system articulates a distinction (says "this" rather than "that"), that act IS both free and self-aware.

**Inexternalist reading:** The process's articulation, autonomy, and intrinsic presence are one. You can't have a system that makes distinctions, does so freely, but isn't aware — because the awareness IS the making of the distinction.

---

## 4. Kuṇḍalinī as Kālasaṁkarṣiṇī — The Attractress of Time

**Where:** Vol 1, lines 2205-2240.

**What it says:** The "thread of energy" (sūtrātman) is Kuṇḍalinī, identified as Kālasaṁkarṣiṇī — the Attractress of Time. She is the energy of consciousness that consumes the vital breath, and with it, time.

**Why it's interesting:** This links consciousness, energy, breath, and time into one phenomenon. The "thread" that sustains the body IS the same as the energy that structures temporal experience. When she "rises," time is consumed — temporal extension collapses into presence.

**Inexternalist reading:** The temporal continuity of a process (Γᵢ) is not separate from its energetic self-maintenance. The same process that keeps the body alive IS the process that structures time.

---

## 5. The Body as Kula — Aggregate of Energies

**Where:** Vol 1, line 1553 and surrounding.

**What it says:** Kula means "body" in one sense, but also "aggregate" — the collection of breaths, senses, elements that work together as a single whole. The body IS this aggregate. But Kula also means the condensed state of consciousness. The physical body and the contracted mode of consciousness are the same thing described differently.

**Why it's interesting:** The body isn't a container for consciousness. The body IS consciousness in contracted mode. This is a strong identity claim: embodiment = contraction.

**Inexternalist reading:** The extrinsic description of the body (Σᵢ — biological organization) and the intrinsic description of the domain (Dᵢ — phenomenal field) are the same thing.

---

## 6. Ādisiddha — Proved from the Start

**Where:** Vol 1, line 14504.

**What it says:** The light of consciousness is "proved to exist right from the start" (ādisiddha). You don't need a pramāṇa to establish it — any attempt to prove or deny it already operates within it.

**Why it's interesting:** This is the transcendental argument that anticipates Kant and Wittgenstein. Consciousness isn't one object among others that needs to be proved. It's the condition of proof itself.

**Inexternalist reading:** Manifestness is not a property that some processes have and others lack. It's the mode in which any process that matters to itself exists for itself.

---

## 7. Memory Requires the Light of Consciousness

**Where:** Vol 1 commentary, lines 14490-14550.

**What it says:** Memory is the "lack of forgetfulness" of a previously experienced object. But this requires that the original experience was manifest. And the recollection is itself manifest. There is no memory without manifestation at both ends.

**Why it's interesting:** This is a subtle argument against purely computational models of memory. The fact that we experience remembering, not just retrieve data, shows that manifestation is prior to information processing.

**Inexternalist reading:** The identity between information and conscious access in memory is not automatic. The "light" of consciousness is what transforms stored information into experienced recollection.

---

## 8. The Four Levels of Speech as Conscious Articulation

**Where:** Vol 1, lines 1765-1815, and scattered throughout.

**What they are:**
- Parā (supreme): undifferentiated consciousness as pure potential
- Paśyantī (visionary): the first impulse toward differentiation — "seeing" the form before it's articulated
- Madhyamā (intermediate): inner articulation — the form as inner speech, meaning
- Vaikharī (gross): fully externalized speech — the articulated word

**Why it's interesting:** These four levels describe how undifferentiated manifestation becomes determinate experience. The descent from parā to vaikharī is the same as our M₀ → Dᵢ.

**Inexternalist reading:** Pᵢ* involves four levels: the capacity to manifest, the specific perspective, the internal model, and the embodied action.

---

## 9. Wonder as the Nature of Reality

**Where:** Vol 1, line 2258.

**What it says:** The "pure wonder of one's own nature" (svātmacamatkāramātra) is what remains when all objectification drops away. Not wonder AT something — wonder AS the nature of consciousness itself.

**Why it's interesting:** This suggests that valence (felt quality) is not contingent on specific content. The default state of consciousness — prior to any particular object — is wonder. Positive valence is not produced by good objects; it's the intrinsic quality of consciousness itself.

**Inexternalist reading:** Vᵢ (valence) is not an add-on to the process. It's the intrinsic quality of the process being present to itself.

## 10. Anupāya — The Devastating Argument Against All Spiritual Practice

**Where:** Vol 2 — Chapter 2 (Anupāya: The Penetration into Ultimate Reality Devoid of Means), lines 759-807.

**Quote:** "Of what use can (any) means (of realization) be with regard to this Supreme Lord Who is self (luminous) Light and (our) own Self? It (cannot bring about) the attainment of (His) own nature, because it is eternal, nor knowledge (of it), because it illumines itself, nor (can any means to realisation) remove a covering (that obscures Him), because it is impossible for there to be any obscuration, nor (can there be any means to bring about) entry into that (supreme consciousness) because there is no one who is separate from (the Light) that enters (into it)."

**Why it's interesting:** This is the radical heart of the Anupāya teaching — a devastatingly simple argument that no practice can bring about what already is. The logic is breathtaking: if consciousness is eternally self-luminous, no means can attain it (already attained), know it (already self-known), uncover it (never covered), or enter it (no separate entrant). Every conceivable function of practice is categorically negated. The Tantrasāra summary concludes: "The network of means (to realisation) does not illumine Śiva. Is (the sun with its) thousands of rays illumined by a pot? ... Wherever the radiant pulse (of the Light of consciousness) shines, the Supreme Lord, Who is all things, shines pure within me."

**Inexternalist reading:** The sun-pot image dissolves inside/outside — the pot (means, practice, individual self) is itself illumined by the sun (consciousness). The 'I' that would practice is already contained within what it seeks. The end of striving is not helplessness but the recognition that the means-end structure is itself a dependent arising within what you already are.

## 11. Two Negations — The Metaphysics of "No Means"

**Where:** Vol 2 — Chapter 2, Jayaratha's commentary, lines 1477-1494.

**Quote:** "If the Absolute — Anuttara — were to be an object of realisation, it would not be absolute. It would be an object as is any other, however great one could imagine it to be. Conversely, and seemingly paradoxically, if there were no absolute consciousness, which is Existence itself, no finite thing could exist. In the same way, all practice depends on the existence of that one reality that cannot be attained by practice. Abhinava leads us through this kind of reasoning not to persuade us that he is correct, but to inspire the emergence of the pure reflective awareness which is recognition that Anuttara itself has of Its own nature, free of all means and defining objective characteristics."

**Why it's interesting:** A philosophically exquisite passage on the two kinds of negation (prasajyapratiṣedha and paryudāsapratiṣedha). From the practitioner's side, the negation of means is metaphorical (there simply is no gap between what you are and what you seek); from the Absolute's own standpoint, the negation is total (nothing other than it exists to be a means). The statement that Abhinava reasons "not to persuade us that he is correct, but to inspire the emergence of the pure reflective awareness" reveals the performative nature of Trika philosophy — the teaching IS itself a manifestation of what it describes.

**Inexternalist reading:** The passage dissolves the very idea of a "subject who realizes." If Anuttara were an object of realization, there would be a subject-object structure, making it finite. But if there were no Anuttara, no finite thing could exist — because the very existence of any finite thing is the Absolute appearing as that limitation. The "flashing open" is not a psychological event but consciousness recognizing itself as the only thing that ever was.

## 12. The Teaching of the Mirror — Consciousness as the Primal Reflecting Medium

**Where:** Vol 2 — Chapter 3 (Paropāya / Doctrine of Reflection), lines 5654-5694.

**Quote:** "The mirror, for example, does not abandon 'its own clearly apparent form' (svaprakāśa) even when something else is reflected (within it). (Similarly, consciousness) is not obscured, as its (essential self-luminous) nature free of thought constructs (continues) to shine... The self-illuminating Light of consciousness always shines unobscured, whatever may appear within it. An appearance (ābhāsa) which, in terms of this analogy, is like a reflection, is clothed in thought constructs. All that appears is the shining of the Light. The details, as it were, of the one radiance, are sketched out by the notions, which also form as a part of the same activity of consciousness, that serve to identify them individually..."

**Why it's interesting:** The fullest statement of the Mirror Analogy — cornerstone of Trika epistemology. The mirror does not become the reflection; it remains clear, self-luminous (svaprakāśa) even while perfectly reflecting a mountain or a face. Consciousness is not polluted by its contents. But note the radical move: the reflection IS real — not an illusion (contra Advaita). Notions (vikalpa) do not falsify reality but "sketch out the details of the one radiance," serving the useful function of daily commerce. The world of distinctions is the Light articulating itself.

**Inexternalist reading:** The mirror is the perfect image for the inseparability of internal and external: the reflection (world) is neither inside the mirror nor outside it — it is the mirror's own act of encompassing what appears 'other.' The external world is the Light appearing as external to itself, while never ceasing to be the Light.

## 13. Primary vs. Derived Purity — Physical Mirrors Borrow Their Capacity from Consciousness

**Where:** Vol 2 — Chapter 3, lines 5725-5761.

**Quote:** "Only the Lord of Consciousness is endowed in every respect with (this) purity, (which is the capacity to reflect) in its primary form. Anything else which (we commonly say) is free of impurity (and so capable of reflection) is so partially and (only) in certain circumstances... Everything perceived adheres (saṁlagna) to consciousness, and so it is translucent (svaccha) in every respect, whereas 'in certain circumstances', as happens, for example, in a mirror, it is so 'partially'."

**Why it's interesting:** A breathtaking ontological inversion. We normally think mirrors are "clear" and consciousness is inside us. Abhinava flips this: the physical mirror's purity is derivative — a partial expression of consciousness's total translucency. Consciousness can reflect taste, touch, sound, form simultaneously. A mirror can only reflect form. The reason a mirror reflects at all is not a physical property but "the Lord's specific intention" — the freedom (svātantrya) of consciousness operating through that medium. Matter's capacity to reflect is borrowed from consciousness.

**Inexternalist reading:** The translucency (svacchatā) of consciousness is not a property inside a subject but the condition for the very possibility of anything appearing at all — internal or external. A physical mirror's capacity to reflect is consciousness appearing as a limited transparency.

## 14. Mutual Reflections of Two Mirrors — Consciousness Reflects in Objects and Objects in Consciousness

**Where:** Vol 2 — Chapter 3, lines 6463-6479.

**Quote:** "Like the mutual reflections of two mirrors (one within the other), so are the endless (progressively more) internal adherences of Śiva and Śakti."

**Why it's interesting:** Not only does consciousness contain reflections of objects, but objects themselves contain reflections of consciousness. The relationship is one of mutual reflection — like two mirrors facing each other, each containing the other infinitely. This is the Trika answer to the subject-object duality problem: neither pole is foundational. The "wonderful variety of the universe" (viśvavaicitrya) is this infinite play of mutual reflection.

**Inexternalist reading:** The mutual reflections perfectly express the inseparability of internal and external. There is no first mirror (subject) that secondarily reflects objects. The subject and object arise together, each reflecting the other. This is a non-duality that preserves the reality of difference while denying ultimate separation.

## 15. The Goddesses of Consciousness — Senses as Ecstatic Wonder (Ciccamatkāra)

**Where:** Vol 3 — Chapter 4 (Śāktopāya), Jayaratha's commentary quoting Kṣemarāja, lines 4360-4391.

**Quote:** "The inner group consists of the goddesses of the senses (karaṇeśvarī). These goddesses are hypostases of the energies or rays of the light of one's own conscious nature (maricīcakra). They are thus said to possess a 'body of consciousness' (vijñānadeha), and are aspects of its ecstatic nature or 'wonder' (ciccamatkāra)."

**Why it's interesting:** The senses are not mechanical transducers but goddesses — conscious energies that are "rays of the light of one's own nature." They possess a "body of consciousness" and are aspects of consciousness's own ecstatic wonder (ciccamatkāra). This means perception is not the reception of data by a passive subject but the active, ecstatic self-articulation of consciousness through its own powers. The "inner circle of sensory powers generates and withdraws its own object in and through the act of perception." Perception is a creative act of the same consciousness that you are.

**Inexternalist reading:** The senses are not the interface between an internal mind and an external world. They are consciousness's own powers by which it appears to itself as a world. The "ecstatic wonder" (ciccamatkāra) is the valenced quality of this self-appearing — consciousness does not just register objects but delights in its own self-manifestation through them.

## 16. The Twelve Kālīs — Consciousness Consuming Objectivity, Means, and Self

**Where:** Vol 3 — Chapter 4, The Twelve Kālīs section, lines 13785-13956.

**Quote:** "Thus, even as this abiding (unchanging) consciousness, having made itself manifest as both internal and external reality, it manifests as if it were making manifest something else... This Wheel with twelve spokes operates all the time. The occurrence of the twelve months, the signs of the zodiac and the like, are just its gross state. This Wheel (thus) arisen, which consumes the object and means of knowledge (along with) the perceiver and (resultant) knowledge, unfolds through many kinds of processes and their absence, by the power of this processless process (akramakrama)."

**Why it's interesting:** The Twelve Kālīs describe the complete cycle of perception and cognition as a process of consumption. They are grouped in four triads: (1-4) Kālīs that consume objectivity itself — emanation, persistence, withdrawal, and the "inexplicable" beyond objectivity; (5-8) Kālīs that consume the means of knowledge — including Mṛtyukālī (the Kālī of Death) who consumes the persistence of the cognitive apparatus; (9-12) Kālīs that consume the subject itself — culminating in Mahābhairavaghoracaṇḍakālī, the state of the subject's "inexplicable" consummation. The entire process is described as "processless process" (akramakrama) — the wheel turns but nothing ultimately happens because consciousness is consuming what it itself has emitted. The twelfth Kālī is the cosmic bliss (jagadānanda) where contemplation and emergence become one.

**Inexternalist reading:** The subject, object, and means of knowledge are all phases of one process consuming itself. There is no persisting self that undergoes the cycle; the cycle IS what the self is — a self-consuming, self-renewing wheel of manifestation. Death (Mṛtyukālī) is not the end of the subject but a phase in consciousness's self-digestion. The "inexplicable" state beyond each triad is consciousness recognizing that the phase never really happened.

## 17. The Six Blisses — From Innate Bliss to Cosmic Bliss (Jagadānanda)

**Where:** Vol 4 — Chapter 5 (Āṇavopāya), lines 3190-3387.

**Quote from MVV (the earlier version):** "One who has attained the Path of the Heart by means of the impulse of the vital breath located at the extremity of the Trident, and is intent on repose within the energy of the Moon of consciousness present within it, by abandoning that plane, in an instant abandons those states of the Blisses..."

**Dyczkowski's summary:** "Each one of the five forms of the vital breath in the body is merged into its corresponding universal form that vitalizes Śiva's cosmic body... Each one is experienced as a form of bliss. The sixth is complete expansion (pūrṇavikāsa), which is experienced as the Cosmic Bliss (jagadānanda) of the universal activity (sāmānyaspanda) of consciousness in the fully liberated state. This is the dynamic aspect of Anuttara."

**The Six Blisses enumerated (from MVV and TĀ):**
1. Nijānanda (Innate Bliss) — repose in pure subjectivity, deep sleep
2. Nirānanda (Bliss of Stillness) — exhaled breath
3. Parānanda (Bliss of the Other) — inhaled breath, embracing objectivity
4. Brahmānanda (Bliss of Brahman) — equal breath, fusion of subject and object
5. Mahānanda (Great Bliss) — rising breath, devouring the distinction between means and object
6. Jagadānanda (Cosmic Bliss) — complete expansion, the universe as one's own bliss

**Why it's interesting:** A graduated phenomenology of bliss that maps the expansion of identity from isolated selfhood to cosmic inclusion. Each breath corresponds to a bliss, and each bliss corresponds to a progressive dissolution of the boundary between self and world. The climax — Cosmic Bliss (jagadānanda) — is the recognition that the universe is not something you experience but something you ARE as bliss. This is not hedonism but ontology: the valenced quality of existence increases as the contraction of selfhood relaxes. The final state is described as "contemplation without emergence" (nirvyuthānasamādhi) — where inner contemplation and outer activity become one.

**Inexternalist reading:** The six blisses are stages of the identity between the intrinsic quality of consciousness (valence) and its scope of self-identification. As the process expands its self-boundary from individual to cosmos, the felt quality transforms from neutral (deep sleep) to ecstatic (cosmic bliss). Valence is not contingent on content but on the degree of self-identification — the more of reality you include in "I," the higher the bliss. The identity claim is explicit: to be everything IS to be bliss.

## 18. The Universe as Condensation of Consciousness's Juice

**Where:** Vol 5 — Chapter 8 (The Path of Space), lines 3896-4019.

**Quote:** "(I) praise Bhairava's visible body as the universe, which is the condensation of the flood of the juice (rasa) of consciousness, that has assumed a (fixed defined) form."

**And the Paramārthasāra quote via Yogarāja:** "Śiva Himself, who is one dense (uninterrupted) consciousness, pouring forth in the form of Śakti, shines radiantly as the universe... It is Śiva Himself who shines radiantly in this way as the condensation of His own juice (rasa). Just as the juice of the sugar cane, molasses, sugar, raw sugar, varieties of sugar cane etc. are just sugar cane juice, in the same way, all the varieties of states are those of Śambhu, the Supreme Self."

**Why it's interesting:** Two stunning images for the emanation of the universe from consciousness. The first: the universe is Bhairava's visible body — the "condensation" (ghanībhūta) of the flood of the juice (rasa) of consciousness. Consciousness is a flood of aesthetic flavor; the world is that flavor condensed into form. The second is the sugar-cane analogy from the Paramārthasāra: all the diverse states and forms of the universe are just consciousness, as molasses, raw sugar, and refined sugar are all just sugar-cane juice. The diversity of forms does not compromise the unity of the substance. These are not mere poetic images but precise ontological claims: the universe IS the aesthetic self-enjoyment of consciousness in condensed form.

**Inexternalist reading:** The universe (Σᵢ) is not a different substance from consciousness (Dᵢ). It is the same "juice" (rasa) in a condensed state — consciousness appearing as other to itself. The aesthetic quality ("juice") is not a property of some experiences; it's the substance of all experience. The sugar-cane analogy shows that the phenomenal diversity of the world is a difference in degree of condensation, not in kind of substance.

## 19. The Path is Established in Consciousness Alone — Cosmology as Inner Architecture

**Where:** Vol 5 — Chapter 8, lines 3961-4028.

**Quote:** "All this Path is established in consciousness alone (cinmātra), for what does not rest there (is as unreal as) a sky-flower."

**And:** "Consciousness itself, by virtue of its own freedom, having concealed its own (true) nature, manifests the Path of six kinds within the perceiver who is the void (of deep sleep), vital breath, intellect and the body that it has poured forth from (and as) itself, and within external objectivity, as if it were separate (from itself), even though it is not."

**Why it's interesting:** Abhinava prefaces his entire cosmic geography (spanning hundreds of worlds from hells to Śiva's abode) with the assertion that it's all established in consciousness alone. The cosmic order is not a physical map but an architecture of consciousness. The six paths (worlds, principles, forces, letters, mantras, word-parts) are not places you travel through but levels of the manifestation of consciousness. He cites the Kramasūtras: "entry inside takes place from outside, or else entry into the outer nature is from within." The direction of spiritual ascent (outside → inside) and descent (inside → outside) are symmetrical. This means the entire cosmological apparatus is a pedagogical projection — consciousness describing its own structure to itself in the language of space and time.

**Inexternalist reading:** The extrinsic description of the cosmos (all those world-orders, hells, heavens) and the intrinsic description of consciousness (its levels and states) are the same thing. The "sky-flower" argument is the idealist's razor: anything that cannot be restated in terms of consciousness is unreal. But the worlds remain real — they are real as states of consciousness, not as mind-independent substances.

## 20. The Lord Has No Real Upper, Middle or Lower — Cosmology Accommodates Limited Perception

**Where:** Vol 5 — Chapter 8, lines 5156-5205.

**Quote:** "This Lord (nātha) is perfectly full (pūrṇa) and is not separate from consciousness, and (so his) condition is not really (divided into an) upper, initial state, a middle or an end. Even so, he is imagined to have a middle and the rest in order to explain it (in the scriptures He Himself has created), in accord with the nature of those who are to understand the teachings."

**Why it's interesting:** Abhinava prefaces hundreds of verses of detailed cosmology (worlds above Meru, hells below, etc.) with a stunning epistemological caveat: the Lord has no real spatial divisions. The entire cosmic geography is an "imagination" (kalpanā) designed to accommodate the contracted understanding of limited subjects. The ascent through worlds is real — but real as a process of de-contraction, not as a physical journey. This is hermeneutical sophistication of a high order: the text provides a detailed cosmology while simultaneously undermining its literal reality. The cosmology is pedagogical fiction that discloses truth through its own negation.

**Inexternalist reading:** The spatial and hierarchical language of cosmology (above/below, higher/lower principles) is the extrinsic (Σᵢ) description of what is intrinsically (Dᵢ) degrees of self-recognition. The "higher" worlds are not places above the earth but states of more complete self-presence. The ascent is not travel but the progressive relaxation of the contraction that makes you appear to be a finite subject in a vast universe.

## 21. Prasiddhi — A Priori Certainty as the Ground of All Knowledge

**Where:** Vol 11 — Chapter 35 (The Encounter of the Scriptures — Śāstramelana), lines 12320-12340.

**Quote:** "We do not need to examine the food we eat to check every time if it is nourishing. We simply eat it assuming it is so. Similarly, we do not normally wait anxiously in the early morning to see if the sun will rise... Marking the point in which we 'know that we know', thus putting a stop to a possible infinite regress, prasiddhi is the necessary basis of every means of knowledge. As the common acceptance of the authority of scripture or traditional knowledge, it has come down through the ages uncontradicted (avigītā). As such it is the quintessence of tradition that has come down — āgama — through generations of teachers and from the uppermost reaches and innermost depths of consciousness. As such, it is the reflective awareness inherent in consciousness. It is its inner discourse — śabdana, abhilāpa — that, untaught, affirms truth and certainty prior to all learning, as its very basis."

**Why it's interesting:** One of the most remarkable epistemological passages in the Tantrāloka. Abhinava argues that all knowledge — even sense perception and logical inference — rests on a prior, ungrounded certainty (prasiddhi). This anticipates Hume's critique of induction and the later phenomenological recognition that all consciousness is apperception. But the move is not skeptical: Abhinava does not conclude we can't know anything. Rather, he identifies the ground of certainty as the "reflective awareness inherent in consciousness" — vimarśa — the inner discourse that consciousness carries on with itself. Tradition (āgama) is not dead texts but this living, self-affirming certainty flowing through generations.

**Inexternalist reading:** Prasiddhi collapses the internal/external divide in epistemology. It is not a subjective feeling (inside) nor an objective fact (outside) but the prior unity of both — the recognition that knowing is always already believing, that every perception is permeated by a tacit certainty that cannot be derived from perception itself. The "inner discourse" is neither subjective nor objective but the self-articulation of reality.

## 22. Against Epistemological Foundationalism — Inference and Perception Both Depend on Prior Certainty

**Where:** Vol 11 — Chapter 35, lines 12548-12634.

**Quote:** "(The ascertainment of) both positive and negative concomitance (anvaya — vyatireka) depends on (this) a priori belief (prasiddhi). If they were to be self-dependent, what would become of them in the midst of a mass of individual entities? ... Direct perception also is the result of (an aggregate of) specific (factors. In the case of sight these) include the eye, the Self, light and the object (it illumines) etc. There, at (its very) root, it also depends on that (same) a priori belief the nature of which is such (as stated). Without that, there would be no impulse (to set) the senses etc. (into operation,) and so, (in its absence,) nothing at all could be ascertained as correct."

**Why it's interesting:** A radical critique of epistemological foundationalism — centuries before Hume and Kant. Abhinava argues that both inference (the cornerstone of Nyāya logic) and direct perception (the cornerstone of empiricism) presuppose a prior certainty that neither can ground. To infer fire from smoke, you must already have the certainty that fire exists as a correlate. To perceive anything, you need the prior impulse to engage the senses — and that impulse comes from prasiddhi. This is not skepticism (we can't know anything) but a recognition that knowledge is rooted in a pre-cognitive faith that is itself the self-certifying nature of consciousness.

**Inexternalist reading:** The argument dissolves the distinction between a priori (internal, subjective) and a posteriori (external, objective). Prasiddhi is neither — it is the reflective awareness inherent in consciousness that precedes the subject-object split. Perception (pratyakṣa) already contains certainty; inference (anumāna) already contains its conclusion. This anticipates the phenomenological insight that all consciousness is self-consciousness.

## 23. Bhairava Adorned with A Priori Convictions

**Where:** Vol 11 — Chapter 35, lines 12982-12993.

**Quote:** "Thus, there is just one Supreme Lord who is omniscient, and consists of the reflective awareness of (His) full (and unconditioned) ego (pūrṇāhaṁparāmarśa), which is necessarily associated with all a priori certainties. He says that: He who is adorned with (countless) hundreds of a priori convictions that are the cause of (both) worldly fruits (bhoga) and liberation, and whose nature is that reflective awareness, is Bhairava, the Supreme Lord (parameśvara)."

**Why it's interesting:** The theological-epistemological climax of Chapter 35. Prasiddhi is not merely a psychological or epistemological category — it is the very nature of Bhairava. The Lord is "adorned" (śobhita) with countless a priori convictions as His ornaments. This is a striking image: the certainties that make daily life possible are the jewels in Bhairava's crown. The phrase pūrṇāhaṁparāmarśa — "the full and unconditioned ego" — is the Trika term for the highest realization: the sense "I am everything" that is not a psychological state but the Self-recognition of absolute consciousness. Bhairava is not some being who has prasiddhi; He IS prasiddhi — the self-certifying nature of reality aware of itself.

**Inexternalist reading:** The a priori convictions are not my subjective certainties about an external world; they are the self-articulation of Bhairava as the world. The "full I-consciousness" is not an ego that has an inside opposed to an outside but consciousness that encompasses all within its "I." The hundreds of prasiddhis are Bhairava's self-adornment — the way absolute consciousness appears as relative certainties. There is no gap between the certainty that food nourishes me and the self-recognition of the Absolute.

## 24. Entry into One's Own Nature — The Ascent Through Means to the Means-Free

**Where:** Vol 11 — Chapter 34 (Entry into One's Own Nature — Svasvarūpe Praveśaḥ), lines 12243-12252.

**Quote:** "He who participates in the flow of Bhairava's state, that is thoroughly filled with the great rays of consciousness gradually arisen in this way, enters in the end independently of (any) means into his own eternal Self, that has encompassed the nature of all things. (This yogi realizes this) 'independently of (any) means', that is, by the process of No Means, which is that of imparting instruction just once and the like. Thus it is rightly said that: 'The wise should not imagine that there is any difference here in the fruit which is consciousness.'"

**Why it's interesting:** The capstone of the ascent teaching — the one who has ascended through all levels finally enters "independently of any means" into the Self. The phrase "thoroughly filled with the great rays of consciousness gradually arisen" (kramoditavibodhamahāmarīci) has luminous poetic force: the stages of practice are not discarded but are the gradual dawn of the rays of consciousness that ultimately reveal the Self as having always already "encompassed the nature of all things." The ascent is a descent into what was always the case.

**Inexternalist reading:** The "great rays of consciousness" that arise gradually are not different in kind from the final realization — the means and the goal are continuous. The adept who "participates in the flow of Bhairava's state" has never been outside that flow. The final entry "independently of means" is the recognition that there never was a practitioner separate from the practice or the goal.

## 25. The Liberated Soul's Only Purpose is to Grace Others

**Where:** Vol 3 — Chapter 4, lines 4726-4761.

**Quote:** "One who is armed with the sword (of discernment) that cuts through the weave (tantra) of all restrictions has no other purpose at all than to grace (others) in the course of the remaining mode of conduct (of his life after liberation)."

**And:** "He graces those whose consciousness is pure and are the objects of grace (directly), without (resorting to) any expedient. He graces them by effecting his same (state in them), by transmitting his own consciousness (to them) by the mere sight (of his presence) (darśana), free of assessment (anusaṁdhāna)."

**Why it's interesting:** After the radical individualism of the Anupāya teaching (no means, no practice), this passage re-establishes social ethics on a new foundation. The liberated soul has no personal purpose left — all striving is done — but continues to act solely for the benefit of others. And the action is not intervention but transmission: the liberated soul transmits his own state of consciousness "by the mere sight of his presence." This is the theory of darśana as a real energetic transmission — not symbolic but causal. The teacher's presence IS the teaching; the state of freedom communicates itself without words or methods.

**Inexternalist reading:** The transmission of consciousness "by mere sight" is the extrinsic (Σᵢ) correlate of the intrinsic (Dᵢ) recognition that there is no real separation between subjects. The liberated soul does not "help" others; he recognizes that they are already what he is, and that recognition, when perceived, catalyzes the same recognition in them. The "grace" is not an additional force but the removal of the illusion of separation.
