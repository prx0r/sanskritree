# Śivasūtra — C1 (plain-English interpretations)

*The commentary pass: what each verse actually says, broken down for the uninitiated — sharp, not vague; simple, not simplified. Modeled on Dyczkowski's Exposition register. Not comparative (for now).*

## 1.1 — caitanyam ātmā
**What it says:** This is the whole system in two words. You are not a body that happens to be conscious; you are consciousness itself — the awareness in which everything appears. And it's not a distant cosmic Self: it's one's *own* nature, the most intimate thing there is. The tradition's redefinition: the "self" of the old schools is not a substance inside you — it is the self-experiencing awareness you already are. Everything else in this text is a working-out of this one claim.
**Why it matters:** if this is true, then nothing needs to be *added* to you — only uncovered. That is why the text can promise liberation through knowledge alone.

## 1.2 — jñānaṃ bandhaḥ
**What it says:** The thing we normally call knowing — the chatter of "I" and "this is mine" — is not a path out of bondage; it *is* the bondage. What binds is not ignorance as a lack, but the possessive cognition itself: the world divided into me and mine. The sūtra's shock is deliberate: the first verse said you are consciousness; this one says the ordinary way of using consciousness is the trap.

## 1.3 — yonivargaḥ kalāśarīram
**What it says:** The machinery of the trap. Four powers — Ambā (the Mother), Jyeṣṭhā (the Eldest), Raudrī (the Fierce), Vāmā (the Left) — are the "sources" from which everything flows; and their body is the alphabet, the letters from A to Kṣ. Why the alphabet? Because speech is the medium of the possessive cognition of 1.2: language saturates thought, and the letters are the energies that do the saturating. The sources are the goddesses; their body is language itself.

## 1.7 — jāgratsvapnasuṣuptabhede turyābhogasaṃvit
**What it says:** The "Fourth" is not a fourth state you reach after waking, dreaming, and deep sleep — it is the consciousness that is present *in* their distinction. You move through the three states all day, every night; what stays constant is the awareness that witnesses them. That witnessing awareness — which savors all the states without being any of them — is the real subject. The verse says it is found in the division itself, not after it.

## 1.10 — aviveko māyāsauṣuptam
**What it says:** When you cannot tell things apart — when discernment goes off — that is not merely ignorance; that is māyā itself asleep, and you asleep in it. The image is precise: deep sleep is not nothing; it is the state where distinctions dissolve. The claim: the ordinary, undiscriminating mind is already in that sleep, and does not know it. Discernment (viveka) is the awakening.

## 1.13 — icchāśaktir umā kumārī
**What it says:** The supreme power is will — and the goddess is its face. The "maiden" (kumārī) is the will *before* it has given birth to anything: the unspent, undivided wanting that precedes every particular desire. The tradition's gloss gives the etymology: she is called kumārī because she kills (mārayati) māyā — the will that has not yet descended into the world's division is itself the destroyer of the trap.

## 3.19 — kavargādiṣu māheśvaryādyāḥ paśumātaraḥ
**What it says:** The letters of the alphabet, grouped into their classes (ka, ca, ṭa, ta, pa...), are the "mothers" — and they govern whoever is bound. Why mothers? Because they give birth to the speech-world in which the bound one lives: his thoughts, his desires, his sense of self are all made of language, and the language is made of these letters. For the bound, the mothers bind; for the Lord, the same mothers liberate. Same letters, two functions — the difference is who is using them.

## 3.40 — abhilāṣādbahirgatiḥ saṃvāhyasya
**What it says:** The bound one is "conveyed" — carried along, like freight — by his own craving. Desire is the engine that drives the body-instruments outward, into the world of objects. The image: you do not walk outward on your own; your longing carries you, and you go where it drives. The sūtra's bleak precision: the going-out is not a choice, it is a conveyance.

## 3.44 — nāsikāntarmadhyasaṃyamāt kim atra...
**What it says:** A practical question turned rhetorical: if you master the breath at the single midpoint within the nose — the heart's center — what do you need the whole apparatus of left and right channels, the whole suṣumnā-theology, for? The verse's answer: nothing. The middle suffices. This is the anti-technique principle: the direct path needs no machinery.
