# Kulānanda + Unmattabhairavahṛdaya — C1 (plain-English interpretations)

*The commentary pass. What the verses actually say — plain, sharp.*

# I. Kulānanda

## vv. 1–4 — the question and the checklist
**What it says:** The goddess asks for "the city-fracture" — the supreme practice — and the question itself is the map: she asks for the noose-thrust, the piercing, the fumigation, the trembling, the sky-going, the equal-taste, the end of age and gray-hair. Read plainly: this is the bundle's practice-list, the same siddhis as the KJN — the "city-fracture" (purabheda) is the practice that breaks open the body-city to reveal what is inside it. The question names the whole curriculum in one verse.

## vv. 6–7 — the flame in the lotus
**What it says:** The first practice: the sixty-four-petaled lotus in the head, and the vow-holder fixing on the lamp-flames there — "by the mere fixing of that, the supreme siddhi arises." Plainly: attention itself is the practice. Not movement, not ritual — the steady gaze of the mind on the flame, until the divine sight opens and he "sees within the whole body." The seeing is the siddhi.

## v. 10 — the entered possession
**What it says:** "By knowing this alone he surely enters the possession." The possession (āveśa) is not something the practitioner makes happen — it is a state he *enters*, like walking into a room. The KJN says the same: the yogī "goes to samādhi, with the marks of the possession." The grammar matters: the siddhi is not manufactured; it is arrived at. Knowing is the door; the possession is the room.

## vv. 25–27 — the amṛta and the gray-hair
**What it says:** The nectar-practice: the thirty-two-petaled lotus in the head, the nectar with its sixteen kalās, contemplated until it floods — and the claim: "he stops the gray-hair within a single month." Plainly: the text's promise is literal — the amṛta-practice is the body's rejuvenation — but the logic is the death-conqueror's: the false self's aging is undone by re-identifying with the immortal. The gray-hair is the symptom; the identification is the cure.

## v. 55 — liberation through the union
**What it says:** The goddess asks for the knowledge that is liberation *through the union* — the śakti-union, the Kaula's central rite — and the answer is the equal-taste: the Lord and the devotee as one taste. The union is not a means to the state; it IS the state, tasted. The lord-servant pair dissolves into the samarasa — one flavor, two names.

# II. Unmattabhairavahṛdaya

## The kīlaka-section — the nailing
**What it says:** The "nailing" (kīlaka) is the mantra-śāstra's term for fixing a power in place: the deity is nailed into the mantra so it cannot slip. The text gives the nailings their genealogy — the Vedic mantras, the seers (Vasiṣṭha, Viśvāmitra, Bhāradvāja), the sun and moon. Plainly: the whole apparatus exists so that the power, once raised, stays. The inverse of the Akulavīra's "no restraint" — here the technique is everything; in the kula's other text, it is negated. The bundle holds both: the kula's machinery and the akula's demolition of it.

## S9–S11 — the twelve names
**What it says:** The theophany-catalog: Unmatta (the Mad One), Bhairava (the Terrible), Bhīmarūpā (the Fierce-Formed), the Lord of the Yoginīs, the Lord of the Mothers, Nirlajja (the Shameless), Paramānanda (the Supreme Bliss), the Eight Bhairavas, the Lord of the Hosts, the Ḍākinī-Slayer, Ghorā (the Fierce), Sātvikī (the Serene). The names are the nyāsa's objects — each placed on a limb — and together they are the portrait of the tradition's god: the mad, shameless, blissful, fierce, and serene Lord of Vārāṇasī. The antinomian and the serene are the same face.

## H50 — the heart-mantra
**What it says:** "Whoever recites the Mad-Lord's heart with all its limbs, day by day — he gains the desired siddhi, free of the manifold afflictions." And the close of the text: the seeker of knowledge gains knowledge, of wealth gains wealth, of liberation gains liberation. The heart-mantra gives each what each seeks — the text's own formula for grace. The copyist's colophon — the "servant of Unmattabhairava," dated 1915 — is the text's last voice: a modern scribe, still copying the mad Lord's heart.
