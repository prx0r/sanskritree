# Kulānanda + Unmattabhairavahṛdaya — C1 (plain-English interpretations)

# I. Kulānanda

## vv. 1–4 — the checklist
The goddess asks for "the city-fracture" — the supreme practice — and the question names the curriculum: the noose-thrust, the piercing, the fumigation, the trembling, the sky-going, the equal-taste, the end of age and gray-hair. The purabheda is the practice that breaks open the body-city to reveal what is inside it.

## vv. 6–7 — the flame in the lotus
The sixty-four-petaled lotus in the head; the vow-holder fixing on the lamp-flames there — "by the mere fixing of that, the supreme siddhi arises." Attention itself is the practice. Not movement, not ritual — the steady gaze of the mind on the flame, until the divine sight opens and he "sees within the whole body." The seeing is the siddhi.

## v. 10 — the entered possession
"By knowing this alone he surely enters the possession." The possession (āveśa) is not manufactured; it is arrived at — a room you walk into. The KJN says the same: the yogī "goes to samādhi, with the marks of the possession." Knowing is the door; the possession is the room.

## vv. 25–27 — the amṛta and the gray-hair
The thirty-two-petaled lotus in the head; the nectar with its sixteen kalās, contemplated until it floods — and the claim: "he stops the gray-hair within a single month." The promise is literal: the amṛta-practice is the body's rejuvenation. The logic is the death-conqueror's: the false self's aging is undone by re-identifying with the immortal. The gray-hair is the symptom; the identification is the cure.

## v. 55 — liberation through the union
The goddess asks for the knowledge that is liberation through the union — the śakti-union, the Kaula's central rite. The answer is the equal-taste: the Lord and the devotee as one taste. The union is not a means to the state; it IS the state, tasted. One flavor, two names.

# II. Unmattabhairavahṛdaya

## The kīlaka-section — the nailing
The nailing (kīlaka) fixes a power in place: the deity nailed into the mantra so it cannot slip. The text gives the nailings their genealogy — the Vedic mantras, the seers, the sun and moon. The whole apparatus exists so that the power, once raised, stays. The inverse of the Akulavīra's "no restraint": here the technique is everything; in the kula's other text, it is negated. The bundle holds both — the machinery and its demolition.

## S9–S11 — the twelve names
The Mad One, the Terrible, the Fierce-Formed, the Lord of the Yoginīs, the Lord of the Mothers, the Shameless, the Supreme Bliss, the Eight Bhairavas, the Lord of the Hosts, the Ḍākinī-Slayer, the Fierce, the Serene. The names, placed on the limbs in the nyāsa, are the portrait of the tradition's god: the mad, shameless, blissful, fierce, and serene Lord of Vārāṇasī. The antinomian and the serene are the same face.

## H50 — the heart-mantra
"Whoever recites the Mad-Lord's heart with all its limbs, day by day — he gains the desired siddhi, free of the manifold afflictions." The seeker of knowledge gains knowledge, of wealth gains wealth, of liberation gains liberation. The heart-mantra gives each what each seeks. The copyist's colophon — the "servant of Unmattabhairava," dated 1915 — is the text's last voice: a modern scribe, still copying the mad Lord's heart.
