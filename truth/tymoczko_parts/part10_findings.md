# Part 10 Findings — Chapter 9 (Scales in Twentieth-Century Music)
Lines 13596-14880

## Core Idea: 20th-century composers used scales (diatonic, octatonic, whole-tone, pentatonic) to counteract the trend toward saturated chromaticism, creating new tonal languages that combined chromatic voice-leading with limited macroharmony.

## Rasa-Relevant Findings

### Finding 1: Scale-First vs Chord-First Composition
Two basic approaches: (1) choose a scale, then find chords within it; (2) choose a chord, then find a scale that contains it. These are dual procedures that explore the same constraint space from opposite directions.

This is analogous to two paths to recognition: (1) stabilize the self-model (scale), then reorganize content (chords) within it; (2) reorganize content directly, allowing the self-model to shift naturally. The Vijñānabhairava offers both approaches.

### Finding 2: The Octatonic Scale as a Symmetry Structure
The octatonic scale (alternating whole-half steps) has maximal symmetry among 8-note scales. Its structure enables efficient voice leading between diminished, dominant, and half-diminished chords. Composers from Rimsky-Korsakov to Stravinsky to Coltrane exploited this.

The octatonic scale is interesting for rasa because it's a LIMIT CASE — maximizing symmetry while maintaining useful structure. It's the scale closest to "pure symmetry" that still allows functional harmony. In QRI terms, it might be near the "maximally symmetric but still organized" point on the valence curve.

### Finding 3: The Subset Technique
20th-century composers often used subsets of symmetrical scales (e.g., 4 notes from the octatonic) to create harmonic ambiguity — the subset could belong to multiple parent scales, allowing smooth transitions.

This is another model for the pivot state: being in a region of chord space that belongs to multiple larger organizations simultaneously. The "not knowing which key you're in" state is the auditory analogue of the Vijñānabhairava's junction.

### Finding 4: Minimalism as Continuous Voice Leading Over Static Harmony
Steve Reich and Philip Glass create motion within a stable harmonic field by using efficient voice leading between closely related chords while the macroharmony changes slowly or not at all. The effect is "motion within stasis."

This is the auditory analogue of the Vijñānabhairava's breath and body practices: maintaining a stable field (macroharmony = fixed scale/number of notes) while content (individual chords/notes) moves efficiently. The "burbling within stasis" of Ligeti/Lutosławski cluster music (Ch. 3) is another example. Both are auditory models of how Mᵢ can be stable while Cᵢ changes.
