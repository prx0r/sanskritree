# Akulavīratantra — C1 (plain-English interpretations)

*The commentary pass. What the verses actually say — plain, sharp, no assumed technical prowess.*

## The maṅgala — the homage
**What it says:** The text opens by bowing to the lineage's founder — and the homage is a compressed statement of the whole teaching. Mīnanātha ("the Fish-Lord") is described as the innate bliss born from his own body: the teaching did not come from outside, it IS his own nature. He is "the support of all" — the ground everything stands on — and he is released from the impurity of māyā. In one epithet-chain: the teacher, the teaching, and the goal are the same reality.

## v. 1 — the secret-of-secrets
**What it says:** "More secret than secret, the secret of secrets" — the formula is the tradition's standard claim, and it means something precise: this teaching is not hidden because it is exclusive, but because it cannot be *said* — it is the lineage of the siddhas' own reality, which is only real when it is one's own. A secret you can be told is not this secret.

## vv. 5–6 — the dharma-chasers
**What it says:** The people who run after every teaching — every dharma, every scripture, every path — never find the innate bliss. Why? Because they are looking *outward* at teachings, and the innate is what they already are. The "net of scriptures" is the image: the more they catch, the more they are caught. The partless guru is not another teacher to find; it is the state itself.

## vv. 7–10 — the dismissal of the schools
**What it says:** Here the text sweeps the whole map of Indian philosophy — the logicians, the Buddhists, the Jainas, Mīmāṃsā, the left and right tantric orders, the Purāṇas — and dismisses them in a double move: their knowledge is *indirect* (they talk about reality) and *constructed* (their attainments are built of thoughts), and what is constructed and indirect does not release anyone. The sharpest line: their attainment is itself pierced by the bonds of sin — the cure is part of the disease.

## v. 12 — the mind stills
**What it says:** The soteriological pivot, in one image: when the Akulavīra is *seen*, the mind goes still. Not disciplined, not trained, not emptied — still. The seeing does it. This is the anti-technique thesis: you do not do anything to the mind; you see what is real, and the stillness follows on its own. This single verse is the whole path.

## v. 23 — the counting negated
**What it says:** The text negates even the arithmetic of reality — the "twenty-formed" scheme of principles, the counting itself — and the "person" who would be counted. Where the Akulavīra is, there is no scheme and no schemer. The point is not a doctrine about what the cosmos is made of; it is that the *activity* of schematizing has ceased.

## v. 39 — the mark-less bound
**What it says:** In the great list of negations (non-dual, unmoving, tranquil, unagitated, construct-free) there is one line that startles: "and the bound, the mark-less." Even the bound state is included in the negation — the paśu too is dissolved. Nothing is excluded from the Akulavīra; that is what "the support of all" means.

## v. 56 — the two paths
**What it says:** The Kaula path has two limbs: the *made* (kṛtaka) — the observances, the rites, the graded practices — and the *innate* (sahaja), which is the equal-taste, the spontaneous. The whole text is a polemic for the second against the first: not against the lineage, but against the lineage's scaffolding. The observances are real and they bind; the innate is real and it releases.

## v. 65 — the title-word
**What it says:** The verse's "when the akula-hero is reached, everything dissolves" is the text's whole claim in miniature — and the title-word itself (akula-vīra: the "kula-less hero") is split across the line. The hero beyond the kula — beyond the lineage's own machinery — is the state in which all dharmas, all supports, all the kula's furniture dissolve. As ghee in curd: the essence was always there, latent, waiting to be released.

## v. 78 — no one, nothing
**What it says:** The negative confession: "I am no one; nothing is mine; no one is bound; no one binds." Not a renunciant's modesty — a claim about reality. If the self is the stainless, then there is no one to be bound and no binder; the whole apparatus of bondage is dissolved because its participants are dissolved. This is the most radically nondual sentence in the text.

## v. 99 — the essence of the essence
**What it says:** The fools do not know "the more-essential-than-the-essential" — the text's name for the supreme teaching, the same superlative used across this corpus (in the Kubjikā, in the KJN). Three texts, one word: the tradition's shared name for what it is all pointing at. "Comparable only to self-experience" — you cannot be told what it is; you can only taste it, and the tasting is knowing.

## v. 130 — the inclusivism
**What it says:** Here the ecumenical moment: whoever stands in the equal-taste IS the whole pantheon — Brahmā, Hari, Rudra, Śiva, the moon, the sun, Skanda, Indra, even the Arhat and the Buddha. One reality, all the names. And yet verses 142–145 dismissed the schools — the gods are all one, but the doctrines are all vain. The unity is in the state, not in the systems.

## v. 152 — the state of difference
**What it says:** The close introduces "the state of difference" — the dualistic state, the divided world — and the text's final arc: the Akulavīra is not merely the negation of the world; it is what the world's difference is dissolved into. The last lines promise the outcome: no age, no death, no grief — "the remover of all diseases," the stainless, everywhere, always.
