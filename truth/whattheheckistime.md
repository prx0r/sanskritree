Science does **not** currently support one single claim called “time.”

It distinguishes at least four different structures:

[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
t_{\mathrm{phys}} &: \text{ordering and duration in physical theory};\
t_{\mathrm{therm}} &: \text{the irreversible arrow associated with entropy};\

\end{aligned}
}
]

The Śaiva claim that *kāla* is a kañcuka is scientifically plausible only if interpreted as:

[
\boxed{
\textit{kāla}
=============

\text{finite access to temporal relations from within one}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{embodied, memory-bearing, action-oriented aperture}.
}

Science does **not** presently support the stronger claim:

[
\boxed{
\text{physical time itself is created by individual consciousness}.

The most defensible synthesis is:

[
\boxed{

# Part I — What physics actually establishes

## 1. Physics has no consensus that time “flows”

Most successful physical theories represent temporal relations mathematically but do not contain a further variable corresponding to the felt passage of time.

In relativity, events form a spacetime structure. The equations describe:

* temporal ordering;
* durations along worldlines;
* causal relations;
* differences between observers’ measurements.

They do not contain an objectively moving present.

This motivates eternalist or “block universe” interpretations, but physical formalism alone does not force one unique metaphysics of time. The experience of flow therefore remains a separate explanatory problem even if physical time is fundamental. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][1])

The first decisive distinction is:

[
\boxed{
\text{temporal order}
\neq
\text{temporal passage}.
}
]

A theory may contain:

[
e_1<e_2<e_3
]

without containing:

[
\text{NOW moves from }e_1\text{ to }e_2.
]

## 2. Fundamental versus emergent time is not one dispute

“Time is emergent” can mean several different things.

### A. Geometry is emergent

Spacetime may emerge from a more fundamental quantum, informational, or causal structure.

### B. The arrow is emergent

The laws may be approximately time-symmetric while thermodynamic irreversibility emerges from boundary conditions and statistical asymmetry.

### C. Clock time is relational

Time may be represented through correlations among changing subsystems rather than as an independent substance.

### D. Phenomenal flow is constructed

The feeling that time passes may arise through memory, anticipation, causal modelling, and neural integration.

These are logically independent:

[
\text{emergent spacetime}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{constructed phenomenal flow},
]

and:

[
\text{fundamental physical time}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{directly perceived flow}.
]

For the aperture project, D is the secure target. A–C remain unsettled physics.

# 3. Gisin and the Bekenstein-bound argument

The claim “the Bekenstein bound proves that time is real” is too strong.

Gisin’s argument is instead:

1. standard deterministic classical physics represents initial conditions using mathematical real numbers;
2. most real numbers contain infinitely many informational digits;
3. finite physical regions should not contain infinite physically instantiated information;
4. therefore infinitely precise real-number initial conditions should not be treated as physically real;
5. future digits can become determinate as physical time unfolds;
6. time may therefore be **creative**, producing genuinely new information rather than merely revealing an already complete block.

Gisin explicitly invokes the idea that finite space cannot contain infinite information, but the step from information bounds to creative temporal becoming is philosophical and model-dependent—not a theorem derived directly from the Bekenstein bound. ([arXiv][2])

The Bekenstein bound itself concerns limits on entropy or information associated with finite energy and spatial regions. Its precise formulation is technically delicate, and some naïve versions have counterexamples. ([arXiv][3])

Therefore:

[
\boxed{
\text{Bekenstein-type finitude}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{objective temporal passage}.
}
]

The more defensible implication is:

[
\boxed{
\text{finite information weakens the assumption that every}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{future physical detail is already encoded in infinitely}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{precise initial conditions.}
}
]

## Why Gisin matters for svātantrya

Gisin gives us a possible scientific ontology in which novelty is not merely epistemic ignorance.

If physical quantities are finitely specified, then later events may add genuinely new information:

[
I(t+\Delta t)>I(t).
]

This would support **creative time**.

But it would not yet establish:

[
\text{new information}
======================

\text{free agency}.
]

Randomness creates novelty without agency.

Thus:

[
\boxed{
\text{indeterminism is necessary for some conceptions of}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{open futurity, but insufficient for freedom.}
}
]

# 4. Computational irreducibility and temporal becoming

Wolfram’s computational irreducibility offers another way to understand the openness of the future.

For many systems, no general shortcut predicts the state after (n) steps faster than effectively running the system through those steps. This means a future can be determined by a rule while still being inaccessible to bounded observers without undergoing the intervening process. ([Writings by Stephen Wolfram][4])

Formally:

[
x_{t+n}=F^n(x_t)
]

but:

[
\operatorname{Compute}(x_{t+n})
\approx
\operatorname{Run}(F,n).
]

This provides:

[
\boxed{
\text{epistemic openness produced by irreducible process}.
}
]

It does not provide:

[
\boxed{
\text{ontological indeterminacy}.
}
]

The system may remain deterministic.

## Importance for the aperture

A finite aperture cannot skip directly to its own future state. It must live through its updating process:

[
O_i(t)
\rightarrow
O_i(t+\Delta t)
\rightarrow
O_i(t+2\Delta t).
]

This gives an operational reason why the future is open **for the agent**:

[
\boxed{
\text{the aperture cannot fully contain a compressed}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{representation of its own irreducible future.}
}
]

That is a strong scientific analogue of *kāla* as restriction:

[
\textit{kāla}
\sim
\text{bounded sequential access to an irreducible trajectory}.
]

# Part II — Neuroscience of lived time

## 5. There is no single neural clock

Brains contain many interacting timescales rather than one central timekeeper.

Different neural circuits integrate information over different durations. Sensory regions tend to operate over shorter windows, while association and higher-order regions retain and integrate information over longer intervals. Reviews of intrinsic neural timescales find hierarchical variation across cortical networks and species. ([Nature][5])

Relevant scales include:

* milliseconds for coincidence and sensory order;
* tens to hundreds of milliseconds for feature binding;
* seconds for action, speech, and working memory;
* minutes and hours for episodic context;
* years for autobiographical identity.

Therefore:

[
\boxed{
\tau_i
\neq
\text{output of one clock}.
}
]

It is better represented as a hierarchy:

[
\tau_i=
\left\langle
\tau_i^{\mathrm{sens}},
\tau_i^{\mathrm{action}},
\tau_i^{\mathrm{context}},
\tau_i^{\mathrm{self}}
\right\rangle.
]

## 6. The specious present

We do not experience a durationless mathematical instant.

The experienced present contains change, movement, rhythm, persistence, and succession. Philosophers call this temporally extended present the **specious present**. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][1])

A melody demonstrates the issue.

If only the current sound existed phenomenally, there would be no melody:

[
n_1,\quad n_2,\quad n_3
]

would occur as isolated tones.

To hear:

[
n_1\rightarrow n_2\rightarrow n_3
]

as one phrase, the current field must contain some structured retention of what has just occurred.

A minimal model is:

[
P_t
===

x_t+\alpha x_{t-1}+\alpha^2x_{t-2}+\cdots,
\qquad
0<\alpha<1.
]

The present is therefore not a point but a weighted temporal field.

Experiments support integration windows in which later input can affect how earlier stimuli are consciously organized. Some visual paradigms show integration over hundreds of milliseconds, while other work finds discrete or quasi-discrete windows for feature integration. ([Nature][6])

This does not prove that consciousness itself is discrete. It establishes that conscious content depends on temporally extended neural integration.

# 7. Three models of temporal consciousness

## Snapshot model

Experience consists of discrete static moments:

[
D_1,D_2,D_3,\ldots
]

Motion and succession are inferred from differences among snapshots.

Problem: a static snapshot seems unable to contain experienced change.

## Retentional model

A present experience contains representations or traces of immediately past phases:

[
D_t=
\langle
x_t,
R(x_{t-1}),
R(x_{t-2})
\rangle.
]

This is close to Husserlian retention and QRI’s pseudo-time arrow.

## Extensional model

Experience itself extends through physical time:

[
D_{[t_1,t_2]}
]

and contains temporally ordered phenomenal parts.

The experienced succession is not represented inside an instant; it is constituted by succession within an extended experience. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][7])

Science has not conclusively selected one model. Neural integration is compatible with both retentional and extensional interpretations.

# 8. QRI’s pseudo-time arrow

QRI proposes that each phenomenal “slice” contains decaying remnants of prior slices. The asymmetrical pattern of these remnants creates an implicit causal direction within experience. ([Qualia Research Institute][8])

In simplified form:

[
D_t
===

G
\left(
x_t,
q x_{t-1},
q^2x_{t-2},
\ldots
\right),
\qquad
0<q<1.
]

Because present experience contains structured evidence of preceding states but not equivalent traces of future states, it has an internal arrow:

[
\text{past}\rightarrow\text{present}.
]

## What is valuable

The proposal correctly separates:

[
t_{\mathrm{phys}}
]

from:

[
\tau_i.
]

It also provides candidate explanations for:

* motion;
* melody;
* temporal continuity;
* altered duration;
* loops;
* timelessness.

## What is speculative

QRI assumes:

* discrete phenomenal slices;
* qualia formalism;
* a graph model of local binding;
* particular decay functions;
* a structural identity between causal graph and felt temporal flow.

These are not empirically established. QRI itself presents the pseudo-time arrow as a proposal rather than a settled neuroscientific model. ([Qualia Research Institute][8])

## Stronger revised version

We can retain the general principle without QRI’s ontology:

[
\boxed{
\text{phenomenal direction arises from asymmetric integration}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{of retained past information and anticipated future states}.
}
]

This gives:

[
\tau_i
======

F
\left(
R_i^{-},
P_i^{+},
x_i^{0}
\right),
]

where:

* (R_i^{-}): retention of past states;
* (x_i^0): current content;
* (P_i^{+}): anticipation of possible futures.

QRI focuses mainly on (R_i^{-}). Agency requires adding (P_i^{+}).

# 9. Temporal context and memory

Memory science shows that the brain encodes events within slowly changing temporal contexts. Reinstating an event can reinstate aspects of the context in which it occurred, helping the system recover order, contiguity, and episode structure. ([PubMed][9])

Let:

[
c_t
]

be temporal context.

Encoding:

[
e_t\leftrightarrow c_t.
]

Retrieval:

[
e_t\rightarrow\hat c_t.
]

Pastness arises partly because:

[
\hat c_t\neq c_{\mathrm{now}},
]

while both are represented as belonging to the same trajectory.

Evidence from medial temporal lobe amnesia suggests that temporal-contiguity deficits can be explained by impaired recovery of prior temporal context. ([PubMed][10])

Thus:

[
\boxed{
\text{the past is not merely weak present content;}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{it is content indexed to a noncurrent context}.
}
]

This maps strongly onto Utpaladeva:

[
\text{“that”}
=============

\text{presently manifest content marked as previously perceived}.
]

No single mechanism is sufficient.

The best current construction combines:

[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
R_i &: \text{retention of the immediate past};\
A_i &: \text{anticipation of possible futures};\
E_i &: \text{event segmentation};\

C_i &: \text{causal modelling}.
\end{aligned}

]

The experienced present is asymmetrical:

* the past is retained but unavailable for intervention;
* the future is anticipated but not remembered;
* the present is where counterfactual action remains possible.

Therefore flow may be represented as:

[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Flow}_i
=====================

\text{movement of the action boundary through a}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{retained-and-anticipated world model}.
}
]

This is more complete than saying flow comes from memory alone.

## Why only the present is experienced

Strictly, we do experience represented past and future content.

But only present processing is causally active as experience now:

[
D_i(t)
======

\text{current manifestation of}
\left[
\text{past traces}
+
\text{current input}
+
\text{future models}
\right].

The past itself is not present again. A present reconstruction is.

Similarly, the future is present as possibility, not actuality.

Thus:

[
\boxed{

}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{names the current operation of the aperture.}

That is nearly analytic, but it becomes explanatory once the aperture’s physical update process is specified.

The agent’s current actions can influence:

[
x_{t+\Delta t},

[
x_{t-\Delta t}.

The past constrains the current state through records and causal effects:

[
x_{t-\Delta t}\rightarrow x_t.

The future is represented as a branching policy space:

[
\Pi_t=
{\pi_1,\pi_2,\ldots,\pi_n}.

Agency selects among these branches.

Therefore:

[
\boxed{
\text{pastness}
===============

\text{historically constraining but no longer intervention-open},

while:

[
\boxed{
\text{futurity}
===============

\text{counterfactually represented and intervention-sensitive}.
}
]

This remains true whether the underlying universe is deterministic or indeterministic.

The future may be physically fixed in a block universe but still be computationally inaccessible and decision-relevant to the agent.

# Part III — Kāla and Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī

## 13. Kāla as a kañcuka

In the Śaiva hierarchy, *kāla* limits unrestricted consciousness to sequential access.

The unrestricted form is not merely endless clock duration. It is the capacity to manifest temporal relations without being confined to one moving position.

Finite consciousness instead has:

[
\operatorname{Access}_i(t)
]

such that direct manifest access is localized:

[
\operatorname{Access}*i(t*{\mathrm{now}})
\gg
\operatorname{Access}*i(t*{\mathrm{past/future}}).
]

Past and future are available only through:

* memory;
* anticipation;
* inference;
* imagination.

This yields a precise reconstruction:

[
\boxed{
\textit{kāla}
=============

\text{restriction of simultaneous temporal access into}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{sequential, memory-dependent, action-oriented disclosure}.
}
]

This is scientifically respectable.

Brains and organisms do not access an entire trajectory at once. They operate through bounded integration windows, incomplete memory, and uncertain prediction.

## 14. Is kāla the production of time or restriction within time?

There are two interpretations.

### Weak interpretation

Physical temporal structure exists independently, while kāla describes the finite agent’s restricted access to it.

[
t_{\mathrm{phys}}
\rightarrow
\operatorname{RestrictedAccess}_i
\rightarrow
\tau_i.
]

This is most compatible with science.

### Strong interpretation

Universal consciousness is timeless or trans-temporal, and kāla creates temporal order itself:

[
C_\infty
\xrightarrow{\textit{kāla}}
t+\tau_i.
]

Science does not currently establish this.

The deepdive should therefore retain:

[
\boxed{
\text{scientific kāla}
======================

\text{bounded temporal access},
}
]

while marking:

[
\boxed{
\text{ontological creation of physical time by consciousness}
}
]

as the full metaphysical Śaiva extension.

# 15. Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī and time-consumption

Wenta’s history of the twelve Kālīs gives a precise account of *kālagrāsa*, the consumption of time.

In later Kālīkrama formulations, temporal differentiation is dissolved into the final goddess or ultimate consciousness. Abhinavagupta reinterprets the twelvefold cycle as a phenomenology of cognition, and Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī is associated with the subjectivity into which projected temporal differentiations are withdrawn. ([Springer][12])

The crucial claim is not:

[
\text{a goddess physically destroys cosmic time}.
]

It is:

[
\boxed{
\text{the sequence of arising, persistence, and dissolution}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{of cognitions is recognized as occurring within—and}
}
]

[
\boxed{
\text{returning to—one self-manifest awareness}.
}

## Scientific reconstruction

Ordinary lived time requires persistent differentiation among:

[
\text{retained past},
\quad

\quad
\text{anticipated future}.

If practices weaken these contrasts:

[
\Delta

“Time consumption” can be reconstructed phenomenologically as:

[
\boxed{
\operatorname{Kālagrāsa}_i

\text{withdrawal of temporal indexing from manifest content}.
}
]

The content may remain present, but it no longer appears strongly ordered as:

[
\text{before}\rightarrow\text{now}\rightarrow\text{after}.
]

This could produce reports of:

* timelessness;
* an eternal present;
* cessation of succession;
* non-separation of arising and dissolution.

## What the Kālīkrama adds

It makes time an activity of differentiation:

[

Agency cannot be reduced to motion, goal-pursuit, complexity, unpredictability, or policy selection.

The best scientific definition is:

[
\boxed{
\textbf{Agency is the graded capacity of a persisting system}
}

[
\boxed{

[
\boxed{

[
\boxed{
\textbf{continued organization.}

The user’s emphasis on endogenous goal revision is correct, but it should not be the criterion for **minimal** agency.

A worm or cell can be an agent without explicitly revising abstract values. Goal revision is a higher grade of agency.

The clean hierarchy is:

[
\boxed{

\rightarrow
\text{flexible goal-pursuit}

\text{goal learning}
\rightarrow
\text{goal revision}
\rightarrow
\text{self-revision}.
