## Candidate Λ_Φ-1: Prakāśa-Localization

**text:** `/root/projects/tantraloka/texts/spandakarika-dyczkowski.txt` lines 4004-4017
**ref:** Spandakārikā commentary (Pratyabhijñā theory of perception)
**tradition:** Kashmir Śaivism (Pratyabhijñā)

> "According to this theory, objects appear in so far as they possess the quality of being manifest. Although it belongs to them rather than the subject who perceives them, they do not possess it inherently (i.e., necessarily or permanently) — otherwise they would be manifest to everybody at all times. They must acquire it, and they do so by being bathed with the radiance of the light of consciousness that shines within the perceiver as his essential conscious nature."

claims:
  weak: "Manifestness is not an inherent property of objects. If it were, all objects would be manifest to all subjects at all times. Manifestness is conferred by the perceiver's own light of consciousness."
  strong: "Therefore phenomenal domains are private because each perceiver has their OWN light of consciousness (prakāśa). Manifestness is ontologically dependent on the individual's consciousness, not on any shared objective property."

audit:
  establishes: |
    A direct argument for domain privacy. The text identifies the key problem: if manifestness were inherent in objects, there would be no privacy (everyone would see everything at all times). Since privacy IS observed, manifestness must depend on the perceiver. This is a genuine explanation — it identifies WHAT makes a domain private (the perceiver's own light of consciousness).
    
    The same argument appears in the Tantrāloka Vol 6 perceptibility debate (Chapter 10, lines 21100-21160) where Abhinavagupta refutes the Mīmāṃsaka view that manifestness (prakaṭatā) is a quality of objects: "if [manifested-ness] were a quality of an entity, then it would be perceivable to everybody" (lines 21139-40). The counter-argument is that if manifestness belongs to the object, "an entity would be manifestly apparent in all respects to everybody without distinction. Thus, the undesired consequence would be that everybody would be omniscient."
    
    The Mālinīvijayavārtika paraphrase (Vol 6 lines 21249-54) makes it even clearer: "If we say that objects appear variously because they are pervaded by light, then the world would be manifest (viśvaprakāśa) to everybody all the time. But if there is light which is called knowledge (i.e., light belonging to the subject), the object becomes manifest."
  missing_premise: |
    The explanation identifies the LOCUS of manifestness (the perceiver's consciousness) but doesn't fully explain what INDIVIDUATES one perceiver's light from another's. If consciousness is ultimately one (as the Pratyabhijñā holds), why is there more than one center of manifestness? The text essentially describes privacy as a brute fact of how manifestness works, rather than deriving it from a deeper principle.
    
    Also: the "otherwise they would be manifest to everybody" argument works as a reductio against objective manifestness, but it still leaves open the question: what CAUSES one perceiver's consciousness to only manifest some objects and not all? The text says objects "must acquire" manifestness by being "bathed" in the perceiver's light — but why does the bath not overflow to other perceivers?
  countermodel: |
    Nyāya plural selves: each self has its own consciousness as a quality. Privacy is simply a fact about the nature of selves — consciousness belongs to each self individually, not as a shared resource. This is equally explanatory without the metaphysical apparatus of universal consciousness contracting into individuals.

verdict:
  status: "strong_candidate_explanatory_gap_in_individuation"
  frontier: "What individuates one center of prakāśa from another? If prakaśa is ultimately one (the Pratyabhijñā position), domain privacy requires an additional principle of partition. If prakāśa is inherently multiple (Nyāya position), privacy is built into the ontology but the 'shared structure without shared identity' problem remains."

## Candidate Λ_Φ-2: Mutual Exclusion of Egos

**text:** `/root/projects/tantraloka/texts/spandakarika-dyczkowski.txt` lines 4370-74
**ref:** Rajānaka Rāma's commentary on Spandakārikā (with Utpaladeva)
**tradition:** Kashmir Śaivism (Spanda)

> "the individual perceiver believes himself conditioned by the countless forms of diversity related to the divided field in which he operates, namely, the egoity (ahampratiti) established on the basis of mutual exclusion between differing egos. It is egoity falsely projected onto the body which is the way in which, according to Utpaladeva also, we perceive the unfolding of the power of Maya."

claims:
  weak: "Individual phenomenal domains are structured by egoity (ahampratiti), which is established through mutual exclusion between differing egos. This ego-sense is projected onto the body."
  strong: "Therefore the privacy of phenomenal domains is a function of the mutual exclusion OPERATION between egos. Privacy is not a brute fact but the ACTIVE negation of other egos within each ego's self-constitution."

audit:
  establishes: |
    A powerful claim: phenomenal domains are not passively separate — they are ACTIVELY constituted through mutual exclusion. Each ego defines itself by negating/excluding other egos. This is not just description but a proposed MECHANISM: egoity is ESTABLISHED ON THE BASIS OF (the text: "pratiṣṭhita") mutual exclusion between differing egos.
    
    This is philosophically sophisticated: it anticipates the Hegelian/Sartrean insight that self-consciousness is constituted through recognition/negation of the other. The "divided field" (bhinna-kṣetra) is the space of operations created by this mutual exclusion.
    
    The text also specifies the BODY as the locus of projection: "egoity falsely projected onto the body." The body is the visible marker of the boundary between self and other, which makes the otherwise purely conceptual mutual exclusion phenomenologically concrete.
  missing_premise: |
    The text treats mutual exclusion as an OPERATION of egoity, not as a foundational principle. It says the ego IS established by mutual exclusion, but doesn't explain what GROUNDS the capacity for mutual exclusion. Is mutual exclusion a fundamental feature of consciousness, or is it derivative of something deeper (like māyā's power of differentiation)?
    
    Additionally, the text describes the FETTERED state (bondage) — it characterizes mutual exclusion as the condition of ignorance. In the liberated state, mutual exclusion is overcome. So mutual exclusion explains why phenomenal domains are private IN SAMṢĀRA, but not necessarily why privacy exists at all (it could be a contingent feature of the bound state that liberation removes).
    
    The text also says "egoity falsely projected onto the body" (line 4373) — this implies the mutual exclusion is a kind of error or misapprehension. If so, it's an explanation of why domains APPEAR private rather than why they ARE private.
  countermodel: |
    The Dharmakīrtian/Buddhist view: mutual exclusion (apoha/apohana) operates at the conceptual level (vikalpa) — it's how thought carves up reality, not how subjects are constituted. Egos are one more conceptual construction among others, not the fundamental ground of partition. Privacy is then a byproduct of conceptuality, not a primitive.

verdict:
  status: "strong_candidate_constitutive_not_foundational"
  frontier: "Is mutual exclusion between egos foundational or derivative? If derivative, what is it derived from? If foundational, does it persist in the liberated state (in which case privacy is permanent) or dissolve (in which case privacy is contingent)?"

## Candidate Λ_Φ-3: Kañcuka Contraction

**text:** `/root/projects/tantraloka/texts/tantraloka-vol6-dyczkowski.txt` lines 34224-35, 34270-99
**ref:** Tantrāloka 6/A1cd-42ab, Pratyabhijñāhr̥daya
**tradition:** Kashmir Śaivism (Trika)

> "Although (his) powers are (always) uncontracted, by (freely) assuming contraction, (they operate) as limited agency (kalā) and capacity to know (vidyā), attachment (rāga), time, and necessity (niyati). Here (in this condition), limited agency is the cause of this individual soul's limited capacity to act. (His) capacity to know is the cause of his limited capacity to know. Attachment is attachment to (particular) objects of sense. Time is the sequence that divides up (and conditions) phenomena whose nature is to manifest (at some particular time) and not manifest (at another). Necessity is the cause of the constraint that 'I must do this' (or) 'I must not do this'. As this group of five obscures his own nature, it is (said to be) an obscuring covering (kañcuka)."

claims:
  weak: "The five kañcukas (kalā, vidyā, rāga, kāla, niyati) are the mechanisms through which universal consciousness contracts into individual phenomenal domains. Each constrains a dimension of experience: agency, knowledge, attachment, temporality, and necessity."
  strong: "Therefore phenomenal domain privacy IS the operation of the kañcukas — they are the Λ_Φ. Individual domains are what you get when universal consciousness freely assumes contraction through these five covering principles."

audit:
  establishes: |
    The most SYSTEMATIC account of domain individuation in the corpus. Each kañcuka specifies a constraint:
    - Kalā: limits agency to "some capacity to act" (not all-powerful)
    - Vidyā: limits knowledge to "some capacity to know" (not omniscient)
    - Rāga: attaches consciousness to particular objects (selective attention)
    - Kāla: temporally sequences manifestation (not everything at once)
    - Niyati: causally constrains what must or must not be done
    
    Together, these five carve out a bounded phenomenal domain from the unbounded universal consciousness. They are explicitly called "obscuring coverings" (kañcuka) — the metaphor is of a lamp placed inside a pierced covering that only lets light out through the holes.
    
    The Appendix B passage (lines 34270-99) adds crucial detail: Śiva's universal powers of consciousness (citi), bliss (ānanda), will (icchā), knowledge (jñāna), and action (kriyā) — through ignorance — assume the form of these five coverings. The individual soul (puruṣa) IS Parameśvara appearing in contracted form.
    
    This answers Q3 (what individuates πᵢ?) and Q4 (what selects biological scale?) from the hermes_corpus_findings to the extent that the kañcukas provide a STRUCTURAL account of limitation. Time and place (niyati) constrain where and when manifestation occurs.
  missing_premise: |
    Two major gaps:
    
    1. WHY does contraction occur? The text says consciousness "freely assumes contraction" (saṃkocagrahaṇa). This is a metaphysical brute fact — consciousness just DOES this. There's no principle explaining WHY it contracts into THIS pattern rather than THAT, or WHY it contracts at all.
    
    2. The kañcukas describe the STRUCTURE of a contracted domain but not the PRINCIPLE that individuates one domain from another. Given two fettered souls, each has all five kañcukas. What makes one soul's kalā (limited agency) different from another's? The kañcukas explain what LIMITATION looks like, not why limitation produces NON-OVERLAPPING domains.
    
    3. The appeal to karma (the "prior Karma of each particular" in Vol 10 line 8843) shifts the explanatory burden rather than discharging it.
  countermodel: |
    Śaivasiddhānta dualism: the kañcukas bind genuinely plural souls (paśu) that are distinct from Śiva (pati). The monist framework tries to have both universal consciousness AND individual domains. The dualist avoids the individuation problem by positing souls as primitively multiple — the kañcukas only describe their bondage, not their individuation. The monist must explain individuation AND bondage with the same mechanism.

verdict:
  status: "best_descriptive_account_individuation_mechanism_missing"
  frontier: "What PRINCIPLE determines the specific parameters of each instance of contraction? The kañcukas describe the dimensions of limitation but not the individuating factor. Is there a Λ_kañcuka — a meta-principle determining each specific contraction's profile?"

## Candidate Λ_Φ-4: Māyā as Plane of Mutual Exclusion

**text:** `/root/projects/tantraloka/texts/tantraloka-vol4-dyczkowski.txt` lines 1008-13
**ref:** Tantrāloka Vol 4 (Chapter on Upāya)
**tradition:** Kashmir Śaivism (Trika)

> "Otherwise, it would not be possible for there to be daily life (vyavahāra), which is the (relationship between) subject and object, without the perception, on the plane of (the duality of) Māyā, of (jar and nonjar) as mutually exclusive. Thus, although one with consciousness, thought consists of relative distinctions (bheda) because (its identity is established by) the denial (apoha) of (all) else (that it is not)."

**footnote 7:** "The differences we perceive between one entity and another ... are the result of determining their particular form and nature. This takes place by a process of identification based on the mutual exclusion (apoha) of one thing from all others."

claims:
  weak: "On the plane of Māyā (duality), entities are perceived as mutually exclusive through apoha — conceptual exclusion of what they are not. This is necessary for daily life (subject-object commerce)."
  strong: "Therefore Māyā IS the principle of mutual exclusion that creates private phenomenal domains. Privacy is not a flaw but a necessary condition for vyavahāra (empirical transaction). The mutual exclusivity of objects within a domain and the mutual exclusivity of domains themselves are structurally identical."

audit:
  establishes: |
    A crucial structural insight: mutual exclusion (apoha) operates at the level of conceptual thought (vikalpa) and is constitutive of the empirical world. The text argues that vyavahāra (empirical transaction) would be IMPOSSIBLE without the perception of things as mutually exclusive.
    
    If this is extended: just as a jar is perceived as mutually exclusive with a non-jar within a single phenomenal domain, one phenomenal domain is mutually exclusive with another domain. The same principle of exclusion that structures object-perception also structures subject-differentiation.
    
    The footnote (line 1032-34) explicitly ties this to the mirror analogy: "Consciousness is one and uniform, like a mirror that reflects many images within itself. The differences we perceive between one entity and another — the images in the mirror — are the result of determining their particular form and nature. This takes place by a process of identification based on the mutual exclusion (apoha) of one thing from all others."
    
    The mirror analogy cuts both ways: the mirror is one, the images are many and mutually exclusive. This perfectly captures the Λ_Φ problem: one ground (M₀), many domains (Dᵢ), mutually exclusive.
  missing_premise: |
    The text describes the OPERATION of mutual exclusion within a domain (jar vs. non-jar) but doesn't explicitly extend it to cross-domain exclusion (my experience vs. your experience). The extension is natural but not argued.
    
    More crucially: if mutual exclusion is a feature of vikalpa (conceptual thought), then it's epistemic/conceptual rather than ontological. On this reading, domains only APPEAR mutually exclusive because we think in terms of "mine" and "not mine." At the pre-conceptual level (nirvikalpaka), there is no exclusion. This would make privacy a cognitive illusion rather than a real ontological partition.
    
    The "mirror" metaphor supports this reading: the images in a mirror are distinct from each other but the mirror itself is uniform. If consciousness is like the mirror, privacy is like the apparent separation of images — it describes how things look, not how they fundamentally are.
  countermodel: |
    The Buddhist apoha theory (Dignāga, Dharmakīrti): mutual exclusion operates entirely at the conceptual level. Real particulars (svalakṣaṇa) are unique and self-standing — their apparent exclusion of each other is a feature of how thought represents them, not of reality. Applied to Λ_Φ: domain privacy is a conceptual construction, not a real partition. The "private domain" is just the ongoing construction of self-representation.

verdict:
  status: "strong_structural_analogy_phenomenal_not_ontological"
  frontier: "Is the mutual exclusion of phenomenal domains a) the same operation as vikalpic apoha (conceptual), b) a distinct ontological principle (māyā's power of differentiation), or c) both — with conceptual exclusion as the internal experience of an ontological partition?"

## Candidate Λ_Φ-5: Vikalpa Partitioning

**text:** `/root/projects/tantraloka/texts/tantraloka-vol1-dyczkowski.txt` lines 28007-17
**ref:** Tantrāloka Vol 1 (interval between cognitions argument)
**tradition:** Kashmir Śaivism (Trika)

> "And this (consciousness) that is free of thought constructs (nirvikalpaka) exists. It is ... present in the interval between both (of the two differentiated) conceptual representations (vikalpa), namely, one which has ceased and the other in the process of arising. It precedes ... the (differentiated) conceptual representations of (the colour) blue and the like, that are not erroneous and considered to be mutually exclusive. Therefore, (that consciousness, free from thought constructs,) consists of the undivided (unity) of (its) endless manifestations."

claims:
  weak: "Consciousness free of thought constructs (nirvikalpaka) is undivided unity. Conceptual representations (vikalpa) are mutually exclusive and partition this unity. The interval between two cognitions reveals the underlying unity."
  strong: "Therefore phenomenal domain privacy is a function of vikalpa — conceptual thought partitions the single undivided consciousness into mutually exclusive representational domains. Λ_Φ = the vikalpa operation that converts nirvikalpaka unity into vikalpic multiplicity."

audit:
  establishes: |
    A clear structural relationship: nirvikalpaka consciousness = unity without partition; vikalpa = partition into mutually exclusive representations. The text explicitly says the differentiated representations (blue, yellow, etc.) are "considered to be mutually exclusive" and that nirvikalpaka consciousness "consists of the undivided unity of its endless manifestations."
    
    This provides a PRINCIPLE of partition: vikalpa (conceptual differentiation) is what creates mutually exclusive representations out of undivided consciousness. Privacy is not a feature of reality but a feature of how reality is REPRESENTED through thought constructs.
    
    The passage about anusandhāna (connective synthesis, lines 28023-24) reinforces this: if the interval between cognitions were not consciousness itself, then recollection and association across cognitions would be impossible. The unity of consciousness persists THROUGH the vikalpa partitioning.
  missing_premise: |
    This theory works well to explain how mutually exclusive CONTENTS arise within one stream of consciousness. It works less well to explain why different STREAMS (different subjects) are mutually exclusive. The text describes how one consciousness generates multiple representations; it doesn't describe how one consciousness generates multiple SUBJECTS.
    
    The missing step: does vikalpa partition consciousness into different simultaneous subjects, or just into different successive representations within one subject? The text describes succession (one vikalpa ceases, another arises) — which is intra-subjective, not inter-subjective.
    
    If vikalpa ONLY operates within each subject's stream, then it explains the structure of each domain but not their separation. The partition between subjects would require a different principle.
  countermodel: |
    The Yogācāra view: each momentary cognition (vijñāna) is its own self-contained unity. The "nirvikalpaka" interval is just the absence of determinate cognition — not a positive unity underlying all cognitions. Privacy is not created by vikalpa; it's built into the momentary nature of each cognition, which is complete in itself and doesn't require a unifying substrate.

verdict:
  status: "strong_explanatory_for_intra_subjective_partition_weak_for_inter_subjective"
  frontier: "Does vikalpa operate inter-subjectively (partitioning one consciousness into many subjects) or only intra-subjectively (partitioning one subject's experience into many representations)? If the latter, what partitions subjects?"

## Candidate Λ_Φ-6: Ābhāsavāda Clustering

**text:** `/root/projects/tantraloka/texts/tantraloka-vol10-dyczkowski.txt` lines 8810-15
**ref:** Tantrāloka Vol 10 (ābhāsavāda theory)
**tradition:** Kashmir Śaivism (Trika)

> "It is the final established conclusion of our (philosophy) (siddhānta) that a (phenomenal) entity (bhāva) consists of a conglomeration of many (constitutive) manifestations (ābhāsamūrchana); thus, it cannot sustain any nature (rūpa) (of its own) apart from that conglomeration of manifestations."

claims:
  weak: "Phenomenal entities are not atomic but emergent — they are conglomerations (mūrchana) of many constitutive manifestations (ābhāsa). The entity is nothing over and above the cluster."
  strong: "Therefore the private phenomenal domain itself is a conglomeration of manifestations. The domain IS the cluster, and its privacy is the fact that the clustering process occurs within a single cognitive stream. Different streams produce different clusters."

audit:
  establishes: |
    The ābhāsavāda theory provides an ONTOLOGY of phenomena that is relevant to Λ_Φ in two ways:
    
    1. **Entity constitution**: What we take to be a unified object (e.g., a jar) is actually a cluster of atomic appearings. The entity doesn't have a nature independently of the cluster. This means that phenomenal objects are essentially CLUSTERINGS of more primitive elements.
    
    2. **Domain constitution by extension**: If the entity is a cluster of appearings, the phenomenal domain (field of experience) is a higher-order cluster — a cluster of clusters. The domain's privacy would then be a function of the clustering PRINCIPLE that determines which appearings cluster together with which.
    
    The "row of altar lamps" (dīpavālī) analogy from the hermes_corpus_findings (§2487-2503) makes this vivid: individual appearings combine into unified objects without losing their distinctness, like a row of lamps that collectively illuminate a space.
    
    The missing piece (from the same corpus finding) is the "common basis" (sāmānyādhikaraṇya) — the most prominent member of the cluster that determines objecthood. Could the subject (pramātṛ) be the common basis that determines the cluster's identity as THIS domain rather than THAT?
  missing_premise: |
    The theory describes how entities are composed but doesn't describe what individuates ONE cognitive stream from another. Ābhāsas are "atomic appearings" — but what groups them into one perceiver's experience rather than another's? The theory has no cross-stream individuation principle.
    
    If ābhāsas are intrinsically un-owned (mere appearings), then a second principle is needed to explain why ābhāsa A and ābhāsa B belong to the same domain. Without this, the theory describes the structure of a domain but not its boundary.
    
    The "conglomeration" metaphor raises a further question: what is the FORCE that conglomerates? Gravity? Attraction? Or just brute contiguity?
  countermodel: |
    Humean/Associationist psychology: the "cluster" is just habit — constant conjunction in past experience creates the expectation of co-occurrence, which we project as unity. The cluster is not real but associative. Ābhāsavāda avoids this by making the cluster ontologically real (siddhānta), but the borderline between ontological clustering and psychological association is thin.

verdict:
  status: "ontological_framework_incomplete_without_individuation_principle"
  frontier: "What determines which ābhāsas cluster together as one domain? Is the clustering principle internal to the ābhāsas (e.g., similarity, causal connection) or external (imposed by the subject)? Could the subject BE the clustering principle?"

## Candidate Λ_Φ-7: Niyati as Causal Restriction

**text:** `/root/projects/tantraloka/texts/tantraloka-vol6-dyczkowski.txt` lines 1732-38, 1850-65
**ref:** Tantrāloka Vol 6 (niyati discussion)
**tradition:** Kashmir Śaivism (Trika)

> "However, because (this) other (correct) insight (prathā) is obscured in the sphere (daśā) in which the constraining principle of natural law (niyati) (operates), people experience (pratīti) that 'the sprout (originates) from the seed and the jar from clay'."

> "Thus, it is supreme (transcendental) Śiva Himself who, on the level (daśā) on which the constraining principle of natural law (niyati) (operates), generates the product (kārya) by the mutual dependence of the potter and the clay etc."

claims:
  weak: "Niyati (the constraining principle of natural law/necessity) operates at a specific level (daśā) of reality, creating the appearance of determinate causal relations and obscuring the underlying freedom of consciousness."
  strong: "Therefore niyati is the Λ_Φ for causal domain restriction. It is what makes this domain have THIS causal structure (seed→sprout, potter→pot) rather than another. The domain's privacy is a function of its specific causal architecture — different niyati configurations produce different domains."

audit:
  establishes: |
    Niyati is one of the five kañcukas but deserves separate treatment because it specifically addresses the CAUSAL boundedness of a domain. The passages identify niyati as operating at a specific "level" or "sphere" (daśā) — it is not all-pervasive but localized to certain ontological strata.
    
    The key function of niyati is to create DETERMINACY: "the constraint that 'I must do this' (or) 'I must not do this'" (line 34234-35). This is what makes a domain have a specific causal shape. Without niyati, all possibilities would be equally available; with niyati, certain causal sequences are fixed and others excluded.
    
    This is explanatory for Λ_Φ because it proposes that phenomenal domains are private not because of a mysterious separation but because each domain has its own causal order (its own niyati configuration). The "privacy" of my experience is the fact that the causal sequences governing my experience are constrained to MY body and MY history.
  missing_premise: |
    Niyati explains the INTERNAL causal structure of a domain but not its BOUNDARY. It tells us that within a domain, things happen in a determinate order. It doesn't tell us WHY this domain's niyati doesn't govern events in another domain.
    
    Additionally, niyati as a kañcuka operates at the level of the "impure creation" (asuddhasr̥ṣṭi — from Māyā down to Earth). At higher levels (pure creation), niyati does not operate. If privacy depends on niyati, then at higher levels of realization there would be no privacy — which may be the tradition's intended meaning (liberation = transcending domain boundaries).
  countermodel: |
    The scientific view (physicalism): causal laws are universal, not domain-specific. What makes my experience private is not different causal architecture but different physical location (my brain, not yours). Niyati as a localized causal principle is unnecessary — a single set of universal laws operating on different physical substrates explains domain privacy.

verdict:
  status: "partial_explanation_causal_structure_not_boundary"
  frontier: "What determines the SCOPE of a niyati configuration? If niyati varies by domain (daśā), what sets the domain boundary independently of the causal content?"

## Candidate Λ_Φ-8: Knowledge Person-Specificity

**text:** `/root/projects/tantraloka/texts/tantraloka-vol11-dyczkowski.txt` lines 22225-39
**ref:** Tantrāloka Vol 11 (āgama prāmāṇya discussion)
**tradition:** Kashmir Śaivism (Trika)

> "What is the meaning of the statement 'the means of knowledge are impartial?' If someone becomes aware of the colour blue through direct perception, then that will manifest blue to everybody? Or, if he perceives smoke, (and infers fire, that inference will manifest the fact that there is) fire (to everybody)? So, (you must admit), it is something for some specific person at some specific point of time."

claims:
  weak: "Knowledge (pramā) is always for a specific person (niyatādhikārin) at a specific time. The impartiality of pramāṇas does not mean that knowledge is universally shared."
  strong: "Therefore the privacy of phenomenal domains is a consequence of the person-specificity of knowledge. Λ_Φ = the principle that ties knowledge to a specific knower — making each knower's domain their own."

audit:
  establishes: |
    A direct argument for the person-specificity of knowledge that uses the PRIVACY OF PERCEPTION as evidence. The opponent challenges: "the means of knowledge are impartial." The response: if pramāṇas were truly impartial, everyone would perceive the same things. Since they don't, knowledge must be person-specific.
    
    This is the flipside of the Prakāśa-Localization argument (Candidate 1): if manifestness were inherent in objects, everyone would perceive everything. Here: if pramāṇas were truly impartial (having no owner), everyone would perceive the same things. Since perception is clearly owner-specific, knowledge must be tied to specific individuals.
    
    The argument establishes a strong link between Λ_Φ (domain privacy) and the nature of knowledge: domains are private because knowledge-acts are private. The privacy of perception is not a defect but a structural feature of how knowledge works.
    
    The text uses this to argue for the authority of scripture (āgama) as conviction (vimarśana) for specific qualified persons — but the perceptual argument stands independently.
  missing_premise: |
    The argument works as a description (knowledge IS person-specific) but doesn't explain WHY it is person-specific. The text doesn't provide a mechanism — it simply points to the fact of privacy and uses it as evidence against the opponent's view.
    
    The argument may run the wrong direction: it uses the fact of domain privacy to argue for person-specific knowledge, which is circular if the goal is to explain domain privacy through person-specific knowledge.
    
    This is a weaker Λ_Φ candidate because it identifies the phenomenon to be explained (knowledge is owned) rather than a principle explaining it. It's like saying "water is wet because it has the property of wetness" — true but circular.
  countermodel: |
    Any theory that accepts domain privacy as a primitive: "knowledge is person-specific" is just a restatement of the fact that subjects have private domains. It doesn't explain WHY. The Nyāya plural-selves theory treats this as brute: selves are primitively separate, so their knowledge is primitively separate. No further explanation needed.

verdict:
  status: "descriptive_circular_not_explanatory"
  frontier: "Is person-specific knowledge a) the foundational fact that other Λ_Φ candidates need to explain, b) a derivative consequence of a deeper principle (like domain partition), or c) the very phenomenon of domain privacy itself relabeled?"

## Candidate Λ_Φ-9: Body-Based Synthesis Restriction

**text:** `/root/projects/tantraloka/texts/tantraloka-vol6-dyczkowski.txt` (cf. hermes_corpus_findings T14 §26-27)
**ref:** Tantrāloka 16.242 (Abhinavagupta)
**tradition:** Kashmir Śaivism (Trika)

> "pradeśavṛtti ca jñānamātmanastatra tatra tat / bhogyajñānaṃ nānyadeheṣv anusandhānam arhati"
> "Knowledge that is localized and enjoyed in one body cannot perform connective synthesis (anusandhāna) with other bodies."

claims:
  weak: "Connective synthesis (the unity of cognition across moments) is restricted to one body. Knowledge in one body does not extend to another."
  strong: "Therefore the body is the principle of phenomenal domain individuation. Λ_Φ = the body (deha) as the locus of anusandhāna. Different bodies = different synthesis streams = different phenomenal domains."

audit:
  establishes: |
    A remarkably clear and direct statement of domain individuation. Abhinavagupta (the tradition's greatest philosopher) explicitly states that anusandhāna — the connective synthesis that unifies cognitions into one stream — is BODY-LOCALIZED. Knowledge "enjoyed" (bhogya) in one body cannot perform synthesis with other bodies.
    
    This provides a concrete, non-mysterious principle of individuation: the body is the BOUNDARY of the synthetic unity of consciousness. Two different bodies = two different synthesis streams = two different phenomenal domains.
    
    This makes the body the Λ_Φ principle for INTER-SUBJECTIVE partition. While the intra-subjective unity of consciousness is explained by anusandhāna (the subject's own synthesizing activity), the inter-subjective boundary is explained by the body as the locus of that synthesis.
    
    The hermes_corpus_findings notes this as a "direct constraint on Utpaladeva's universalization argument in APS 17" — Abhinavagupta himself limits anusandhāna to one body, blocking the inference from "synthesis within one stream" to "synthesis across all streams."
  missing_premise: |
    The verse is from a ritual context (dealing with knowledge in different bodies during initiation). Its generalizability to the Λ_Φ question should be checked against the original context.
    
    If the body is the principle of individuation, then the question becomes: what individuates the body? If the body is a physical object, then it's individuated by its position in space — but this leaves unexplained why consciousness is tied to one physical object rather than another (the hard problem of embodiment).
    
    Additionally, the text doesn't explain why anusandhāna is restricted to one body — it states it as a fact. A deeper principle (karma, māyā, niyati) may be doing the actual individuating, with the body as the visible expression of that deeper principle.
    
    The Pratyabhijñā response to this verse (mentioned in hermes_corpus_findings) reads the limitation as epistemic (vyavahāra) rather than metaphysical (paramārtha), which would undermine the claim that the body is the ontological Λ_Φ.
  countermodel: |
    Parapsychology/psi research (if robust): evidence of cross-body cognitive access (telepathy, clairvoyance) would refute the claim that anusandhāna is absolutely body-restricted. The tradition can absorb this as "extraordinary cases" but such cases would undermine the principle.
    
    The eliminativist response: if the body is the principle of individuation, then there's no need for a separate self — the body-synthesis stream IS the self. This eliminates the subject of experience in favor of the synthesis process itself (Buddhist stream-of-consciousness model).

verdict:
  status: "strongest_concrete_principle_origin_of_body_limitation_unexplained"
  frontier: "Does the body individuate the domain, or does a deeper principle (karma, māyā) individuate the body? If the body is the Λ_Φ, we need a theory of what makes THIS body the locus of THIS anusandhāna — which may require a theory of embodiment that the Tantrāloka only gestures at through karma."

## Candidate Λ_Φ-10: Māyā as Domain of Differentiated Perception

**text:** `/root/projects/tantraloka/texts/doctrine-of-vibration-dyczkowski.txt` lines 7836-42
**ref:** Doctrine of Vibration (Dyczkowski on Spanda/Utpaladeva)
**tradition:** Kashmir Śaivism (Spanda)

> "At this level all the powers of consciousness fuse and both phases are manifest as part of one reality. This unity is in fact apparent to everybody at each moment. However, within the domain of Maya, which is the sphere of differentiated perceptions (vikalpa), it is clearly manifest only at the juncture (madhya) between two cognitions."

claims:
  weak: "Unity (the fused state of all powers) is apparent to everybody at each moment. But within the domain of Māyā (sphere of vikalpa), it is clearly manifest only in the interval between cognitions."
  strong: "Therefore Māyā is the DOMAIN of differentiated perceptions — it creates the space within which mutual exclusion operates. Unity is the ground, Māyā is the sphere of partition. Λ_Φ = Māyā as the differentiating principle that creates the sphere of separate phenomenal domains."

audit:
  establishes: |
    A clear architectural claim: there are two levels:
    1. The level of unity (where the unity is "apparent to everybody at each moment")
    2. The domain of Māyā (sphere of differentiated perceptions, vikalpa)
    
    The unity is AVAILABLE to all (it's "apparent to everybody") but within Māyā it's only clearly manifest in the interval between cognitions. This suggests that the privacy of phenomenal domains belongs to the Māyā level, not the ultimate level. At the ultimate level, unity obtains.
    
    This gives Māyā a precise role in Λ_Φ: Māyā is the PRINCIPLE that creates the sphere within which domains appear separate. It's not that domains are REALLY separate — it's that Māyā makes them APPEAR separate. The privacy is real at the level of Māyā (vyavahāra) but not at the level of ultimate reality (paramārtha).
    
    The "junctuve between two cognitions" (madhya) is where the underlying unity is glimpsed within Māyā — consistent with the nirvikalpaka argument (Candidate 5).
  missing_premise: |
    This account explains why domains APPEAR private (Māyā makes them appear so) but not whether they ARE private. If Māyā is a principle of illusion (as in Advaita), then domain privacy is an illusion — real at the empirical level, unreal at the ultimate level. But if Māyā is a real power of differentiation (as in Trika), then the domains are really separate at their own level.
    
    The text's claim that "this unity is in fact apparent to everybody at each moment" is empirically questionable — most people do NOT experience unity with all other subjects at each moment. If the unity is always present but not recognized, then the explanation for why it's not recognized IS the explanation for domain privacy — which means an epistemic factor (ignorance) rather than an ontological factor (real partition).
    
    This raises the Advaitin challenge: if domain privacy is a form of ignorance, then liberation removes it. But the fact that we normally experience privacy remains to be explained.
  countermodel: |
    The Advaita Vedānta view: Māyā is beginningless ignorance (avidyā) that projects the appearance of separate selves onto the single Brahman. Domain privacy is entirely illusory and is removed by knowledge. Trika differs by making Māyā a real power (śakti) of consciousness, not mere ignorance.

verdict:
  status: "domain_privacy_as_māyā_level_phenomenon_ultimate_status_ambiguous"
  frontier: "Is Māyā a real ontological principle of differentiation or an epistemic principle of ignorance? If the former, domain privacy is real at its level and Λ_Φ is the structure of Māyā itself. If the latter, domain privacy is a kind of error and the real question is: what sustains the error?"

## Frontier Questions After First Pass

These questions emerged from the first pass across all 20 files:

1. **Individuation vs. Limitation**: The kañcukas describe LIMITATION within a domain but not INDIVIDUATION between domains. Is there a single principle that does both, or are they separate?

2. **Body as Boundary vs. Body as Instrument**: The anusandhāna restriction (Candidate 9) makes the body the boundary. The general Trika view makes the body an instrument. Can both be true? Is the body both the locus of synthesis AND a derivative manifestation?

3. **Unity Always Available**: The Doctrine of Vibration claims unity is "apparent to everybody at each moment." If so, what explains our failure to experience it? Is the answer to this the same as the answer to Λ_Φ?

4. **Vikalpa Scope**: Does vikalpa partition intra-subjectively (one subject's contents into mutually exclusive representations) or inter-subjectively (one consciousness into many subjects)? The texts discuss the former but are largely silent on the latter.

5. **The Māyā Question**: Is domain privacy a feature of Māyā (differentiation principle) or of vikalpa (conceptual construction) or of the body (synthesis locus)? These three candidates point in different directions.

6. **Prakāśa-Localization Meets Universal Consciousness**: If all perceivers share the same universal consciousness (prakāśa), why does each have their OWN light of consciousness? The universal and the local are both asserted but not reconciled.

7. **Freedom and Privacy**: The tradition characterizes privacy (mutual exclusion of egos) as bondage. If liberation removes this exclusion, does it also remove domain privacy? Would the liberated subject experience all domains as their own?

8. **The Hard Question**: Is domain privacy a) real and fundamental, b) real but derivative (from a deeper principle), or c) illusory (a feature of ignorance)? The tradition's answer: (b) — real at the level of Māyā/vyavahāra, but not ultimate. How compelling is this answer?
